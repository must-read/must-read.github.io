# Story Plan: "Gnawed Clean"

## Premise
A former line cook named Pruitt, living in a tent camp beneath an overpass in Birmingham, Alabama, gnaws a rib bone scavenged from behind a barbecue restaurant, identifying every flaw in its preparation with the precision of a man who once ran a kitchen. A recently evicted grandmother named Dovie Stovall arrives at the camp with a rolling suitcase and the conviction that her son will collect her in the morning. Over one night in the confined space of the camp, their conversation becomes a contest between his unspoken knowledge that she will stay and her performance of departure.

## Protagonist
**Pruitt** — 54 years old, former line cook at a Birmingham restaurant called Locklin's that closed when the building was sold. Lost his apartment when Locklin's closed (his pay was what kept him solvent). Chose the camp over the shelter because the shelter's organized kindness was worse than the camp's indifferent geography. He WANTS nothing — or wants people to believe he wants nothing. He NEEDS to be useful, to apply his knowledge to something, which is why he catalogues everything about the camp with culinary precision. His FLAW is that his competence has become a wall — he names and identifies everything to avoid feeling anything about it. The naming is the substitute for the living.

## Antagonist / Counterforce
**Dovie Stovall** — 71 years old, evicted from an apartment she'd lived in for twenty-two years when the landlord sold the building. Her son lives in Hoover (Birmingham suburb). His wife (Dovie will not say her name, only "his wife") said boundaries. Dovie is NOT sympathetic — she is opinionated, critical, certain of her status, and convinced that her situation is temporary. She performs departure from the moment she arrives. Her gentility is real but weaponized. She asks what cut the bone is. She knows food. Her competence mirrors Pruitt's but from a domestic rather than professional context.

## Structure (5 Beats)

**Beat 1: The Bone** (~900 words)
Pruitt gnaws the rib bone under the overpass. Full culinary autopsy: the cut (St. Louis-style spare rib, trimmed but not well), the smoke (pecan, not hickory — Birmingham uses pecan), the rub (too much garlic powder, not enough time in the rub before cooking), the pull temperature (too high, fat rendered greasy instead of silky). The camp described through his cataloguing eye: who lives where, what the overpass sounds like, the drainage line, the weather. The Maillard reaction referenced naturally — the crust on the meat, or lack thereof. Birmingham grounded through specific detail: the Sloss Furnaces visible in the distance, the particular geography of the overpass.

**Beat 2: The Arrival** (~900 words)
Dovie arrives from the street side, dragging a rolling suitcase. She's wearing church clothes. She announces herself. "I was told this was the place." She asks where she should put her things. She says she's only staying one night. Pruitt directs her to an open pallet. She criticizes the camp's arrangements with the authority of a woman who has run a household. She asks what he's eating. He says a rib. She asks what cut. He tells her. She says, "They pulled it too early. The fat hasn't finished." This shared knowledge opens something between them — not warmth, but recognition.

**Beat 3: The Night** (~1000 words)
The camp settles. Dovie produces provisions from her suitcase: instant coffee, a jar of bread-and-butter pickles she made herself, a transistor radio. She offers Pruitt a pickle. He eats it and identifies the brine — too much sugar, turmeric instead of the mustard seed she should have used, the cucumbers were past their prime. She is offended. Then she asks: "Where did you cook?" and he tells her about Locklin's. She tells him about her kitchen — the four-burner gas stove, the cast-iron pan that was her mother's, the oven that ran cold by fifteen degrees. Two people describing kitchens that no longer exist. The traffic thins. Other camp residents come into focus: a man called Shiloh who collects aluminum cans, a woman named Bette who knits scarves for nobody.

**Beat 4: The Refusal** (~1000 words)
Dovie's phone rings. It's her son. She answers it and walks to the edge of the camp for privacy but Pruitt can hear her voice — the controlled brightness of a woman performing normalcy. She says "Of course" several times. She says "Tomorrow is fine." She comes back and sits on her pallet. "He's coming tomorrow afternoon," she says. Pruitt says nothing. She arranges her things on the pallet with the care of someone who will be leaving. She asks Pruitt how long he's been here. He says a number. She processes it. She begins to say something about shelters, about programs, about getting back on your feet. He cuts her off — not with anger but with the flat precision of a man correcting a wrong temperature. He tells her about the shelter's clipboard and the check-in times and the laminated rules. He tells her the camp is not what she thinks it is — not a way station. It's a place. She refuses this. The argument is quiet and total.

**Beat 5: Dawn** (~800 words)
Dovie prays before sleeping — hands folded, lips moving, the same prayer she's always prayed, but the prayer goes up through concrete instead of a ceiling. Pruitt watches from his pallet. He has seen this before. The new arrivals always pray the first night. In the morning, the light changes on the concrete. Dovie wakes, changes into a fresh blouse from her suitcase, combs her hair, checks her phone. She says her son will come this afternoon. Pruitt heats water for coffee. He pours half into a second cup — an act his hands perform before his mind consents. He brings it to her. She takes it, drinks, makes a face. "This water's too hot," she says. "You've scalded the crystals. You of all people should know the difference." He should know. He does know. He scalded it on purpose because at a certain temperature instant coffee tastes like something other than what it is, and sometimes that's better. He doesn't say this. He drinks his half. The bone from last night is on the ground beside his pallet, gnawed clean. She is still there. He knew she would be.

## Emotional Trajectory
From solitude to involuntary recognition. Pruitt's cataloguing eye, which has been his armor, encounters someone who can read the same details — and this shared language doesn't save either of them. It just means they can describe what's happening with precision as it happens.

## Formula Integration Plan
- **AuthorA (McCarthy)**: Long, accumulative sentences with polysyndeton. Physical sensation rendered with forensic precision. Dialogue without quotation marks, embedded in narrative. The overpass as constant sonic presence. Spare, muscular descriptions of weather and light. Biblical cadence applied to poverty.
- **AuthorB (O'Connor)**: Dovie's self-deception and performance of gentility. Dark comedy in the gap between her self-image and her circumstance. The shared food knowledge creating a kind of violent recognition. The prayer scene as adjacent-to-grace. The ending refusing resolution.
- **WorkX (Suttree)**: Episodic night structure. A man who chose marginality over institutional submission. The camp's social world rendered without sentiment. Cataloguing of marginal characters as complete worlds.
- **WorkY (A Good Man Is Hard to Find)**: Collision between self-image and reality. Grandmother figure performing status. The moment where pretense meets fact and neither yields. Economic violence replacing physical violence.

## Risk Card Integration: CONSTRAINT OF SPACE
Everything happens under the overpass. One night. No flashbacks to other locations — only the characters' verbal descriptions of places that no longer exist (Locklin's kitchen, Dovie's apartment). The overpass is not a metaphor. It is the place where people live. Its geometry, sounds, and light changes provide the story's clock. The confined space forces everything through conversation and physical detail.

## Wikipedia Concepts to Fuse
1. **Maillard reaction**: Pruitt knows the chemistry of cooking at a molecular level. The Maillard reaction — the browning that creates flavor — is something he can describe but no longer practice. It enters the text as technical knowledge deployed in conversation, not as metaphor.
2. **Sloss Furnaces**: Visible from the overpass. The furnaces — massive industrial structures that operated for decades, closed, and were preserved as a landmark — provide a Birmingham-specific geographic anchor and an unspoken parallel to the camp's inhabitants: structures that still stand after their purpose has ended.

## Title
"Gnawed Clean" — the bone, and the stripping of pretense. Does not start with "The."
