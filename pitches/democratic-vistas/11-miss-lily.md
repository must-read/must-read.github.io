## Premise
A 61-year-old Vietnamese shrimp fisherman in Bayou La Batre, Alabama, takes his 65-foot trawler -- the Miss Lily, named after his dead wife -- out for what may be the last profitable season. Southeast Asian imports have halved the price per pound, fuel costs have doubled, insurance is climbing, and fewer boats dock each year. His son David, 32, works the boat with him and is one of the youngest shrimpers left on the Gulf Coast. The story follows a three-day trip: departure at dawn with incense lit at the home altar, the work itself (nets, diesel, salt, the crooked finger that healed wrong from a winch cable break), a storm that tests the rebuilt Detroit Diesel engine, and a return to a dock that has one fewer boat than when they left. Between the father and son, two languages operate: English, which is direct and sparing, and Vietnamese, which is expansive and carries humor. The story is in English. The reader can feel what is missing.

## Source Inspiration
The portrait of Thanh Nguyen in "Democratic Portraits" -- a Vietnamese refugee who arrived in Alabama in 1983, eight years after Saigon, via a Philippine refugee camp, Arkansas resettlement, and a cousin in Biloxi. He rebuilt the Miss Lily after Katrina left her in a parking lot 300 yards from the water. His wife died of a stroke in 2017 while hanging laundry. His mother is 90 and lives with him. He maintains a home altar with his wife's photograph, a Quan Am figure, an incense holder, and a daily-replaced orange. The essay describes him as "existing between two languages" and his industry as visibly dying.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Viet Thanh Nguyen)**: Nguyen's Vietnamese American perspective, his postcolonial critique, and his ability to render the refugee experience without sentimentality or simplification. The fisherman is not a "symbol of resilience." He is a man doing a job. Nguyen's literary precision makes the cultural displacement legible without making it the story's thesis.
- **AuthorB (Ernest Hemingway)**: Hemingway's sea-narrative mastery -- the physical reality of the work, the salt and the diesel and the crooked finger, rendered in sentences that refuse to explain their own emotional weight. The three-day trip should carry the pressure of The Old Man and the Sea without its allegory. The omission theory: what the father and son do not say to each other is the story.
- **WorkX (The Sympathizer by Viet Thanh Nguyen)**: Structural DNA of a consciousness operating between two worlds -- here not espionage but the simpler, more common doubleness of an immigrant who has lived in America for forty years and still keeps a Vietnamese altar. The confession-to-no-one structure, the internal monologue that switches registers.
- **WorkY (The Old Man and the Sea by Hemingway)**: Thematic DNA of an aging man on the water performing a labor that the world has moved past, with a younger companion who may or may not continue. Santiago and Manolin; Thanh and David. The sea does not care about economics. The fish are there or they are not.

## Notes
The storm should be real but not catastrophic -- a test, not a crisis. The rebuilt Detroit Diesel engine should hold. The return to dock should be quiet: they made expenses, barely. The one fewer boat at the dock should be mentioned in a single sentence. The incense at the home altar -- morning ritual, the replaced orange -- should bookend the story without being made to mean more than it means. David should say one thing in Vietnamese that the father does not expect, late in the third day. Do not translate it.
