## Premise
A 71-year-old Vietnam veteran grows tomatoes in five-gallon buckets on his apartment porch using eggshells, coffee grounds, and fish emulsion from a Koreatown market. Every August he gives the tomatoes to his neighbors with the same line: "These are pretty good this year." The story covers a single growing season -- March through September -- from his perspective. He does not garden because it heals him, and the story does not claim it does. He gardens because he learned soil from his mother in Michigan, because the balcony gets six hours of sun, and because the alternative is the television. The neighbors include a recently separated man who talks too much, a sixteen-year-old girl who cries on the bus, and a woman who folds laundry at the laundromat at 6 AM with the care of someone handling a document. Gerald watches all of them. He does not intervene. In August, he gives them tomatoes. The tomatoes are, in fact, pretty good.

## Source Inspiration
Gerald appears in "Between Two Chasms" as a recurring figure -- a 71-year-old Vietnam veteran neighbor who grows tomatoes in five-gallon buckets and gifts them with ritualistic understatement. The essay uses him as evidence that "small acts of attention and care constitute the actual texture of human life" and persist despite systemic failures. He is surrounded by the essay's other characters: the woman folding laundry "with the care of someone handling a document," the crying girl on the bus who receives a stranger's hand on her forearm, the digital feed where a man dies and the next item is someone's croque monsieur.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Marilynne Robinson)**: Robinson's theological attention to the ordinary -- a tomato plant on a porch as an act of faith, a season's labor as communion with creation. Her long, cadenced sentences can carry the patience of gardening without making it precious. The meditative prose rhythm matches the slow work of soil, water, sun.
- **AuthorB (George Saunders)**: Saunders's radical empathy for people living constrained, often absurd lives. His vernacular warmth prevents Robinson's theological register from becoming solemn. Gerald's inner monologue should have Saunders's quality of self-aware dignity -- he knows the tomatoes are a small thing, and he does the small thing anyway, and Saunders's voice can hold both the smallness and the enormity.
- **WorkX (Gilead by Marilynne Robinson)**: Structural DNA of an elderly man reflecting on a life that was mostly ordinary, finding in its ordinariness something that approaches grace. John Ames writing to his son; Gerald tending his tomatoes for his neighbors. The same structure of late-life accounting where the ledger contains no grand achievements, only sustained attention.
- **WorkY (Stoner by John Williams)**: Thematic DNA of a quiet life lived with private intensity, visible to almost no one, valuable in ways the world does not measure. Gerald's tomatoes will not change anything. His neighbors' lives will not improve because of them. The gift is the gift itself -- presence, care, the refusal to stop showing up -- and the novel's argument is that this is enough, and also that it is not enough, and that both are true.

## Notes
The Vietnam backstory should appear exactly once, in a detail -- a scar, a reflex, a way of holding a tool -- and never be elaborated. Gerald does not garden because of the war. The growing season structure (March-September) should carry seven short sections, one per month, each centered on a single gardening task and a single observation of a neighbor. The August gift-giving should be understated to the point of comedy: he knocks, hands over the tomatoes, says the line, leaves. No one says thank you. Or someone does and he is already walking away. The tomatoes are the entire point and also beside the point.
