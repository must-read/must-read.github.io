## Premise
A social worker in Baltimore spends her days between two impossible positions: the nihilistic despair of her caseload (systems that cannot be reformed, suffering that cannot be prevented) and the false optimism demanded by her agency's reporting metrics (measurable outcomes, success stories for grant applications). She calls her position "the ridge" -- the narrow path between the chasm of giving up and the chasm of pretending things are fine. Over one week, the story follows her through five home visits, each revealing a different face of the impossible balance. The final visit is to a woman who has folded the same laundry three times because it is the only thing she can control. The social worker recognizes the gesture. The story ends not with resolution but with the two women sitting in a kitchen where the light is coming through a window, and neither of them looks away.

## Source Inspiration
"Between Two Chasms" identifies the central philosophical tension as the ridge between nihilistic despair and false optimism. The essay argues that "the work is not to explain the world but to refuse to look away from it." Specific images: a woman folding laundry with "the care of someone handling a document," the claim that "to refuse to see the apricot sky is to begin to refuse to see the woman praying at the vending machine" -- perception of beauty and perception of suffering are the same muscle. The essay references Simone Weil: "attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity."

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Albert Camus)**: Camus's lucid, Mediterranean prose and his philosophical framework of the absurd are foundational. The social worker is a Sisyphean figure -- she pushes the boulder knowing it will roll back, and the story asks whether she can be imagined happy. His clean, direct sentences carry moral weight without moralizing.
- **AuthorB (Annie Dillard)**: Dillard's mystical attention to the physical world provides the counterbalance. The light through the window, the texture of folded cloth, the specific quality of a February afternoon in Baltimore -- Dillard's precision turns observation into a form of prayer, which is exactly what the essay argues attention becomes.
- **WorkX (The Fall by Albert Camus)**: Structural DNA of a confession that is also an accusation. The social worker's week of home visits could carry the structure of Clamence's monologue -- each visit revealing not just the client's situation but the social worker's own complicity, her own ridge-walking, her own moments of looking away.
- **WorkY (Pilgrim at Tinker Creek by Annie Dillard)**: Thematic DNA of radical attention as spiritual practice. The essay's argument that seeing beauty and seeing suffering are the same capacity maps directly onto Dillard's project of looking so hard at the natural world that it becomes numinous. The social worker's attention to her clients is the same practice.

## Notes
The five home visits should not escalate toward a climax. They should accumulate like evidence in a case that has no verdict. Each visit reveals a different relationship to the ridge: one client has fallen into despair, one is performing false optimism, one has found something the social worker cannot name, one is simply enduring, one is folding laundry. The story's refusal to resolve is its philosophical argument.
