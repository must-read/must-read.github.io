## Premise
In a near-future where AI has made professional content creation instantaneous and free, a small-town librarian in Kentucky discovers that she is the last person in her county who reads books that were not algorithmically generated. The library still exists -- it has been converted into a "content wellness center" where AI generates personalized stories calibrated to each reader's emotional needs. Engagement metrics are excellent. Loneliness rates have not changed. The librarian, who grew up on Whitman and Baldwin, begins hosting a secret Tuesday evening group where eight people read a single human-written short story aloud and then sit in silence. The group includes a woman raising three children on two jobs, a retired content optimizer, and a teenager who has never experienced a blank page. Nothing dramatic happens. The story is about whether reading together constitutes resistance or nostalgia.

## Source Inspiration
The essay argues that Whitman called for a "class of native authors, literatures" to provide the soul that democratic institutions alone cannot -- and that this literatus never arrived. Instead, what came was "content" -- a "loneliness engine" that simulates meaning while replacing it. The essay describes "mountain-stacks of white paper" and "proud crashing presses" producing work that never becomes literature. It names the specific failure: entertainment machinery replaced the soul that should animate democracy, and "the content is not the problem. The problem is that the content is all there is."

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Kurt Vonnegut)**: Vonnegut's simple, devastating sentences and his humanist rage dressed in comic delivery. The "content wellness center" should be described with Vonnegut's deadpan -- so it goes -- while the reading group scenes carry his radical tenderness for ordinary people doing small, futile, beautiful things.
- **AuthorB (Joan Didion)**: Didion's cool, precise observation and her California-grief sensibility transplanted to Appalachia. The librarian's interior monologue should have Didion's scalpel quality -- she sees exactly what has happened and cannot look away, but she also cannot fix it, and she knows the difference between seeing and doing.
- **WorkX (Fahrenheit 451 by Bradbury)**: Structural DNA of a world where books are not banned but rendered irrelevant -- a more subtle and effective destruction. The librarian is not a rebel hiding contraband. She is a custodian of something the world has peacefully decided it no longer needs. Bradbury's warmth and anger coexist in the reading-aloud scenes.
- **WorkY (Amusing Ourselves to Death by Neil Postman)**: Thematic DNA of Huxley's dystopia defeating Orwell's -- the world ending not with censorship but with entertainment so satisfying that truth becomes unnecessary. The "content wellness center" is Postman's prophecy fulfilled: people have what they want, and what they want has hollowed them out.

## Notes
The Tuesday reading group should not be idealized or sentimentalized. Two of the eight members drop out because the human-written stories are "worse" than the AI-generated ones -- less tailored, less satisfying, harder to metabolize. The librarian must reckon with the possibility that they are right. The teenager who has never experienced a blank page should be the story's moral center: she does not know what she is missing, which makes her presence in the group either hopeful or devastating depending on whether the story believes in what it is doing.
