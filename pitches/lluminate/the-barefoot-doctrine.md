## Premise
In 1886, a U.S. Army cartographer is sent to map the final unmapped canyon system in the Arizona Territory. His Apache guide -- a former scout for the Army who fought his own people during the Geronimo campaign -- leads him deeper into terrain that resists mapping. The canyon walls are too close for triangulation. The compass behaves strangely near the iron-rich stone. As the expedition deteriorates, the cartographer realizes his guide is not lost but is deliberately leading him into country the Army cannot chart -- territory that will remain unmapped and therefore unclaimable. The story becomes a meditation on what happens when the weaker party's only strategy is to make the land itself illegible to the conqueror.

## Source Inspiration
Drawn from "The Empire's Ulcer," which argues that "the barefoot fighter wins by losing until winning and losing become the same thing." The essay's core insight -- that asymmetric warfare turns terrain into a weapon and that "the weak don't defeat the strong but prevent the strong from achieving anything decisive" -- becomes literal when applied to cartography and the American frontier. The historical parallel to Arminius (a Germanic chieftain trained in Roman methods who destroyed three legions by exploiting terrain) maps directly onto an Apache scout using Army training against the Army itself. The essay's observation that "geography's persistence" remains decisive resonates with the story's unmappable canyon.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (McCarthy)**: McCarthy's sparse, biblical prose and his treatment of the American West as a moral landscape of mythic violence. The canyon country should feel ancient and indifferent to human claims upon it. His refusal of quotation marks and his stripped-down dialogue suit a story where two men communicate more through silence and terrain than speech.
- **AuthorB (le Carre)**: Le Carre's mastery of betrayal between allies, of institutional loyalty corroding under moral pressure, and of two intelligent men who understand each other perfectly while serving opposing purposes. The cartographer and his guide are locked in a le Carre-style intelligence operation disguised as an expedition.
- **WorkX (Blood Meridian)**: McCarthy's structure of episodic, violent westward movement through an indifferent landscape. Each day's failed attempt to map the canyon provides a new encounter with the land's refusal to be captured. The judge's obsessive documentation in Blood Meridian inverts here: the cartographer's maps become instruments of their own failure.
- **WorkY (The Quiet American)**: Greene's themes of well-intentioned imperial agents who cannot see the damage they cause, and the local operative who understands the occupier better than the occupier understands himself. The cartographer believes he is merely making a map; the guide knows that maps are weapons.

## Notes
Should run 5,000-7,000 words. The landscape should be a third character -- the canyon's geology, light, acoustics, and weather should resist the cartographer's instruments in specific, concrete ways. The Apache guide should never explain his motives; the reader should understand through his actions and the cartographer's slow comprehension. The ending should leave the canyon unmapped -- the cartographer returns with incomplete charts, and the story should make the reader feel this as both failure and victory.
