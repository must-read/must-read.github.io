## Premise
In 1952 West Germany, a former schoolteacher classified as a "Follower" during denazification works as a filing clerk in the new federal government. She processes Article 131 reinstatement applications -- the law that restored former Nazi officials to their posts. Each day she stamps approvals for men whose wartime roles she can infer but not prove. One application crosses her desk bearing a name she recognizes: a colleague who informed on her Jewish neighbors. The story unfolds across a single workday as she decides whether to lose the file, approve it, or do nothing at all.

## Source Inspiration
Drawn from "Tolerance by the Numbers," specifically the devastating data on post-war accountability: "Of 8.5 million Nazi Party members, only ~100,000 faced conviction across all courts worldwide." The essay documents how "95% of defendants were classified as 'Followers' or 'Exonerated' in fast-track proceedings" and how the 1951 Article 131 law reinstated former Nazi officials into government. The concept of the "impunity funnel" -- how accountability collapses at each institutional stage -- provides the story's moral architecture.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Mantel)**: Mantel's present-tense immersion in historical consciousness -- the way she renders institutional power as bodily experience -- suits this material perfectly. Her ability to make bureaucratic process feel like a knife fight.
- **AuthorB (Ishiguro)**: Ishiguro's mastery of self-deception, of narrators who know more than they admit, creates the essential tension. The filing clerk's measured, deflecting voice should carry the weight of what she refuses to examine.
- **WorkX (The Remains of the Day)**: The structure of a single constrained journey during which the narrator gradually reveals the full scope of their complicity and loss. Here, a single workday replaces Stevens's road trip, but the principle of incremental revelation holds.
- **WorkY (The Reader)**: Schlink's themes -- the impossibility of clean moral categories in post-war Germany, the way ordinary people participated in systems they later claimed not to understand, and the inadequacy of legal frameworks to address historical evil.

## Notes
Should run 4,000-6,000 words. The story's power depends on what the clerk does NOT say -- the gap between her procedural language and the moral enormity of what passes through her hands. The ending should be ambiguous: whatever she decides about the application, it should feel like both the right choice and a betrayal. Avoid making her either a hero or a collaborator -- she should be recognizably, uncomfortably ordinary.
