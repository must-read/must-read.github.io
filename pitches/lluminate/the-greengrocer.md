## Premise
A greengrocer in a small Central European city has been placing a sign in his window every morning for eleven years. He does not remember who told him to display it or what the words mean -- they are in a language he does not speak. He only knows that removing it would be noticed. One Tuesday, the sign falls and breaks. He spends the day attempting to replace it: visiting the sign office, filling out the replacement requisition form, waiting in line at the lamination bureau. Each office directs him to another. By evening, he realizes no one in the entire municipal apparatus knows what the sign says either. Everyone has been maintaining a system of compliance whose original purpose has been lost. The story follows his night of decision: does he fabricate a new sign with different words, put up a blank sign, or leave the window empty?

## Source Inspiration
Drawn from "Organized Bewilderment" and "The Vertigo of Certainty," both of which reference Vaclav Havel's greengrocer analogy -- the man who displays communist slogans "not from belief but from survival necessity." Edwards writes that "the real conversation runs beneath the official one like a river under ice." The greengrocer becomes a symbol for compliance whose original meaning has evaporated. "The Vertigo of Certainty" extends this into epistemological territory: when the systems that produce truth lose their authority, what remains? The story takes Havel's thought experiment and literalizes it -- what happens when the sign itself is lost and must be reconstructed?

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Kafka)**: The bureaucratic nightmare of attempting to replace the sign -- offices within offices, forms that reference other forms, officials who defer to other officials -- is pure Kafka. The prose should be precise, matter-of-fact, and increasingly absurd without ever acknowledging its own absurdity.
- **AuthorB (Orwell)**: Orwell's clarity about how political language works, how compliance is manufactured, and how ordinary people navigate systems of power they did not choose. Where Kafka provides the dream-logic, Orwell provides the political specificity.
- **WorkX (The Trial)**: Kafka's structure -- a protagonist caught in an institutional process whose rules are never fully explained, each encounter revealing another layer of inaccessible authority -- provides the blueprint for the greengrocer's day of office visits.
- **WorkY (The Unbearable Lightness of Being)**: Kundera's themes of life under political pressure, the weight versus lightness of moral choices, and the way private decisions (what sign to display, what to say in public) carry existential significance in authoritarian contexts.

## Notes
Should run 3,500-5,000 words. The humor should be bone-dry and cumulative -- each bureaucratic encounter slightly more absurd than the last, but none individually outrageous. The greengrocer should be sympathetic but not heroic; his compliance is not cowardice, it is simply how he has always lived. The ending should not resolve into either rebellion or capitulation but into something stranger: the discovery that the system of signs has been operating without content for years, that compliance has become self-sustaining, and that this recognition changes nothing at all.
