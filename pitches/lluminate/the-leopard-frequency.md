## Premise
A behavioral neuroscientist discovers that the human fear response has not evolved in 200,000 years -- the amygdala still fires identically whether triggered by a predator or a push notification. She develops a therapy that can selectively dampen the ancient predator-response while leaving social and moral fear intact. The treatment works brilliantly: patients stop doom-scrolling, sleep through the night, cease hoarding supplies. But something else changes too. They stop locking their doors. They walk alone at night without checking over their shoulders. They trust strangers completely. The ancient leopard, it turns out, was also the thing that kept them cautious enough to survive. The story follows the neuroscientist as her own patients begin disappearing -- not through violence, but through a radical, terrifying openness to the world.

## Source Inspiration
Drawn from "The Evolution of Fear," which traces fear through three evolutionary stages: "the original leopard" of prehistoric predation, the community that "can either save or consume its members," and the modern internalized fear where "You can" becomes "You must." The essay's central provocation -- that we carry a 3.5-million-year-old fear response calibrated for leopards and eagles into a world of algorithmic feeds -- becomes the story's scientific premise. The archaeological evidence cited (a Paranthropus skull with leopard bite marks, a child's skeleton bearing eagle talon wounds) grounds the speculative element in real paleontology.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Butler)**: Butler's unflinching engagement with how biology constrains social possibility -- her refusal to let speculative premises resolve into easy utopias. She would insist that removing fear has consequences the inventor cannot predict, and that those consequences fall hardest on the vulnerable.
- **AuthorB (Chiang)**: Chiang's crystalline philosophical precision and his ability to follow a premise to its logical conclusion without flinching. His stories are thought experiments rendered as human drama; the neuroscientist's discovery should feel as inevitable and devastating as the revelations in "Story of Your Life."
- **WorkX (Exhalation)**: Chiang's collection models the structure needed: a single speculative premise explored through its implications for consciousness, free will, and what it means to be human. Each section of the story should peel back another layer of consequence.
- **WorkY (Parable of the Sower)**: Butler's themes of hyperempathy as both gift and vulnerability, of communities forming and dissolving under pressure, and of the tension between openness and survival. The treated patients are experiencing a biological version of Lauren Olamina's hyperempathy -- radical receptivity that makes them both more human and more endangered.

## Notes
Should run 5,000-7,000 words. The story should resist the obvious reading (fear is bad, removing it is good) and also resist the contrarian reading (fear is necessary, removing it is foolish). Instead, it should sit in the genuinely uncomfortable space where both are true. The neuroscientist should be sympathetic and brilliant and wrong in ways she cannot see. The disappearing patients should not be dying -- something else should be happening to them, something the story suggests but does not fully explain.
