## Premise
In a drought-ravaged town in the American Southwest, a retired high school shop teacher builds things no one has asked for. A half-pipe from reclaimed plywood in a foreclosed house. A birdhouse for a species that no longer migrates through the valley. A rain gauge in a place where it has not rained in fourteen months. His adult daughter, who has come home to convince him to evacuate before the water runs out, cannot understand why he will not leave. The story moves between their conversations -- spare, circling, never quite landing -- and the teacher's solitary construction projects, which are described with the technical precision of someone who knows exactly what he is doing and cannot explain why.

## Source Inspiration
Drawn from "Two Ruins," which examines meaning-making during civilizational collapse through two novels. The poem's central figure -- Ray, who "builds a useless half-pipe from plywood in an abandoned mansion" -- embodies the idea that construction in the face of destruction is "holy precisely because it's doomed." The closing assertion -- "Begin again -- not from hope, but from refusal" -- provides the story's emotional key. The poem anchors this in February 2026 specifics: "Lake Powell at 26% capacity, Colorado River Basin entirely in drought, the January 2025 Palisades Fire destroying 13,000 homes." The treatment of futile construction as sacred act drives the father's character.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (McCarthy)**: McCarthy's spare, unpunctuated prose and his treatment of landscape as the dominant force in human affairs. The drought-ravaged Southwest is McCarthy country, and the father's silence and stubbornness belong to McCarthy's lineage of men who have made a pact with a place.
- **AuthorB (Robinson)**: Robinson's meditative, theological attention to ordinary acts -- the way she renders small domestic moments as radiant with meaning. The daughter's perspective should carry Robinson's quality of reverent attention, seeing holiness in her father's futile building where he sees only necessity.
- **WorkX (The Road)**: McCarthy's structure of episodic movement through a devastated landscape, with a parent-child relationship at the center. Here the movement is inverted -- the daughter has arrived, not departed -- but the same pressure operates: the land is dying, the choice is to stay or go, and the relationship must be renegotiated under extreme conditions.
- **WorkY (Housekeeping)**: Robinson's themes of what it means to maintain a house -- to keep house, to stay -- when the world around it is becoming uninhabitable. Ruth and Sylvie's choices about staying or leaving map onto the father and daughter's negotiation. The question of whether keeping a place is an act of love or madness.

## Notes
Should run 3,000-4,500 words. The story should be quiet and slow, trusting the reader's patience. The father's building projects should be described with real technical detail -- the kind of specific craft knowledge that makes fictional construction feel embodied. The daughter should be right that they need to leave. The father should also be right that leaving would mean something worse than staying. Neither should convince the other. The story should end with both of them still in the house, not having decided, the rain gauge empty outside the window.
