## Premise
In a sealed community of 200 survivors after a global catastrophe, a utilitarian philosopher has calculated that the colony's long-term viability requires the permanent exile of eleven members whose genetic profiles predict resource consumption exceeding their productive contribution. He has written a twelve-thousand-word justification that no one reads. A second thinker -- a former rabbi -- argues that any community founded on calculated sacrifice is already dead. The story unfolds across a single governance meeting where the vote must happen, narrated by a woman who has realized she is one of the eleven.

## Source Inspiration
Drawn from "The Algebra of the Intolerable," which examines how "rational frameworks -- when divorced from ethical grounding -- become instruments of atrocity." The essay describes a character named Douglas who "believes moral reasoning is a skill most lack, like advanced calculus" and writes "a twelve-thousand-word essay ('The Algebra of Suffering') that no one reads." His utilitarian framework is "internally valid and mathematically sound, yet produces monstrousness." The essay's devastating observation -- that "Douglas's framework cannot process grief, only guilt" -- and his eventual breakdown ("I have been wrong about everything that matters, and I used numbers to hide from it") provide the philosopher's arc.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Saramago)**: Saramago's flowing, unpunctuated prose suits a story told as a single long breath -- the governance meeting should feel like it cannot be stopped once begun. His ability to render allegorical premises with humanist warmth prevents the story from becoming a mere thought experiment.
- **AuthorB (Atwood)**: Atwood's precision about how power operates through language and bureaucracy, and her refusal to let speculative premises remain abstract. She would insist on the specific, embodied cost of the philosopher's calculation -- what it means to be one of the eleven, not in theory but in the body.
- **WorkX (Blindness)**: Saramago's structure -- a group of people confined together under extreme conditions, observed by one person who can see what others cannot -- maps directly. The narrator's awareness of being on the list gives her the "sight" that others in the meeting lack.
- **WorkY (The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas)**: Le Guin's central question -- can a community's happiness be justified by one person's suffering? -- scales up here to eleven. The story should make the utilitarian argument genuinely compelling before revealing its human cost. The "walking away" is not an option in a sealed community; that is the point.

## Notes
Should run 4,000-6,000 words. The governance meeting structure provides a natural container, but the narrator's interiority -- her memories, her observations of other members, her calculations about whether to speak -- should carry as much weight as the debate itself. The philosopher should not be a villain; he should be someone who has done the math honestly and arrived at a monstrous conclusion through rigorous means. The rabbi's counter-argument should also be flawed -- he offers no alternative, only refusal. The narrator's position as one of the condemned should emerge gradually, not announced.
