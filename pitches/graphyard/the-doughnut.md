## Premise
On a generation ship designed around degrowth principles -- a closed-loop economy where every resource is tracked and no growth is possible -- the community has thrived for sixty years. But a young systems engineer discovers that the ship's ecological accounting has a flaw: a small, compounding surplus that the community has been unconsciously hoarding rather than redistributing. The surplus is tiny. The math says it doesn't matter for another century. But the discovery forces a political crisis that mirrors every argument about growth, limits, and fairness that the ship's founders thought they'd left behind on Earth.

## Source Inspiration
The "Beyond Growth" essay's exploration of degrowth as a deliberate, planned alternative to endless expansion -- and the specific challenges of implementing it. The Doughnut Economics model (outer ecological ceiling, inner social foundation) as a literal design constraint for a closed system. The essay's key tension: even communities that agree on limits will fight about distribution within those limits. The finding that "beyond material sufficiency, wellbeing comes from relationships, meaningful work, health, security, community, and leisure -- not stuff accumulation."

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Ursula K. Le Guin)**: Le Guin's anthropological worldbuilding and her ability to make political philosophy feel like lived experience. *The Dispossessed* is the benchmark for fiction that takes alternative economic systems seriously -- not as utopia but as flawed, human, specific. Her spare, precise prose would ground the ship's community in tangible daily life rather than abstraction.
- **AuthorB (Kim Stanley Robinson)**: Robinson's commitment to the technical specifics of how alternative systems actually work -- the plumbing, the politics, the committee meetings. His *Mars* trilogy and *Ministry for the Future* show that the most interesting conflicts in post-capitalist societies are not ideological but practical: who gets what, decided how, enforced by whom.
- **WorkX (The Dispossessed)**: Structural influence -- Le Guin's novel alternates between chapters on the anarchist moon and chapters on the capitalist planet, creating a dialectical structure. This story could alternate between the ship's present crisis and flashbacks to the founding debates, revealing that the founders' compromises created the flaw the engineer has discovered.
- **WorkY (The Ministry for the Future)**: Thematic influence -- Robinson's novel confronts the political economy of climate response: who pays, who benefits, who decides. The generation ship's surplus crisis is a miniature version of the same question: when a system designed for sustainability accidentally produces abundance, the political fight over that abundance recreates the dynamics the system was designed to escape.

## Notes
The essay's finding that "wealth inequality exceeds income inequality" suggests the ship's hidden surplus should be in a form that some community members benefit from more than others -- perhaps a computational resource, or a reserve of biological material, or simply time (some families have been quietly accumulating leisure hours while others haven't). The engineer should not be a revolutionary; she's someone who found a bug in a system she believes in, and the bug turns out to be human nature.
