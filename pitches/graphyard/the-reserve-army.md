## Premise
A gig worker for a delivery app notices that the algorithm has started sending her to the same house every night -- a suburban home where no one answers the door, the porch light is always on, and the food she leaves is always gone by morning. The app's tracking shows her completing the delivery. The customer's rating is always five stars. Over two weeks, the deliveries escalate: the orders become stranger (quantities for twelve, then twenty, then fifty people), the house begins showing signs of recent renovation that wasn't there the night before, and other drivers she talks to deny that the address exists. The story is about a woman trapped in a system that sees her only as a data point discovering that the system has its own appetites.

## Source Inspiration
The Marx essay's analysis of gig workers as a "reserve army" -- available on demand, paid only during active use, immediately discarded. The essay's specific details: "Algorithm surveys every action; every rating matters. Bear entrepreneurship risks without entrepreneurship rewards or autonomy. Employees without employment rights, contractors without contracts, workers without legal protection." The essay also describes Amazon warehouse workers whose "handheld scanner tracking every movement" and "cameras and software constant surveillance" represent "the dark satanic mill of digital age." The horror version: what if the algorithm isn't just tracking you but feeding something?

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Ottessa Moshfegh)**: Moshfegh's unflinching interiority -- protagonists who are isolated, self-aware, slightly repulsive, and utterly compelling. Her characters notice everything and trust nothing, which makes them perfect vessels for horror that operates through accumulating wrongness rather than sudden shocks. The gig worker's growing unease would be filtered through Moshfegh's quality of disgusted clarity.
- **AuthorB (Yoko Ogawa)**: Ogawa's quiet, domestic horror -- the way ordinary objects and routines become sinister through subtle displacement. Her prose is calm and precise even when describing the impossible, which makes the impossible more frightening. The house that changes, the orders that escalate, would have Ogawa's quality of dreamlike wrongness that never announces itself as horror.
- **WorkX (Severance)**: Structural influence -- Ling Ma's novel about a woman who keeps going to work during an apocalypse because routine is more powerful than survival instinct. The gig worker keeps making the deliveries because the algorithm tells her to, and because stopping means losing income. The structure follows the escalation of deliveries, each one slightly more wrong than the last.
- **WorkY (The Memory Police)**: Thematic influence -- Ogawa's novel about an island where things disappear and people forget they existed. The other drivers who deny the address exists, the customer whose identity cannot be verified, the house that keeps changing -- the story takes the gig economy's already dehumanizing anonymity and pushes it into a space where the worker herself might be the thing that disappears.

## Notes
The horror should be quiet and systemic, not gory. The scariest thing about the story is that the gig worker cannot afford to stop making deliveries, even as the deliveries become clearly wrong. Her economic precarity is the cage. The five-star rating that appears after each delivery is a detail that should become increasingly sinister -- who is rating her, and for what? The story does not need to explain what is in the house; the algorithm's indifference to her safety is the horror.
