## Premise
In 1991, a self-taught statistician who works for the New York City water department has just been hired as a secret consultant by a major league baseball team. His analysis is brilliant. His social skills are catastrophic. When a newspaper reporter discovers the arrangement and writes a mocking profile -- describing his appearance, his cramped apartment full of printouts, his lack of credentials -- the baseball world turns him into a punchline. The story follows the week between the article's publication and the team's decision to fire him, during which he watches his work validated and his person destroyed, all while the metrics he developed are quietly adopted by the same people who laughed at him.

## Source Inspiration
The Bill James article's devastating account of Mike Gimbel -- the NYC water department employee who taught himself statistics, developed his own player evaluation system, consulted for the Expos and Red Sox, and was publicly humiliated when the Boston Globe exposed his unconventional appearance and self-taught methods. "Gimbelized" became a cautionary term among analytically-minded insiders warning against exposing statistical methods publicly. The article notes his Wikipedia page doesn't exist despite his contributions. A man who was right about everything and rewarded with erasure.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Philip Roth)**: Roth's ability to inhabit the mind of an obsessive, brilliant, socially wounded American man -- someone whose intelligence is both his gift and the source of his isolation. Roth's protagonists rage against the institutions that refuse to see them, and their rage is both justified and self-defeating. The Newark/New York Jewish intellectual tradition gives this character a specific cultural grounding.
- **AuthorB (George Saunders)**: Saunders brings the institutional absurdity -- the corporate voice of baseball front offices, the way organizations use and discard people, the cruelty that operates through politeness. His radical empathy would prevent the story from becoming a simple revenge fantasy: everyone in the story, including the reporter, is doing what their institutional position requires.
- **WorkX (The Loser by Thomas Bernhard)**: Structural influence -- Bernhard's novel about a pianist who encounters genius (Glenn Gould) and is destroyed by the comparison. The structure is a monologue that circles obsessively around the moment of recognition and its aftermath. The statistician's week of vindication-and-destruction would have this spiraling, returning quality.
- **WorkY (A Confederacy of Dunces)**: Thematic influence -- the brilliant eccentric rejected by every institution he encounters, whose genius is visible only to the reader. Ignatius J. Reilly as a model for a protagonist who is simultaneously right about the world and unable to survive in it. The comedy and the tragedy are inseparable.

## Notes
The article's detail that Gimbel's analysis helped build the competitive 1992 Expos -- a team that nearly won the World Series before the 1994 strike -- adds another layer of erasure: even his best work was undermined by forces beyond his control. The story should be funny and painful in equal measure. The baseball establishment's resistance to analytics was not just stupidity; it was also a defense of a community's self-understanding, which makes the rejection more human and more cruel.
