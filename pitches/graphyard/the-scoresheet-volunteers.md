## Premise
In the summer of 1986, three strangers -- a retired actuary, a college dropout working at a copy shop, and a divorced schoolteacher -- meet at a minor league ballpark as volunteers for Project Scoresheet, manually recording every pitch, every batted ball, every defensive play on hand-drawn scoresheets and cassette tape recorders. Over the course of one season, they become the kind of friends you can only make through shared obsessive devotion to something no one else cares about. Thirty-eight years later, the college dropout -- now a senior data scientist at a tech company -- receives an email that the actuary has died and left him three cardboard boxes full of scoresheets from that summer. The data they collected by hand in 1986 is now worth more than any of them imagined, and worth less than the friendships they built collecting it.

## Source Inspiration
The Bill James article's account of Project Scoresheet (founded 1984) -- volunteers who attended games and "meticulously recorded every play on scoresheets and cassette recorders." This became STATS, Inc., which became the foundation of modern sports analytics infrastructure. The article's observation that "data collection itself is analytical -- what you choose to record shapes analyzable questions." These volunteers were doing something that looked like obsessive fandom and turned out to be revolutionary, but the article is most moving when it treats them not as precursors to Moneyball but as people who loved a game enough to sit in the stands and write down everything that happened.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Richard Russo)**: Russo's warm, generous prose about small-city working-class Americans who find meaning in loyalty, habit, and institutions that don't always deserve their devotion. His characters are funny without being satirized; their limitations are visible but treated with affection. The three volunteers need Russo's combination of humor and tenderness -- they are ridiculous and admirable.
- **AuthorB (Amor Towles)**: Towles's elegance and his fascination with people who build entire worlds within constrained circumstances. *A Gentleman in Moscow* is about a man who creates a meaningful life within a single building; the volunteers create a meaningful community around a single activity. Towles's prose would give the actuary -- the oldest, most precise, most devoted -- a certain dignity and formality that offsets Russo's warmth.
- **WorkX (Empire Falls)**: Structural influence -- Russo's novel about a dying New England town, told through the daily routines and small interactions that constitute a life. The story would use the 1986 season's rhythm -- game by game, scoresheet by scoresheet -- to structure the friendship's development. The present-day frame (receiving the boxes) bookends the deeper story.
- **WorkY (Kavalier and Clay)**: Thematic influence -- Chabon's novel about two men who pour their dreams into comic books before the form is taken seriously by the culture at large. The volunteers are doing the same thing with baseball data: creating something the world doesn't yet know it needs. The novel's engagement with how popular culture becomes art, and how art becomes an industry, mirrors the trajectory from hand-drawn scoresheets to billion-dollar analytics departments.

## Notes
The story's emotional core is the gap between what the data is worth (enormously, as the foundation of modern sports analytics) and what the experience of collecting it was worth (priceless, unreproducible, deeply personal). The actuary's death and the boxes of scoresheets should function the way a time capsule does: evidence of a version of yourself you can barely remember being. The 1986 setting matters -- pre-internet, pre-Moneyball, a world where recording data by hand was the only way to get it.
