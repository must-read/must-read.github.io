## Premise
A veteran education professor at a mid-tier state university has spent her entire thirty-year career teaching learning styles theory to future teachers. She knows -- has known for at least a decade -- that the research shows the effect size is essentially zero. She has watched her department incorporate this knowledge selectively, has attended the conferences, has read the Pashler review. She continues teaching learning styles because her students need to pass the state certification exam, which still tests for it, and because her tenure review is next year, and because every textbook her department has adopted includes a chapter on it. The story follows her through one semester during which a first-generation graduate student in her class reads the research and asks the question she has spent a decade not asking herself.

## Source Inspiration
The "Learning Styles Myth" essay's stunning statistic: 90% of educators believe students learn better when taught in their preferred style, while the research shows the effect size is 0.04. The essay's analysis of why the myth persists -- institutional inertia, intuitive appeal, investment in prior effort, confirmation bias -- describes a systemic failure that is also a deeply personal one for anyone who has built a career around the wrong answer. The finding that "higher education researchers: 89% implicitly endorse learning styles" means this is not a problem of ignorant practitioners but of the profession's knowledge infrastructure.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Lorrie Moore)**: Moore's precision with academic and professional women whose intelligence is both their defining quality and the instrument of their self-deception. Her wit -- sentences that are simultaneously funny and devastating -- would prevent the professor from becoming either a villain or a victim. Moore's characters know exactly what they're doing and do it anyway.
- **AuthorB (Kazuo Ishiguro)**: Ishiguro's mastery of the narrator who reveals complicity through the very language of professionalism and duty. The professor's voice -- measured, reasonable, full of qualifications -- would gradually expose the architecture of her accommodation. The Never Let Me Go quality of a system that everyone inside it has agreed not to question.
- **WorkX (Stoner)**: Structural influence -- John Williams's novel about a man who devotes his life to a university and is ground down by its politics, mediocrities, and institutional cowardice. The novel's quiet devastation comes from watching a good mind fail to fight the system it serves. The professor's semester follows Stoner's arc in miniature: a life of accommodation confronted by one person who won't accommodate.
- **WorkY (Educated)**: Thematic influence -- Westover's memoir about discovering that everything she was taught was wrong, and the cost of that discovery. The graduate student's intellectual awakening echoes Westover's, but from the other side: this time, the person doing the wrong teaching is not a fundamentalist parent but a tenured professor who knows better.

## Notes
The essay's comparison between Finland's educational decline and Estonia's quiet rise -- two approaches to evidence-based education, one failing, one succeeding -- could appear as a subplot: the professor attended a conference in Helsinki a decade ago and saw the warning signs. The story should resist making the graduate student a hero; the essay's own analysis of the "Science of Reading" movement shows that change takes decades and comes from coalition-building, not individual acts of courage. The professor's accommodation may be the realistic response to an intractable system.
