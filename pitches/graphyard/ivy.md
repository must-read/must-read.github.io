## Premise
A young data scientist joins a struggling minor league baseball team's front office in 2013, building a proprietary player evaluation system she names after the ivy on the parent club's outfield walls. Over four years she watches the system work -- identifying an undervalued pitcher everyone else had given up on, flagging the exact trade that would complete the roster -- and then watches it fail to predict the human things: the domestic violence arrest, the contract that becomes an albatross, the way a championship window closes faster than any model forecasts. The story ends not with the championship but two years after, when she's dismantling the system she built.

## Source Inspiration
The Cubs article's detailed account of the "Ivy" operating system -- the revolutionary analytics platform built by Ryan Kruse and Jeff Greenberg that aggregated data by player name rather than team affiliation. The article's most compelling arc is not the championship itself but the aftermath: the rapid window closure, the farm system depletion, the prospect trades that looked brilliant in 2016 and devastating by 2020. The article's conclusion that "championship windows remain shorter than expected" despite sophisticated planning.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Michael Chabon)**: Chabon's lush, enthusiastic prose and his love for the romance of American institutions -- baseball, comic books, the spaces where passion and craft intersect. His sentences have an excited, digressive energy that would serve the joy of discovery in the early sections. *Summerland* proves he can write baseball; *Wonder Boys* proves he can write the gap between talent and fulfillment.
- **AuthorB (Denis Johnson)**: Johnson's raw, hallucinatory prose -- the way beauty and degradation coexist in the same sentence. As the story darkens (the arrest, the decline, the dismantling), Johnson's fractured consciousness and spiritual yearning amid wreckage would take over the prose register. The shift from Chabon's warmth to Johnson's rawness mirrors the protagonist's journey.
- **WorkX (Moneyball)**: Structural influence -- the narrative of using data to see what conventional wisdom cannot, told through the specific human relationships between analysts and the athletes they evaluate. But this story inverts Moneyball's triumphalism: the system works and still isn't enough.
- **WorkY (The Great Gatsby)**: Thematic influence -- the green light at the end of the dock, the thing you build your life around reaching, and what happens after you reach it. The championship is the green light; the story is about the morning after. The protagonist, like Nick Carraway, is the observer-participant who builds the machinery of someone else's dream.

## Notes
The article's detail about Gleyber Torres being traded for Aroldis Chapman -- one prospect's entire career for a few playoff innings -- is the kind of transaction that becomes a story about what we're willing to sacrifice for a single moment. The protagonist should understand both sides of the trade: the ruthless logic that says "do it" and the human cost the model can't quantify. The Heyward contract (8 years, $184 million for declining production) is the system's blind spot made material.
