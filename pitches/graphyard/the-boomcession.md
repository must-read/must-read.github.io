## Premise
In a near-future American city, a government statistician responsible for the Consumer Sentiment Index discovers that the Bureau has been quietly adjusting its methodology to make the numbers look better -- not through fraud exactly, but through a series of technical decisions that each seem reasonable in isolation and together produce a fiction. She begins keeping a parallel, unadjusted index on her personal laptop. As the official numbers and her shadow numbers diverge further, she must decide what to do with a truth that no one in power wants to hear, and that the public, exhausted by contradictory information, may no longer be able to absorb.

## Source Inspiration
The "Boomcession" essay's central revelation: that the rolling ten-year correlation between real GDP growth and consumer sentiment, which historically hovered near 0.7-0.8, collapsed after 2015 and turned negative by 2021. The economy's measuring stick broke. The essay's specific detail that GDP counts "financial services furnished without payment" and gambling as productive economic activity -- that the measurement itself has become ideological. The finding that the top 20% command 60% of consumer spending while the bottom 80% accounts for only 40%.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Jennifer Egan)**: Egan's ability to capture how technology and institutional systems reshape human consciousness. Her prose is precise, contemporary, and structurally inventive -- *A Visit from the Goon Squad* showed she could make PowerPoint slides feel like literature. A story about statistics and measurement benefits from her formal inventiveness: sections structured around data tables, index values, methodology notes that double as narrative.
- **AuthorB (Ling Ma)**: Ma's *Severance* proved she could make corporate-institutional dystopia feel both absurd and emotionally devastating. Her characters are complicit in the systems that dehumanize them, and they know it, and they keep showing up to work. The statistician's daily participation in a measurement system she no longer believes would have Ma's quality of exhausted, clear-eyed complicity.
- **WorkX (White Noise)**: Structural influence -- DeLillo's novel about a family surrounded by consumer products, data streams, and toxic events they can sense but not articulate. The "airborne toxic event" as a model for a crisis that is simultaneously real and mediated by information systems that cannot accurately describe it.
- **WorkY (Nickel and Dimed)**: Thematic influence -- Ehrenreich's immersion in low-wage work revealed the economy that official statistics obscured. The protagonist's shadow index is a fictional version of what Ehrenreich did: looking at the same economy through a lens that the official measurement refuses to provide.

## Notes
The essay's finding that "Costa Rica ($13,420 GDP per capita) achieves 80.8-year life expectancy exceeding U.S. performance (77.5 years) with one-fifth American income" could appear in the story as a detail the protagonist discovers and cannot stop thinking about. The ending should resist resolution: the protagonist doesn't become a whistleblower or a hero. She has the data and no institution willing to act on it, which is the essay's central despair -- that measurement failure produces political paralysis.
