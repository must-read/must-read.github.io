## Premise
In a post-authoritarian American future, a community organizer in a mid-sized Southern city runs a "civic reconstruction" program -- teaching democratic process to teenagers who grew up entirely under the regime and have no memory of functioning institutions. One of her students, a seventeen-year-old whose father was a mid-level regime official, begins asking questions that expose the gap between the democratic values she teaches and the compromises the transitional government has already made. The organizer realizes she is teaching an ideal that the adults around her have already betrayed, and the student is the only person in the room honest enough to say so.

## Source Inspiration
The essay's conclusion that "tolerance is a relay race, not a sprint or marathon" and its analysis of Germany's 20-25 year timeline for building genuine democratic culture through generational change. Young Germans in 1945 spent their entire young adulthood building something they would not see completed until middle age. The essay's specific finding that "civic and political mobilization, not courts or individual leaders, are primary recovery mechanisms" -- and the corollary that 90% of democratic recoveries fail. The organizer's work is the 10% case: the unglamorous, local, institutional work that the essay argues is the only thing that works.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie)**: Adichie's precise, luminous realism and her ability to write about political systems through intimate human relationships. Her warmth is always laced with political edge -- her characters navigate power structures with full awareness of what those structures cost. The organizer needs Adichie's combination of idealism and clear-eyed pragmatism.
- **AuthorB (Mohsin Hamid)**: Hamid's second-person address and his ability to make the reader complicit in the story's political dynamics. *Exit West* showed how ordinary life continues inside political catastrophe; *The Reluctant Fundamentalist* showed how a conversation between two people can contain an entire geopolitical argument. The student-teacher dynamic would benefit from Hamid's tension between intimacy and confrontation.
- **WorkX (The Underground Railroad)**: Structural influence -- Whitehead's novel literalizes a metaphor (the underground railroad as actual railroad), creating a slightly surreal American landscape where historical violence is made physically present. The "civic reconstruction" classroom could function similarly: a space where abstract democratic values are made concrete through exercises and role-playing that keep revealing the gap between theory and practice.
- **WorkY (The Handmaid's Tale)**: Thematic influence -- Atwood's novel is about the generation that lives inside the regime, but this story is about the generation that comes after. The Handmaid's Tale asks "how does authoritarianism begin?" This story asks the harder question: "how does a society learn to be free again when freedom is not something anyone alive remembers?"

## Notes
The essay's breakdown of post-MAGA institutional reforms -- "reforms to presidential pardon power, DOJ independence, congressional subpoena enforcement, emergency powers guardrails" -- are exactly the kind of unsexy, procedural changes that would be background to this story. The teenager's father being a former regime official creates the essay's core tension: you cannot build a winning coalition by writing off a third of the electorate, but the people you need to include are the people who caused the damage. The organizer must teach democracy to the child of someone who helped dismantle it.
