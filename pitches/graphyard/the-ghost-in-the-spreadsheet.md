## Premise
A dead economist -- not Marx, but a fictional mid-century Keynesian who helped design the postwar welfare state -- haunts the Bureau of Labor Statistics, watching each monthly jobs report get assembled. He observes the living economists at their desks, notes what they count and what they choose not to count, and gradually understands that the numbers themselves have become a kind of ideology. When he tries to communicate with a young data analyst who suspects the metrics are broken, she can only hear him as static in her earbuds.

## Source Inspiration
The "Capital in the Twenty-First Century: A Ghost's Perspective" essay -- its conceit of a dead thinker examining modern data -- combined with the "Boomcession" essay's central finding that the correlation between GDP growth and consumer sentiment has collapsed and even inverted. The idea that measurement shapes reality, that "what we measure matters," and that the economy's measuring stick has become detached from the thing it claims to measure.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (George Saunders)**: Saunders's ability to make the absurd corporate-institutional voice feel both satirical and deeply humane. His vernacular characters in surreal institutional settings -- cubicle workers in purgatory, theme-park employees trapped in their roles -- map perfectly onto government economists trapped in metrics that no longer describe reality.
- **AuthorB (Jose Saramago)**: Saramago's matter-of-fact magical realism and his treatment of institutional systems as both absurd and terrifying. His prose style -- long, unwinding sentences without conventional punctuation -- could create the ghost's perspective as a continuous stream of observation.
- **WorkX (Lincoln in the Bardo)**: Structural influence -- multiple dead voices narrating from an institutional afterlife, the living unable to hear them, documents and reports interspersed with the ghost's observations. The polyphonic structure of competing perspectives on what is "real."
- **WorkY (Invisible Man)**: Thematic influence -- the experience of being rendered invisible by systems that claim to see you, the way institutions create blindness as a feature rather than a bug. The young analyst, like Ellison's narrator, begins to see what everyone around her has agreed not to see.

## Notes
The "Boomcession" essay's specific detail that GDP counts "financial services furnished without payment" (below-market deposit rates) as economic activity is the kind of institutional absurdity that Saunders-inflected prose could make both hilarious and devastating. The ghost should have been wrong about some things in his lifetime too -- the story shouldn't be a simple critique of the present through the wisdom of the past. He designed systems that had their own blind spots.
