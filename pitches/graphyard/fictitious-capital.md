## Premise
A junior analyst at a Wall Street firm specializing in collateralized debt obligations discovers she can predict which financial instruments will fail -- not through analysis but through a synesthetic sensation, a physical revulsion when she handles certain portfolios. Her boss, who has been packaging worthless debt into complex instruments for years, recognizes her ability and begins using her as a human canary in a coal mine: she signs off on the products that don't make her sick, and he sells the rest to pension funds. The story follows one week in which a portfolio makes her violently ill but her boss insists it's clean, and she must choose between her body's knowledge and the institution's certainty.

## Source Inspiration
The Marx essay's analysis of "fictitious capital" -- financial claims on future value rather than actual productive assets. The essay's specific tracing of the 2008 crisis: "mortgage-backed securities, collateralized debt obligations, and credit default swaps created multi-trillion-dollar financial claims based on approximately $1 trillion in subprime mortgage assets." The observation that the financial sector grew from 4% of GDP in 1970 to over 8%, and that the circuit M-C-M' (money to commodities to more money) has been replaced by M-M' (money making money without production). The essay's devastating conclusion: "No major banker prosecuted. Workers lost homes and jobs. Socialization of losses, privatization of gains."

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (William Gibson)**: Gibson's noir-inflected, densely textured prose -- the way he renders technology and finance as environments that reshape human perception. His characters navigate systems they partially understand, sensing patterns they can't fully articulate. The analyst's synesthetic response to bad debt would feel at home in Gibson's world, where the body knows what the mind hasn't caught up to.
- **AuthorB (Mariana Enriquez)**: Enriquez brings Latin American horror into urban, institutional settings -- her stories make the mundane suddenly visceral, bodies rebelling against the systems that contain them. The analyst's physical revulsion at toxic financial instruments would have Enriquez's quality of body horror: something is wrong in the most literal, physical sense, and the institution demands she ignore it.
- **WorkX (Cosmopolis)**: Structural influence -- DeLillo's novel follows a billionaire across Manhattan in a single day as his fortune disintegrates in real-time. The compressed timeframe (one week), the financial setting, and the protagonist trapped inside a moving system she cannot stop. The novel's rhythm of market data interspersed with human interaction.
- **WorkY (American Psycho)**: Thematic influence -- Ellis's novel about a man whose monstrousness is invisible because it is indistinguishable from the culture around him. The boss's crime is legal, or nearly so, and the system rewards it. The analyst's ability to sense what is wrong is the real aberration -- not the fraud but the person who can detect it. The horror is not that the system is broken but that it is working exactly as designed.

## Notes
The essay's observation that "share buybacks now exceed capital investment spending" and that executives whose compensation ties to stock price benefit from artificial price inflation rather than productive investment could provide background texture. The analyst's synesthetic ability should not be presented as magical -- it's a metaphor for the physical cost of proximity to institutional fraud. The story is noir because the outcome is inevitable: the boss will sell the portfolio, the analyst will lose either her integrity or her job, and the pension fund will eat the loss.
