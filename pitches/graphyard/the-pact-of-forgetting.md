## Premise
Three siblings in a Spanish family discover, while clearing their recently deceased grandmother's apartment in Madrid, a cache of documents revealing their grandfather -- whom they knew as a beloved retired schoolteacher -- served as a municipal judge during the Franco regime and signed execution orders. Each sibling responds differently: one wants to publish the documents, one wants to burn them, one wants to understand. The story is told through the documents themselves, text messages between the siblings, and fragments of the grandmother's unfinished memoir, which she started and abandoned three times over forty years.

## Source Inspiration
The essay's devastating section on Spain's "Pacto del Olvido" -- the Pact of Forgetting. Spain chose zero prosecutions, zero exhumations, zero accountability, and became a stable, prosperous democracy with a Freedom House score of 94. Yet 114,000 dead remain in unmarked mass graves. The essay's core tension: the pragmatic logic of forgetting worked by every conventional measure, yet something remains unresolved. The 2022 Democratic Memory Law declared the regime illegal but never repealed the 1977 Amnesty Law. The paradox: Spain's success as a democracy was built on a foundation of silence.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Javier Marias)**: Spain's greatest modern novelist, whose long, digressive, philosophically precise sentences circle around secrets and complicity. *Your Face Tomorrow* and *A Heart So White* are about the things families and nations agree not to say. His prose rhythm -- the way a sentence can delay its meaning through subordinate clauses -- enacts the experience of approaching a truth you don't want to reach.
- **AuthorB (Rachel Cusk)**: Cusk's austere, observational style where characters reveal themselves through what they say to a listener who barely speaks. Her *Outline* trilogy's structure -- other people's stories reflecting the narrator's unspoken situation -- could shape the sibling who wants to understand, the one who mostly listens.
- **WorkX (Austerlitz)**: Structural influence -- Sebald's method of accumulating photographs, documents, architectural descriptions, and embedded narratives to circle around historical trauma. The story would use the grandmother's documents and abandoned memoir fragments the way Sebald uses photographs: as evidence that is also a kind of silence.
- **WorkY (A Visit from the Goon Squad)**: Thematic influence -- time as the force that transforms everything, the way the same events mean different things across decades. The story spans multiple timeframes through its documents: the grandfather's Franco-era judicial records, the grandmother's three attempts at memoir (1978, 1995, 2018), and the siblings' present-day texts.

## Notes
The essay notes that "only Cambodia has more" unmarked mass graves than Spain. The grandmother's three abandoned memoirs -- each begun at a moment of Spanish political change (transition, EU membership, democratic memory debates) -- would chart the nation's shifting relationship to its own past through one woman's failed attempts to write it down. The sibling who wants to burn the documents isn't a villain; the essay makes clear that Spain's calculation was rational, even if it was morally incomplete.
