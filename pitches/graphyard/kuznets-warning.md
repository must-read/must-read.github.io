## Premise
An economist at a national statistics bureau, recently separated from her husband, flies to a conference in a European capital where she is supposed to present a paper on alternative welfare metrics. On the flight, at the hotel, at conference dinners, she listens to other people describe their lives -- a seatmate's failing business, a hotel clerk's immigration story, a colleague's research on happiness measurement in Bhutan -- and each conversation becomes a lens through which she examines the collapse of her own marriage. She had spent years developing a "Genuine Progress Indicator" for her country, adjusting GDP for inequality and environmental damage, and the resulting number was always lower than the official figure. Her marriage, she realizes, had a similar accounting problem: the headline number looked fine while the adjustments told a different story.

## Source Inspiration
The GDP essay's central insight, quoting Kuznets himself: "The welfare of a nation can scarcely be inferred from a measurement of national income." The essay's exploration of alternative measures -- the Genuine Progress Indicator, which "adjusts personal consumption by adding household work value and volunteer contributions while subtracting crime costs, pollution damage, and inequality effects," and the finding that "U.S. GPI per capita has remained relatively flat since the 1970s despite rising GDP per capita." The metaphorical resonance: a marriage whose GDP (income, house, career milestones) looks healthy while its GPI (emotional connection, shared purpose, intimacy) has been flat for years.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Rachel Cusk)**: Cusk's *Outline* trilogy invented a form for exactly this story: a woman who travels to a foreign city, listens to strangers narrate their lives, and through their stories assembles a negative-space portrait of her own situation. The austere, observational prose -- other people revealing themselves while the narrator remains mostly opaque -- is the perfect register for a character whose professional life is about measurement and whose personal life resists it.
- **AuthorB (Lydia Davis)**: Davis's extreme compression and her ability to make a single sentence feel like a complete story. Her work often treats domestic and intellectual life as inseparable: a thought about philosophy leads to a thought about dishes. The economist's conference conversations would alternate with Davis-inflected micro-observations -- a hotel room's emptiness, the precise weight of a name badge, the mathematics of a bill split.
- **WorkX (Outline)**: Structural influence -- the novel's architecture of embedded narratives, each told by a stranger to the protagonist, each inadvertently illuminating her situation. The conference setting provides natural access to multiple speakers, each presenting a different way of measuring what matters.
- **WorkY (Dept. of Speculation)**: Thematic influence -- Offill's novel about a marriage told in fragments, where the accumulation of small details reveals the shape of a relationship more honestly than any narrative summary could. The economist's realization that her marriage needed a different metric is Offill's central insight in economic language.

## Notes
The essay's specific detail that "leisure absence" means "reduced working hours with stable productivity register as GDP decline despite potential welfare improvements" is a metaphor the protagonist could discover mid-story: her separation has given her more time alone, which feels like a decline but might be a genuine improvement. The conference paper she is supposed to present would be the story's structural spine, but the paper itself is never shown -- only the conversations that surround it.
