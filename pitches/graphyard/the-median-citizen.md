## Premise
A tax accountant in a quiet Midwestern city becomes obsessed with tracing exactly where her clients' federal tax dollars go -- not in the abstract way that budget pie charts show, but to specific line items: this many cents to a drone program, this many to a detention facility's food contract, this many to a missile that struck a specific building on a specific date. The essay follows her descent from professional curiosity into something that upends her practice, her marriage, and her understanding of complicity. She can never go back to not knowing the math.

## Source Inspiration
The "Blood Money" essay's central conceit: calculating the "Median Taxpayer Military Contribution" to quantify individual complicity in state violence. The essay traces this across Victorian Britain, Nazi Germany, Maoist China, and modern America. The most unsettling implication is not that citizens fund atrocity -- everyone abstractly knows this -- but that the math is specific and calculable. Your particular tax return funded a knowable fraction of a knowable thing.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (W.G. Sebald)**: Sebald's prose style -- accumulative, digressive, haunted by documents and photographs -- perfectly suits a narrative that follows a paper trail into moral territory. His work is always about the way the past deposits itself in records, buildings, and bodies. The tax accountant's spreadsheets would become the kind of documentary evidence Sebald embeds in his narratives.
- **AuthorB (Herta Muller)**: Muller's spare, sensory prose about living inside systems of state violence -- the way she renders the texture of complicity and surveillance from the inside. Her characters know what is happening and cannot stop participating. The accountant's growing awareness would have Muller's quality of claustrophobic lucidity.
- **WorkX (The White Album)**: Structural influence -- Didion's essay collection as a model for creative nonfiction that braids personal experience with cultural disintegration. The fragmented structure -- short sections, each with its own internal logic, accumulating into a portrait of a mind under pressure.
- **WorkY (Eichmann in Jerusalem)**: Thematic influence -- the banality of evil, the way ordinary institutional processes produce extraordinary harm, and the question of whether understanding the mechanism constitutes a defense or an indictment. Arendt's central insight that evil is often not demonic but bureaucratic is exactly what the accountant discovers.

## Notes
The creative nonfiction genre allows this to be presented as a crafted personal essay rather than fiction, which may be more powerful given the material. The accountant should not be a political activist or a saint -- she's good at her job and liked the orderliness of tax work. Her crisis is not ideological but mathematical: the numbers add up to something she can't unknow. The essay's detail about the "impunity funnel" -- the gap between complicity and consequence -- would haunt the piece.
