## Premise
At a mid-size insurance company, a compliance officer whose job is to enforce regulations that everyone -- including her supervisors -- knows are purposeless begins documenting her daily tasks in a private journal. Each entry starts with what she did and ends with what it accomplished, and the answer is always nothing. The journal entries grow stranger as she starts calculating the economic value of her non-work: the GDP contribution of her salary, the supply chains her lunch activates, the downstream effects of her health insurance claims. She discovers that her greatest economic contribution is not her compliance work but the fact that she exists as a consumer. The story is a comedy about a woman who realizes she is more useful to capitalism as a body that purchases things than as a mind that works.

## Source Inspiration
The Marx essay's discussion of "bullshit jobs" -- positions whose holders believe they contribute nothing -- and the finding that 15-20% of workers describe their own jobs as pointless. The essay's analysis of "aspirational alienation ideology": workers encouraged to be "passionate" about work, to see themselves as "team members," to bring their "whole selves" to jobs that produce nothing. The specific detail that "work-as-calling ideology obscures fundamental exploitation relationship; worker love merely makes extraction more efficient." Also the concept of workers as simultaneously consumer and product in the digital economy.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (George Saunders)**: Saunders is the master of the corporate-absurdist voice -- characters trapped in institutional systems that speak in the language of empowerment while enacting control. His protagonists often have a dawning awareness that arrives too late and changes nothing, which is simultaneously the funniest and saddest thing about them. The compliance officer's journal entries would shift from Saunders's deadpan corporate-speak to something more desperate and more alive.
- **AuthorB (Sayaka Murata)**: Murata's *Convenience Store Woman* is the ideal complement -- a protagonist who finds purpose in a system everyone else considers beneath her, and whose clarity about her own meaninglessness is more threatening to those around her than any rebellion would be. Murata's flat, affectless prose about bodily existence inside capitalism ("I am a store worker. That is what I am.") would shape the compliance officer's growing identification with her role as a consumer rather than a worker.
- **WorkX (Then We Came to the End)**: Structural influence -- Ferris's novel is told in first-person plural ("we"), capturing the collective consciousness of an office during layoffs. The compliance officer's journal could begin in first-person singular and gradually shift as she starts seeing herself as a data point in an economic system -- "we purchase, we consume, we generate demand."
- **WorkY (Bartleby, the Scrivener)**: Thematic influence -- Melville's story about a worker who simply stops working ("I would prefer not to") remains the foundational text about refusal within institutional systems. But the compliance officer's version is more insidious: she doesn't refuse. She keeps working, keeps producing nothing, and the system doesn't notice the difference. Her discovery is worse than Bartleby's: at least Bartleby's refusal was visible.

## Notes
The story should be funny first and philosophical second. The Marx essay's observation that "gig workers experience peculiar alienation: own means of production but depend utterly on externally-controlled platforms" could appear as the compliance officer's comparison with her Uber driver -- he owns his car and is trapped; she owns her education and is trapped. Different traps, same architecture. The ending should not resolve into either despair or liberation; the compliance officer goes to work on Monday.
