## Premise
A young journalist at a Halifax newspaper discovers that the beloved senior editor -- a large, graceful Black man who moves through the newsroom like a legend -- is not who he says he is. Not a criminal, not a fraud, but someone who decades ago walked away from his entire American life and rebuilt himself in Canada, constructing a new identity not to escape prosecution but to escape a self he could no longer inhabit. The journalist must decide whether to investigate or to honor the reinvention. The story is told years later, after the editor has died, when the journalist can no longer ask the only question that mattered: was the man he became more real than the man he left behind?

## Source Inspiration
Jon Tattrie's article in The Walrus about Charles Saunders, a legendary Halifax Daily News editor described as "built like a heavyweight boxer, but he moved like a cat." Colleagues discovered he "hadn't always been a Canadian" and had "originated in America" before relocating northward decades earlier. The hidden past contrasted sharply with his beloved status as a Canadian cultural institution. The article captures the particular vertigo of discovering that someone you admired had a secret origin.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Kazuo Ishiguro (style)**: The restrained narration that withholds more than it reveals, the way memory distorts and self-serving recall shapes the story we tell about others. His unreliable narrators who don't know they're unreliable.
- **Paul Auster (style)**: The doubling of identity, the way a man can become someone else through sustained performance until the performance becomes the truth. His fascination with disappearance and reinvention as American themes.
- **Austerlitz (structure)**: Sebald's archaeological approach to identity -- peeling back layers of a life through photographs, documents, and uncertain recollections. The story of a person whose past was hidden from them (here, hidden by them).
- **The Talented Mr. Ripley (themes)**: Identity as performance, the question of whether a constructed self is any less authentic than an inherited one. But inverted: Ripley kills to become someone better; Raines simply walks away.

## Notes
This is not a mystery about what the editor did in America. The story should resist the investigative impulse. The American past should remain mostly unknown -- a few fragments, a few guesses. The real subject is the journalist's belated recognition that he envied the editor's ability to become someone new, and that his reluctance to investigate was not ethics but complicity in a fantasy he wished were available to him.
