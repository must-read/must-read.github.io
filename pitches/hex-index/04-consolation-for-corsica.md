## Premise
A philosophy professor whose wife died eight months ago has been writing her daily letters -- a practice her therapist encouraged and she now cannot stop. The letters have become less about grief and more about an argument with Seneca, whose Consolation to Helvia she read at the therapist's suggestion and found both profound and enraging. The story unfolds entirely through these unsent letters, which begin addressed to her dead wife and gradually shift addressee -- to Seneca, to her teenage son, to herself, to no one. The question at the center: can you think your way through grief, or does the attempt to philosophize suffering betray the person you lost?

## Source Inspiration
Maria Popova's article on Seneca's Consolation to Helvia highlights a structural paradox: the exile himself becomes consoler to the grieving mother. Seneca wrote from Corsica after losing everything -- and yet chose to comfort his mother, who had endured the death of her own mother in childbirth, her husband, her uncle, three grandchildren, and then her grandson dying in her arms followed twenty days later by her son's exile. The article frames grief as a "learnable skill" per Stoic philosophy, which is either profoundly liberating or profoundly insulting depending on where you stand.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Marilynne Robinson (style)**: The long, meditative sentences that circle around spiritual questions without resolving them. Her prose treats grief as a form of attention rather than a problem to solve.
- **Rachel Cusk (style)**: The austere, essayistic mode where observation becomes narrative. Her stripped-down approach to emotional devastation, the refusal of consolation even as consolation is the subject.
- **A Grief Observed (structure)**: Lewis's raw, journal-form grief writing that begins as theology and disintegrates into something more honest. The epistolary structure, the way the addressee shifts as the writer's understanding shifts.
- **Meditations (themes)**: The Stoic practice of writing to yourself as a way of constructing the self that can endure. The tension between philosophical discipline and emotional truth.

## Notes
The epistolary format is the story's engine and its risk. The letters must deteriorate in craft as they increase in honesty. Early letters should be polished, intellectually controlled. Late letters should be fragments. The philosophy professor's training should be both her armor and the thing she must eventually drop. The son should appear only through the letters -- never directly -- which makes him more vivid, not less.
