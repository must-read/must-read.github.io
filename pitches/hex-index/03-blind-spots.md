## Premise
In a near-future American suburb, automated license-plate-reading cameras mounted on every other telephone pole have made crime essentially impossible and privacy essentially extinct. A woman who installs and maintains these cameras for a private surveillance company begins to notice that someone is systematically destroying them -- not randomly, but in patterns that create "blind corridors" through the city, paths where a person could move unseen. She is tasked with finding the vandals. What she finds instead is a community of people who need to be invisible for reasons that are not criminal but are, under the surveillance regime, indistinguishable from crime: undocumented families, domestic violence survivors, teenagers, lovers.

## Source Inspiration
Brian Merchant's article about people across five US states dismantling Flock surveillance cameras. The destruction appears "staged to leave a message" and follows failed civic resistance through normal channels. The cameras conduct warrantless vehicle surveillance and share data with ICE. The article describes a nationwide pattern of direct action against surveillance infrastructure -- cameras smashed, components removed, solar panels destroyed.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **William Gibson (style)**: The texture of near-future technology embedded in everyday life, brand-name realism, the way corporate infrastructure becomes landscape. His prose makes surveillance feel like weather.
- **Carmen Maria Machado (style)**: The body under threat, the way systems of control are experienced physically, the feminist register that recognizes surveillance as gendered. Her ability to find the uncanny in the domestic.
- **The Circle (structure)**: A protagonist embedded within the surveillance system who gradually recognizes its totalitarian implications. The corporate campus as dystopia. The structural irony of someone who builds the panopticon discovering what it costs.
- **We (themes)**: The oldest dystopia-of-transparency novel -- the idea that total visibility is total control, and that the desire for a private life is inherently revolutionary.

## Notes
The protagonist should not become a hero. She should be compromised throughout -- she needs the job, she believes in the product, she is genuinely good at finding the blind spots. The story's tension comes from her recognizing that her competence is the problem. Consider the "protagonist is wrong" risk card.
