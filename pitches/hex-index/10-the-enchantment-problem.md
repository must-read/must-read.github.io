## Premise
A couple in their late thirties, together twelve years, attend a weekend philosophy retreat on the Greek island of Hydra. The retreat's theme is "the epistemology of love" -- how we know what we feel. Over three days, as lecturers discuss Nussbaum's "incompleteness theorem of the heart" and Auden's theory of enchantment, the couple begins to suspect -- separately, silently -- that what they have is not love but a sustained mutual agreement to behave as though it is. Neither raises this with the other. The story is told in precise, clipped prose that mirrors the philosophical framework: every observation is also a question, every tenderness is also evidence.

## Source Inspiration
Maria Popova's article on love draws on Martha Nussbaum's exploration of a fundamental paradox: we simultaneously deceive ourselves about love while possessing the capacity to recognize and correct those deceptions. Nussbaum describes this as an "incompleteness theorem of the heart's truth." The article identifies "alternations between love and its denial, suffering and denial of suffering" as "the most essential and ubiquitous structural feature of the human heart." Auden's concept of enchantment -- a state of certainty that bypasses doubt -- provides the counterpoint.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Rachel Cusk (style)**: The austere observation-as-narrative mode, where what characters notice reveals more than what they say. Her stripped-down dialogue frames that turn conversation into philosophical inquiry.
- **Milan Kundera (style)**: The essayistic interruptions that philosophize about what the characters are feeling, the way he treats relationships as thought experiments, the ironic distance that is itself a form of tenderness.
- **Normal People (structure)**: Two people orbiting each other across time, the way Rooney tracks the micro-adjustments of power and vulnerability within a relationship through precise physical and conversational detail.
- **The Unbearable Lightness of Being (themes)**: The weight/lightness paradox applied to love -- is a love that could be otherwise any less real? The idea that examining love too closely is itself a betrayal of the enchantment that makes love possible.

## Notes
The island setting is a container, not a metaphor. The philosophy retreat should be rendered with mild comedy -- the other attendees, the bad wine, the lecturer who cries. The couple's crisis should remain entirely interior: no fight, no revelation, no affair. The story ends with them on the ferry back to Athens, and the reader does not know whether they have resolved anything or simply resumed the agreement. The minimalist register is essential -- this should feel like a Cusk novel compressed into 4,000 words.
