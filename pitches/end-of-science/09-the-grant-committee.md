## Premise
A federal grant review committee convenes to evaluate proposals for fundamental research funding. Over three days of deliberation, the committee -- composed of scientists who once did real research and administrators who speak fluent venture-capital -- must decide which questions deserve public money. The proposals range from a fifteen-year study of deep-ocean microbial communities to a two-year sprint toward a patentable nanomaterial. The committee chair, a former particle physicist now in policy, watches the deliberation reveal everything the essay describes: questions promising products win over questions promising understanding, "timelines and markets" language colonizes the evaluation criteria, and the most important proposal -- one with no foreseeable application -- is tabled for "further review," which everyone knows means death. The story is a dark comedy about the bureaucratic machinery that determines what we are allowed to be curious about.

## Source Inspiration
Section 10 ("Economy") describes how "grant committees adopting venture-capital language demanding timelines and markets distort fundamental inquiry," and how the monopoly economy "prioritizes questions promising products while deferring questions promising understanding without revenue paths." The essay distinguishes "scientific risk (hypothesis willing to fail)" from "financial risk (narrative sellable to shareholders)," arguing that institutional structures narrow ambition while appearing to expand it.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **George Saunders (style)**: Saunders's pitch-perfect institutional satire -- the way organizations generate their own internally coherent language that is, from outside, insane. The grant committee's vocabulary ("impact pathways," "stakeholder alignment," "translational readiness") should be both funny and horrifying.
- **Viet Thanh Nguyen (style)**: Nguyen's ability to render institutional power as a form of violence that operates through polite procedures. The committee is not evil; it is following its logic. The violence is structural, not personal, and Nguyen's prose captures how that feels from inside.
- **CivilWarLand in Bad Decline (structure)**: Saunders's institutional settings where the rules make perfect sense internally while producing monstrous outcomes. The grant committee as a theme park of misaligned incentives, narrated by someone who can see the absurdity but cannot stop participating.
- **Pictures from an Institution (themes)**: Jarrell's satirical portrait of academic life as a self-sustaining ecosystem of vanity, compromise, and occasional genuine devotion. The committee members are real people with real scientific pasts, and the satire should be compassionate enough to show what they've lost.

## Notes
The committee chair should be the conscience of the story -- someone who understands what is happening, participated in creating the system, and cannot dismantle it from inside. The funniest and most devastating scenes should be the moments when venture-capital language is applied to fundamental curiosity: "What is the five-year commercialization pathway for understanding why the universe exists?" The tabled proposal should be specific and beautiful -- something genuinely worth knowing that no market will fund.
