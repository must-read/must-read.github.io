## Premise
Ila Mendes has maintained handwritten calibration notebooks for twenty-three years in a materials lab. Ink on paper, every mistake mapped in the margins, corrections that are themselves a record of how instruments drift. She has trained a new generation of lab engineers, and now the facility is automating. A corporate partner wants to replace her notebooks with a logging system that is faster, cheaper, and incapable of remembering why a particular sensor was suspect last February. The story follows one week as Ila is asked to transfer her knowledge into a digital system -- and discovers that the things she knows cannot be transferred, because they are not information but judgment. The story is about what gets lost when a culture of caution is replaced by a culture of efficiency.

## Source Inspiration
Section 12 ("The Hands") describes Ila Mendes, a lab engineer who maintains worn calibration notebooks by hand because "ink-to-paper memory works better than automatic logging," mapping mistakes in margins as preventative reminders. The essay frames her as embodying how "real science looks as accumulated caution rather than breakthrough narratives." The broader essay argues that invisible infrastructure -- calibration, replication, failure-documentation -- is the real substrate of science, but it is invisible to markets demanding grand reveals.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Kazuo Ishiguro (style)**: Ishiguro's restrained narration of people who have devoted their lives to institutional service, and who only late discover the gap between their dignity and their replaceability. Ila should have Stevens's quiet pride and his devastating blindness about whether his precision mattered to anyone but himself.
- **Richard Powers (style)**: Powers's ability to render technical work as deeply human -- the beauty of process, the way a person's entire intellectual life can be embedded in a practice. His prose gives scientific work its full emotional and philosophical weight.
- **The Remains of the Day (structure)**: A retrospective narration covering a compressed time period in which the protagonist slowly confronts whether a lifetime of careful, invisible work amounted to anything. The journey structure (here, the week of knowledge transfer) forces the reckoning.
- **Galatea 2.2 (themes)**: The attempt to transfer human understanding into a machine, and the discovery of what resists transfer. The gap between what can be encoded and what must be lived. The melancholy of teaching something that will outlast you but cannot inherit your judgment.

## Notes
The danger is sentimentality about handcraft versus technology. Ila should not be a Luddite -- she should understand why automation makes sense. The devastation comes from the fact that she agrees with the logic but knows something the logic cannot capture. Strong candidate for a quiet, unresolved ending: the digital system works fine. Her notebooks go into storage. Nothing breaks. The loss is invisible, which is exactly the point.
