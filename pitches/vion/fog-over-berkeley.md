## Premise
A widowed law professor cannot finish an essay about the Fourth Amendment's inadequacy in the age of algorithmic governance. Every time she reaches for the legal argument, the essay turns into a letter to her dead wife. The fog that rolls in over Berkeley each evening becomes something more than weather -- it becomes the medium through which the past communicates, blurring the boundary between constitutional theory and grief until the professor can no longer distinguish between the inadequacy of the law and the inadequacy of language to hold what she has lost.

## Source Inspiration
Ruth Abramson's unfinished op-ed in Book 2 -- her inability to complete an argument about Fourth Amendment doctrine while grieving Susan (dead 14 months in Book 2, six years in Book 3). The Berkeley fog that "obscures while supposedly clarifying" -- visibility that doesn't translate into understanding. The legal argument about precedent from Olmstead through Katz to Carpenter failing to address "systems that operated through prediction rather than surveillance, inference rather than intrusion."

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Toni Morrison)**: Morrison's incantatory, lyrical prose -- the way she writes grief as a physical presence that occupies rooms, changes the quality of light, alters the temperature of a sentence. Susan's absence should haunt the essay the way Beloved haunts 124 Bluestone Road: not as ghost but as gravity.
- **AuthorB (Albert Camus)**: Camus's lucid examination of the absurd -- the clarity with which he confronts meaninglessness. Ruth's legal mind demands Camus-level precision even as the emotional content exceeds what precision can hold. The tension between Morrison's lyricism and Camus's clarity IS the story's engine.
- **WorkX (Grief Is the Thing with Feathers)**: The structural DNA of grief rendered as genre-bending form -- Porter's mixture of prose, poetry, and surrealist visitation. Ruth's essay should keep slipping form, becoming letter, becoming legal brief, becoming something else.
- **WorkY (Kindred)**: The thematic DNA of the past reaching physically into the present -- Butler's premise of being literally pulled backward in time. Ruth's grief and her legal argument converge: both are about the past's inadequacy to govern the present, and the present's inability to escape the past.

## Notes
The slipstream subgenre captures the liminal quality -- the fog is both literal Berkeley weather and a genre-boundary dissolving. The story should never confirm whether the supernatural element is real or metaphorical. The "genre subversion" risk card fits: the reader begins in legal realism and ends somewhere between ghost story and philosophical treatise, without being able to identify the moment the genre changed.
