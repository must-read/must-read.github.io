## Premise
A nurse practitioner at a community health clinic in the desert Southwest treats fifty patients before noon -- rationed insulin, work injuries no one can afford to heal, a teenager having panic attacks because he understands the news. She medicates her own despair with double shifts. Over one twelve-hour shift, the story accumulates bodies as evidence of systems no clinic can cure, until a patient asks her a question she cannot answer: "Is this going to get better?"

## Source Inspiration
Elena Varga in Book 2 -- the Phoenix nurse practitioner who works "downstream of the poison," treating bodies damaged by economic structures beyond her clinical scope. Specific patients: Mrs. Delgado rationing insulin, Ray Torres working on a fractured radius, Jaylen Mitchell (16) experiencing panic attacks from recognizing institutional dysfunction. The critical essay calls her patient log a "calendar of loss."

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Denis Johnson)**: Johnson's raw, hallucinatory, fractured prose captures the dreamlike exhaustion of a twelve-hour shift where bodies blur together. His spiritual yearning amid degradation matches Elena's despair-and-compassion duality.
- **AuthorB (Marilynne Robinson)**: Robinson's radiant attention to ordinary life provides the counterweight -- the moments of grace between patients, the theology of showing up. Her meditative cadence slows the pace enough to feel each person.
- **WorkX (Jesus' Son)**: The structure of vignettes that accumulate into something devastating. Each patient encounter is a self-contained moment, but together they form an argument about systemic failure that no single encounter can make.
- **WorkY (Cutting for Stone)**: The thematic DNA of medicine as both sacred practice and inadequate response to suffering. The clinic as a place where the body is the text and the diagnosis is always also political.

## Notes
The minimalist-fiction subgenre fits because the prose should be stripped down -- clinical, precise, exhausted -- with emotional depths hidden beneath surface-level medical routine. The "constraint of space" risk card would work beautifully: the entire story takes place in the clinic, with the world only visible through what walks through the door.
