## Premise
When a woman dies after an algorithmic healthcare triage system deprioritizes her case, her death becomes a convergence point: the employment algorithm that flagged her as "low economic viability," the insurance model that raised her premiums, the scheduling system that pushed her appointment three weeks out. No single system killed her. The story follows five people who each operated one piece of the machine, none of whom feel responsible, all of whom are correct that they personally did nothing wrong.

## Source Inspiration
Halima Hassan's death in the trilogy, described in the critical essay as the narrative convergence point where "employment algorithms and healthcare systems combine as components of a single integrated apparatus." Elena Varga's MedAssist system that triages patients by economic viability rather than medical need -- healthcare as "sorting mechanism" and "calendar of loss." The concept of "distributed mendacity" -- harm through the aggregate of reasonable decisions.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Octavia Butler)**: Butler's visceral, unflinching prose about power dynamics and survival. Her gift for making systemic violence feel personal and embodied -- not abstract, not theoretical, but happening to a specific body. Halima's death should land like a Butler death: inevitable, structural, and enraging.
- **AuthorB (Jose Saramago)**: Saramago's flowing, unpunctuated prose and allegorical vision. His ability to take a single premise (everyone goes blind, everyone dies at the same time) and follow it to its social conclusions. The algorithmic sorting system is his kind of premise: what happens when triage becomes automated?
- **WorkX (Blindness)**: The structural DNA of a catastrophe that reveals social structures rather than creating them. Saramago's plague didn't make people cruel -- it removed the architecture that hid cruelty. The algorithm doesn't create healthcare inequality; it systematizes and accelerates it.
- **WorkY (Automating Inequality)**: The thematic DNA of how digital systems encode and amplify existing social hierarchies. Eubanks's argument that automated decision-making is not neutral but a continuation of historical practices of sorting poor people into categories of deserving and undeserving.

## Notes
The five-perspective structure is ambitious for short fiction but could work if each section is tight (600-800 words) and focused on a single moment of decision. The "multiple voices" risk card is almost mandatory here. No perspective should be villainous -- the horror is that everyone is reasonable, everyone followed protocol, and someone is dead.
