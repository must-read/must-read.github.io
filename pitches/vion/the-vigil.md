## Premise
Three years after a technological crisis that everyone agrees happened but no one can precisely define, a documentary filmmaker, a retired journalist, and a law professor each try to create a definitive record of what occurred. They discover that the event has already been absorbed into entertainment, conspiracy theory, and meme -- accurate information exists everywhere and means nothing. The story asks whether "witnessing and remembering" can constitute resistance when the systems being resisted have already metabolized the witness.

## Source Inspiration
The entire arc of Book 3 -- Delphine the filmmaker, Jerome the journalist, and Ruth the professor, all three years post-crisis, all questioning whether their forms of documentation (film, investigative journalism, legal opinion) accomplished anything. The critical essay's concept of "vigil" as the only remaining form of resistance, and the post-truth epistemology argument that accurate information gets "metabolized as entertainment, conspiracy theory, or meme."

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Margaret Atwood)**: Atwood's speculative precision -- her ability to extrapolate current trends to their logical endpoints while maintaining wry, ironic narration. The crisis should feel like an Atwood dystopia: not dramatic, not cinematic, just the slow accumulation of what we already tolerate.
- **AuthorB (Olga Tokarczuk)**: Tokarczuk's fragmentary, constellation-novel approach -- her wandering, digressive narratives that connect disparate lives through philosophical resonance rather than plot. The three protagonists don't need to meet; they need to rhyme.
- **WorkX (Station Eleven)**: The structural DNA of multiple timelines converging around a civilization-altering event, where the event itself matters less than how people reconstruct meaning afterward. Mandel's focus on art and memory as survival tools maps onto the documentary, the journalism, the legal framework.
- **WorkY (Amusing Ourselves to Death)**: The thematic DNA of Postman's argument that information overload doesn't produce ignorance but a kind of passive, entertained awareness where everything is known and nothing is acted upon. The crisis was documented, broadcast, discussed, and absorbed without changing the underlying system.

## Notes
This is the most philosophical pitch and needs the strongest characters to avoid becoming an essay. Each protagonist should want something concrete and personal (Delphine wants to finish a film, Jerome wants his son to understand, Ruth wants to write one honest sentence) that the philosophical question keeps blocking. The "non-linear time" risk card fits naturally -- the three-year gap allows the story to move between crisis and aftermath without chronological obligation.
