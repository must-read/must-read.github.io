## Premise
A former labor economist, now working as a community organizer in a mid-sized American city in the near future, has eighteen months -- according to her private calculations -- before the window of effective intervention closes permanently. After that, the structural pressures (wage stagnation at fifty-year lows, credential inflation producing a surplus of frustrated professionals, state budgets in irreversible decline) will have crossed the threshold where reforms are only 5-10% effective instead of 40-50%. She is not trying to save the country. She is trying to save her city -- building local institutions (a credit union, a cooperative grocery, a skills-exchange network) that might survive the national disintegration she believes is coming. The story follows her through the final weeks of the window as she confronts the paradox the policy essay identifies: "the same structural conditions that make reform necessary also empower those who resist it."

## Source Inspiration
The policy essay's central finding that "interventions are most effective when applied early in the secular cycle" and that beyond 60-70% of peak crisis "normal political processes become impossible" creates a ticking-clock narrative. The essay's analysis of why the New Deal succeeded (it arrived before terminal crisis) and why the Gracchi failed (they arrived too late) provides the historical framework. The forecasting essay's claim that decision-makers have "only years, not decades" to implement reforms makes the urgency visceral.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Octavia Butler (style)**: Butler's visceral, unflinching prose about power dynamics and survival in extreme conditions is essential for a story about building community resilience in a collapsing system. Her characters are practical, unsentimental, and driven by a desperate clarity about what is actually happening.
- **Margaret Atwood (style)**: Atwood's precise, sardonic prose and her ability to render dystopia through accumulation of bureaucratic detail and social observation provides the register for documenting how collapse looks not like a Hollywood disaster but like a series of small, administrative failures.
- **The Handmaid's Tale by Margaret Atwood (structure)**: The structure of testimony -- a voice speaking from inside a system that is failing, documenting the progression in real time -- provides the narrative frame. The story is told as if being recorded for someone who will need to understand what happened and when.
- **Parable of the Sower by Octavia Butler (themes)**: The theme of building a new community inside the ruins of a collapsing one -- not waiting for rescue but creating resilience through deliberate, practical organization. Lauren Olamina's hyperempathy and relentless pragmatism mirror the protagonist's dual nature: she feels the collapse viscerally but acts with cold precision.

## Notes
The story should not resolve the question of whether the protagonist succeeds. The window closes during the narrative -- that is structural and inevitable -- but whether her local institutions survive the national crisis is left to implication. The most important scene might be the one where she stops trying to convince people the crisis is coming and starts simply building as if it has already arrived. The shift from persuasion to construction is the emotional pivot.
