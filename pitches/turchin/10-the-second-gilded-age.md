## Premise
A woman in her late forties -- a professor of economic history at a mid-tier state university whose department is being cut -- attends a three-day academic conference on inequality in a luxury hotel in Scottsdale, Arizona. The conference is funded by a foundation endowed by a tech billionaire. The irony is not lost on anyone; it is simply not mentioned. Through a series of conversations -- with a graduate student who cannot afford health insurance, with a retired union organizer serving as a respondent on a panel, with a hotel housekeeper who is also taking community college classes, with the billionaire's program officer who genuinely believes in the foundation's mission -- the professor assembles a portrait of what the essays call "the Second Gilded Age" at the level of individual encounters. She never uses that phrase. She does not theorize. She listens, and what she hears is the sound of a system explaining itself through the voices of the people it contains.

## Source Inspiration
The America essay describes the current period as "a Second Gilded Age paralleling the late 1800s robber baron era," characterized by wealth concentration returning to Gilded Age extremes, stagnating real wages since 1973, and the paradox that "personality-driven explanations miss the root problem -- structural conditions preceded Trump and will outlast any individual leader." The policy essay notes that "the same structural conditions that make reform necessary also empower those who resist it." The conference setting embodies this contradiction: the system produces the critique of itself and then funds it.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Rachel Cusk (style)**: Cusk's austere, essayistic approach -- observation as narrative, stripped-down dialogue frames where other people's stories become the architecture -- is the ideal mode for a story told primarily through overheard and elicited conversations. Her ability to make listening an active, almost aggressive form of narration.
- **Joan Didion (style)**: Didion's precise, sardonic observation of American dysfunction, her ability to find the telling detail that reveals structural failure, and her willingness to implicate herself in the systems she critiques provide the essayistic register.
- **Slouching Towards Bethlehem by Joan Didion (structure)**: The structure of a journalist moving through a specific American landscape at a specific historical moment, assembling meaning from encounters and observations rather than arguing a thesis. The essay as portrait, not polemic.
- **Outline by Rachel Cusk (themes)**: The theme of a protagonist who disappears into other people's narratives, whose own story is told entirely through what she draws out of others. The professor's crisis (her department is being cut) is present but never foregrounded; it is the lens through which she sees, not the subject of what she sees.

## Notes
The story should be structured around the three days of the conference, with each day organized around a key conversation. The professor's own precarity should emerge only through implication -- a mention of subletting her house, a reference to a job application, a moment when she calculates the cost of her hotel room in hours of adjunct teaching. The billionaire's program officer should be the most sympathetic character: someone who understands the structural critique perfectly and has chosen to work within the system anyway, not out of cynicism but out of a pragmatism that the professor finds both admirable and unbearable.
