## Premise
In a far-future galactic civilization, the Seshat Institute maintains a database of every known sentient society across twelve thousand worlds -- their rise, their peak complexity, their decline, their collapse or transformation. A junior indexer is assigned to code a newly discovered dead civilization on a remote planet, reducing its archaeological remnants to 1,494 standardized variables: administrative hierarchies, writing systems, military technology, religious institutions. As she works through the coding, she realizes this civilization does not fit any known pattern. Its complexity metrics suggest it should have survived; its collapse indicators suggest it should have declined gradually. Instead, it vanished completely and instantly, leaving behind structures that suggest it was trying to build its own Seshat -- a database of itself, a civilization attempting to predict and prevent its own collapse by measuring itself against all others.

## Source Inspiration
The Seshat Databank essay describes how civilizations are reduced to quantifiable variables for comparison -- and raises the question of what is lost in that translation. The essay notes that "complexity develops as an interconnected package" and that "missing data" constitutes its own kind of mystery. The idea of a civilization that "actively resisted standardized measurement" or one that "deliberately erased its records" is directly suggested as fiction-worthy. The recursive concept -- a civilization building a database to prevent its own collapse, mirroring the real Seshat project -- creates a strange loop.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Stanislaw Lem (style)**: Lem's cerebral, satirical, deeply philosophical prose -- particularly his fascination with the limits of human comprehension facing the genuinely alien -- is perfect for a story about the impossibility of fully encoding a civilization. His dry wit and intellectual rigor prevent the concept from becoming merely whimsical.
- **Italo Calvino (style)**: Calvino's precise, crystalline prose and his ability to create worlds through pure description -- cities that exist as ideas, architectures of the imagination -- provide the register for describing an alien civilization through its database entries. His combinatorial playfulness matches the story's structural premise.
- **Invisible Cities by Italo Calvino (structure)**: The structure of a catalog of places, each described as a discrete entry with its own logic and beauty, mirrors the indexer's work of coding a civilization variable by variable. Each variable becomes a window into a different aspect of the vanished society.
- **Solaris by Stanislaw Lem (themes)**: The theme of encountering something genuinely beyond comprehension -- not merely unknown but unknowable through existing frameworks. The Seshat database, like Solaristics, is a system of knowledge that fails precisely at the point where it matters most. The civilization that does not fit the pattern is the ocean that refuses to be understood.

## Notes
The story should avoid revealing what actually happened to the vanished civilization. The mystery is not solvable -- that is the point. The indexer's growing frustration as she tries to make the data fit known patterns should mirror the reader's desire for resolution. The final entries she codes -- the ones about the civilization's own database project -- should create an uncanny mirror effect without explaining it. Some things resist quantification, and the attempt to quantify them is itself a kind of hubris the story should embody without naming.
