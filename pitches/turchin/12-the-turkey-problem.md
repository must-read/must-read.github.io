## Premise
A data analyst at a prosperous, well-run small town's municipal government has been tracking the town's metrics for twelve years: crime rates, property values, school performance, civic participation, median income, satisfaction surveys. Every indicator shows steady, unbroken improvement. The town is, by every measurable standard, thriving. She presents her annual report to the town council and receives warm applause. That night, walking home, she notices something she has never been able to quantify but has always felt: the town is too good. Not good in the way that functional communities are good -- messy, imperfect, held together by compromise -- but good in the way that a system optimized for a specific output is good. She begins to suspect that the twelve years of data she has been tracking are the thousand days before Thanksgiving, and she is the turkey.

## Source Inspiration
The Monte Carlo essay introduces "The Turkey Problem" -- how one thousand days of positive data cannot predict a structural break, a regime change that makes all historical patterns irrelevant. The essay uses this as a caution about the limits of forecasting, but the concept works as pure horror: a system that produces consistent positive results right up until the moment it doesn't, and the impossibility of distinguishing genuine stability from the lead-up to catastrophe using only historical data.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Shirley Jackson (style)**: Jackson's ability to render the ordinary as menacing -- her precise, domestic prose that accumulates unease through normalcy rather than deviation -- is the essential register for a story where the horror is that everything is going well. Her matter-of-fact tone amid gathering dread.
- **Carmen Maria Machado (style)**: Machado's surreal, body-aware horror and her facility with narratives that make the familiar grotesque through slight distortion provide the mechanism for the story's escalation. Her ability to locate horror in systems and structures rather than individual monsters.
- **The Lottery by Shirley Jackson (structure)**: The structure of a community ritual that appears benign until its true function is revealed -- the slow revelation that the system everyone participates in has a purpose no one examines. The annual report is the annual lottery.
- **Her Body and Other Parties by Carmen Maria Machado (themes)**: The themes of quantification as violence, of reducing complex human experience to data points, and of the body (or body politic) as a site where systemic forces become visceral. The analyst's body knows something her data does not.

## Notes
The horror should remain ambiguous and structural rather than supernatural. The story should never reveal what is "wrong" with the town, because the point is that the data cannot tell her -- and the data is all she has. The most frightening possibility is the most mundane: that the town's prosperity is real but fragile, built on conditions that could reverse at any moment, and her twelve years of positive metrics are genuinely meaningless as predictors of the future. The turkey does not die because of a conspiracy. It dies because the pattern it observed was never the pattern that mattered.
