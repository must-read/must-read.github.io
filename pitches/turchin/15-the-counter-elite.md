## Premise
A brilliant young political theorist at an elite university -- the kind of person the cliodynamics essays would classify as a "counter-elite," someone with the education and ambition for power but no path to it -- gathers a small circle of graduate students around an increasingly radical reading of structural-demographic theory. Their seminar begins as intellectual exercise: what if you could identify the exact structural conditions under which revolution becomes mathematically inevitable? It becomes ideology: if the equations say the system must break, then helping it break is not destruction but acceleration of the necessary. It becomes something darker when one member of the circle, the quietest, begins applying the theory not to abstract civilizations but to their own university -- mapping its elite overproduction, its adjunct immiseration, its fiscal crisis -- and proposes that the campus itself is a miniature polity ripe for controlled destabilization.

## Source Inspiration
The essays' central concept that "frustrated elites provide leadership, resources, and organization for revolutionary movements" -- that "discontented masses without elite leadership produce unfocused riots; the same masses led by excluded lawyers and intellectuals become revolutionary forces" -- provides the social dynamic. The forecasting essay's "reflexivity problem" (predictions that influence what they predict) gives the intellectual framework. The policy essay's observation that "structural problems require structural solutions" is the argument the theorist twists into justification for action.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Fyodor Dostoevsky (style)**: Dostoevsky's feverish, philosophically dense prose -- his ability to render ideological possession as a psychological and spiritual crisis -- is essential for characters who have reasoned themselves into dangerous territory. His dialogue, where ideas are weapons and conversations are battlefields, drives the seminar scenes.
- **Donna Tartt (style)**: Tartt's lush, seductive prose and her ability to render intellectual life as sensually compelling -- making ideas feel glamorous and dangerous simultaneously -- provides the atmosphere of an elite academic circle that mistakes aesthetic refinement for moral clarity.
- **The Secret History by Donna Tartt (structure)**: The structure of a retrospective narration by a participant in a small, closed intellectual group whose shared ideas lead to violence -- the movement from scholarly fascination to moral catastrophe. The narrator's dual position as insider and eventual dissenter.
- **Demons by Fyodor Dostoevsky (themes)**: The themes of intellectual radicalism curdling into nihilism, of brilliant people who convince themselves that ideas justify destruction, and of the specific danger posed by educated, frustrated young people who have been given the tools of analysis but denied the positions of power those tools were supposed to unlock.

## Notes
The story should take the intellectual content seriously -- the structural-demographic theory is genuinely compelling, and the circle's analysis of their university's political economy should be largely correct. The horror is not that they are wrong about the diagnosis but that they make the catastrophic leap from description to prescription, from "the system is failing" to "therefore we should help it fail." The quietest member's campus analysis should be the most chilling section: precise, well-sourced, and entirely plausible, presented in the same dispassionate tone as a peer-reviewed paper. The story should end before anything happens -- the plan is proposed, the group's reaction is documented, and the narrator walks out into the night unsure whether she will report what she has heard.
