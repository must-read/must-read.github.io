## Premise
Six people who graduated from the same elite law school in the same year, fifteen years later. One is a senator's chief of staff. One runs a legal aid clinic making $58,000. One is a mid-level associate who has been passed over for partner three times. One left law entirely to become a high school teacher. One is a political podcaster with 2 million followers and mounting rage. One is dead -- suicide at thirty-two, student debt at $340,000. The story moves between their perspectives in non-chronological fragments, assembling a mosaic of what "elite overproduction" looks like from inside the individuals it produces: not a demographic trend but a lived experience of being told you are exceptional and then discovering there is no room for you.

## Source Inspiration
The essays repeatedly identify "elite overproduction" as the most dangerous driver of societal instability -- when aspirants seeking power exceed available positions, the frustrated become counter-elites who destabilize the system. The introduction notes that lawyers and PhDs per capita have tripled relative to available positions since 1960. The insight that frustrated elites provide "leadership, resources, and organization" for revolutionary movements -- that mass discontent without elite direction produces riots, but mass discontent with elite direction produces revolutions -- gives this demographic abstraction a human face.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **George Saunders (style)**: Saunders's radical empathy and absurdist vernacular voices in surreal situations are ideal for characters trapped in systems that promise meaning and deliver absurdity. His ability to find genuine pathos inside corporate/institutional satire matches the story's need to take these people's suffering seriously even when the system that produced them is grotesque.
- **Jennifer Egan (style)**: Egan's facility with time-jumping, multi-perspective narrative and her interest in how people's trajectories diverge over decades provides the structural and tonal model. Her prose balances cool observation with emotional precision.
- **A Visit from the Goon Squad by Jennifer Egan (structure)**: The interconnected-stories-across-time structure, where characters appear as protagonists in one chapter and minor figures in another, mirrors how the same graduating class can produce wildly divergent outcomes from identical starting positions.
- **Tenth of December by George Saunders (themes)**: The themes of institutional capture, economic desperation dressed up as personal failure, and the radical act of empathy within dehumanizing systems. Saunders's characters are always negotiating the gap between what they were promised and what they received.

## Notes
The podcaster should be the most dangerous character -- not because she is evil but because she is brilliant, articulate, and genuinely wronged, and has discovered that her elite education prepared her perfectly to channel mass frustration into political force. She is a counter-elite in real time, and the story should make the reader understand why without endorsing it. The dead classmate should appear only through others' memories and a single document -- perhaps the draft of an unsent email.
