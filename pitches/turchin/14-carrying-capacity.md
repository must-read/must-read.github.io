## Premise
A retired ecologist living on a small farm in the Ozarks has spent forty years studying carrying capacity -- the maximum population a given environment can sustain. She has watched the concept migrate from ecology into economics and political science, where it has been applied to human civilizations with what she considers dangerous imprecision. Now, as drought depletes her own farm's aquifer and the rural county around her experiences its own quiet version of structural decline (the school closing, the hospital reducing to a clinic, the young leaving), she writes a series of reflections on what carrying capacity actually means when you live inside it -- not as a variable in a differential equation but as the well that determines whether your garden survives August.

## Source Inspiration
The introduction essay describes carrying capacity as fundamental to structural-demographic theory: "the maximum population sustainable with available technology and resources." The mathematics essay models it as a parameter in logistic growth equations. But the essays also note that societies can "expand carrying capacity through innovation, buying time but potentially overshooting catastrophically." The ecologist's perspective inverts the abstraction: she has spent her career measuring carrying capacity in deer populations, prairie ecosystems, and watershed systems, and she knows that the mathematical elegance conceals a messy, granular reality.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Annie Dillard (style)**: Dillard's fierce, ecstatic attention to the natural world -- her ability to render ecological processes as simultaneously beautiful and violent -- provides the prose register for a narrator who sees carrying capacity not as a number but as a landscape phenomenon she can read in the height of grass, the behavior of birds, the clarity of creek water.
- **Robin Wall Kimmerer (style)**: Kimmerer's integration of scientific knowledge with indigenous ecological understanding -- her ability to hold both ways of knowing without reducing either -- provides the philosophical framework for a narrator who questions whether Western mathematical models capture what they claim to capture.
- **Braiding Sweetgrass by Robin Wall Kimmerer (structure)**: The structure of interconnected essays that move between scientific observation, personal narrative, and philosophical reflection, braiding multiple ways of knowing into a unified understanding. Each reflection is a self-contained meditation that contributes to a larger argument.
- **Pilgrim at Tinker Creek by Annie Dillard (themes)**: The theme of a solitary observer in a specific landscape, paying such intense attention to natural processes that they become transparent to larger truths. The farm as Tinker Creek: a specific place that contains the universal.

## Notes
The piece should avoid polemic. The ecologist's critique of cliodynamics should be respectful -- she understands why political scientists borrowed her discipline's concepts -- but she should insist that carrying capacity in a human context is not a fixed number to be estimated but a relationship to be maintained. The farm itself should be the primary text: what is actually happening to the water table, the soil, the insect populations, the migrating birds. The county's decline should be documented through the same attentive observation she brings to ecology, suggesting that human communities and ecosystems fail in similar ways but for reasons that differential equations can describe without explaining.
