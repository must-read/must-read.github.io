## Premise
On a world where the dominant civilization has survived for eleven thousand years -- not through stability but through conscious, managed cycling -- a historian approaches the most dangerous moment in each 200-year secular cycle: the planned crisis. Her civilization long ago discovered the mathematics of structural-demographic theory and concluded that secular cycles cannot be prevented, only managed. So every two centuries, they engineer a controlled disintegrative phase: deliberate elite pruning, managed wage readjustment, orchestrated institutional renewal. It is brutal, it is anticipated, and it works -- it has kept their civilization alive for fifty-five cycles while every other society on their world has collapsed and been forgotten. Now, in her generation's crisis, something unprecedented is happening: the population is refusing to participate. They have decided, for the first time in eleven millennia, that the cost of surviving is too high.

## Source Inspiration
The mathematics essay describes how "the mathematics of collapse is also the mathematics of renewal" -- the same equations predict both crisis and recovery. The policy essay notes that successful interventions require arriving "before terminal crisis" and that timing is everything. This story asks: what if a civilization took the theory to its logical conclusion and made crisis itself a managed institution? The Rome essay's observation that Rome "reinvented itself multiple times across a thousand-year span" -- from Republic to Principate to Dominate -- suggests that civilizational survival requires periodic, painful transformation.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Ursula K. Le Guin (style)**: Le Guin's anthropological worldbuilding, her ability to render alien social systems as fully realized and internally coherent, is essential for a civilization that has institutionalized its own crises. Her philosophical depth and Taoist-inflected narrative restraint suit a story about acceptance and resistance of cyclical necessity.
- **Kim Stanley Robinson (style)**: Robinson's detailed, process-oriented prose about how societies actually function -- the infrastructure, the logistics, the committee meetings where history happens -- provides the texture for depicting a managed crisis. His ability to make institutional process dramatic.
- **The Left Hand of Darkness by Ursula K. Le Guin (structure)**: The structure of an outsider encountering a society with fundamentally different assumptions about what is natural and necessary. The historian is an insider, but her perspective shifts as she watches her society question its deepest assumption for the first time.
- **The Years of Rice and Salt by Kim Stanley Robinson (themes)**: The theme of civilizational persistence across deep time -- how cultures survive not by staying the same but by transforming repeatedly. The novel's reincarnation framework mirrors the secular cycle's pattern of death and renewal, and the question of whether endless recurrence constitutes survival or merely repetition.

## Notes
The central tension is between those who see managed crisis as the price of survival and those who see it as a form of perpetual violence sanctified by mathematics. The historian should begin as a true believer -- someone who has studied all fifty-four previous cycles and understands in her bones why they are necessary -- and should end somewhere more complicated. The population's refusal should not be presented as ignorant or irrational; they may be right that a civilization that requires periodic brutality to survive is not worth surviving. The story should not resolve this question.
