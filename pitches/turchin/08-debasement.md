## Premise
A mintmaster in the Roman Empire during the Crisis of the Third Century (circa 260 CE) receives orders to reduce the silver content of the denarius from 5% to 3%. He has been receiving similar orders for twenty years -- he remembers when the coins were 40% silver, then 20%, then 10%. Each reduction was presented as temporary, emergency, necessary. He has watched the empire's fiscal health chart itself in the metal he handles daily. Now, as the empire fragments into three competing states and fifty men claim the purple in a single generation, he must decide whether to obey the latest order or flee with his family to one of the breakaway provinces, where a different emperor is minting coins with slightly more silver and slightly more legitimacy.

## Source Inspiration
The Rome essay highlights currency debasement as "a vivid marker of fiscal collapse," with coin silver content plummeting from 50% to under 5% during the third century crisis. The essay describes this period as the nadir when "it seemed the Roman Empire might simply cease to exist." The mintmaster's perspective literalizes the abstraction of "state fiscal crisis" -- he holds the evidence in his hands, weighs it, stamps it, knows exactly how much each coin lies about its own value.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Robert Harris (style)**: Harris's crisp, thriller-paced historical fiction that makes Roman political intrigue feel like a contemporary conspiracy is ideal for a story built around institutional corruption and survival. His prose treats ancient Rome as a functioning (or malfunctioning) state rather than a monument.
- **Mary Renault (style)**: Renault's ability to inhabit ancient minds without anachronism -- to render the ancient world's emotional and moral textures as fully real rather than exotic -- provides the interior register for a character whose daily work is both utterly ordinary and historically catastrophic.
- **Imperium by Robert Harris (structure)**: The structure of a functionary navigating a political system in crisis, where institutional knowledge becomes simultaneously invaluable and dangerous. Tiro's secretarial perspective on Cicero's career mirrors the mintmaster's artisanal perspective on imperial collapse.
- **The Sorrow of War by Bao Ninh (themes)**: The theme of a professional witness to state failure -- someone whose craft forces them to see what others can deny. Bao Ninh's narrator cannot stop remembering; the mintmaster cannot stop measuring. Both are trapped by the precision of their knowledge.

## Notes
The coins themselves should function as the story's primary metaphor without the narrative announcing them as such. Each order to debase is a political event, a military event, and a personal crisis simultaneously. The mintmaster's expertise -- his ability to detect fraud by weight, by ring, by color -- makes him the most honest man in a system built on accelerating dishonesty. The decision to flee should be motivated not by ideology but by metallurgy: at 3% silver, the coins are no longer coins in any meaningful sense. They are promises with nothing behind them.
