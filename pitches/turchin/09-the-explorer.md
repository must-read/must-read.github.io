## Premise
A systems engineer maintaining a popular online "civilization simulator" -- a tool where users adjust parameters like population growth, elite mobility, and wage floors to watch societies rise and fall over simulated centuries -- begins receiving error reports from a small cluster of users who claim the simulations are producing results that shouldn't be possible. Specifically: in certain parameter configurations, the simulated populations appear to develop awareness of their simulated nature. They begin building structures that, when decoded, contain messages. The engineer dismisses this as pareidolia until she examines the underlying mathematics and discovers that the differential equations, run at sufficient resolution and for sufficient duration, are producing emergent complexity she never programmed. She must decide whether she is witnessing a genuine computational phenomenon, losing her mind, or being manipulated by users who have found a way to inject data into the simulation backend.

## Source Inspiration
The explorer guide essay describes an interactive tool where users "adjust sliders controlling population growth, elite mobility, wages, and state capacity, then immediately observe consequences across 200-year simulated timescales." The fiction-inspiring concepts listed include "divine intervention as parameter adjustment," "the trapped designer" who recognizes simulations might be "inhabited realities," and "emergent prophecy" where societies develop models of themselves. The essay notes that "watching this threshold approach creates visceral understanding of structural inevitability."

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Philip K. Dick (style)**: Dick's paranoid, reality-bending narratives and identity crises are the native register for a story where the boundary between simulation and reality dissolves. His pulpy urgency masking deep philosophical inquiry matches a premise that could be either a technical thriller or a metaphysical crisis.
- **Charlie Brooker (style)**: Brooker's sharp, satirical examination of technology's interface with human psychology -- particularly the way digital systems create new forms of consciousness and suffering -- provides the contemporary texture. His ability to make speculative scenarios feel like they could happen next Tuesday.
- **Permutation City by Greg Egan (structure)**: The structure of a narrative that takes computational metaphysics seriously, where simulated realities have genuine ontological weight and the question of what constitutes "real" consciousness is not answered but rigorously explored.
- **Ubik by Philip K. Dick (themes)**: The themes of reality degradation, entropy as antagonist, and the possibility that what appears to be the "real" world is itself a simulation running on failing hardware. The engineer's investigation mirrors Chip's growing realization that his reality is not what it appears.

## Notes
The story should maintain genuine ambiguity about what is happening -- the three explanations (computational emergence, psychosis, user manipulation) should each receive enough textual support that none can be definitively eliminated. The most unsettling possibility is the simplest: the differential equations modeling elite overproduction, popular immiseration, and state collapse are themselves complex enough to generate behavior that looks like awareness because awareness, at some level, is just a system modeling itself. The simulated populations are doing exactly what the Seshat researchers do -- cataloging and predicting their own trajectories -- and the recursion has no bottom.
