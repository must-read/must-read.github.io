## Premise
A woman who has been performing normalcy for so long she has forgotten what preceded it begins to notice the performance failing. Small things first: she cannot remember the name of the dish she orders every Tuesday, her signature comes out wrong, she laughs a beat too late. She works at a call center where scripts are mandatory, and the scripts have been her skeleton — without them, she discovers, there may be nothing underneath. The story follows a week during which the performance degrades, told from her perspective with the slow horror of someone watching their own mask slip and finding not a face beneath it but another mask, and beneath that, nothing.

## Source Inspiration
The site's "Theater of Starvation" essay argues that the narrator of *Hunger* performs self-sufficiency as "a private theatrical scene where only he judges himself." Identity shifts rapidly — "writer, thief, penitent, pastor, lover, madman" — but each role lasts only as long as the scene requires. The essay's key insight: "the performer loses stable self entirely." Poverty strips away social props, and what remains is not an authentic core but more performance. This pitch asks: what if the performance is all there ever was? What if removing it reveals not vulnerability but void?

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Jackson)**: The domestic horror of a woman whose world is shrinking. Jackson's protagonists inhabit houses and routines that are simultaneously shelter and prison. The unreliable narrator who may be the most dangerous person in the story. *Castle*'s Merricat performs a version of normalcy that is entirely constructed and entirely believed.
- **AuthorB (Moshfegh)**: The flat-affect protagonist who observes her own dissolution with clinical detachment. Moshfegh's narrators are repulsive and magnetic. The specific horror of a character who is performing humanness rather than experiencing it, rendered in prose that is precise, cold, and darkly funny.
- **WorkX (We Have Always Lived in the Castle)**: The architecture of isolation maintained by ritual. Merricat's routines — burying objects, naming things, following rules — create a performance of order that conceals catastrophe. The call center scripts serve the same function.
- **WorkY (Convenience Store Woman)**: Murata's protagonist has no authentic self outside her role as a convenience store worker. The store's manual is her personality. The horror and comedy of discovering that social performance might be the entirety of identity, not a layer over it. The protagonist who finds this revelation not tragic but relieving — until others notice.

## Notes
The call center setting should be rendered with the specific, banal detail of actual call center work — the headset, the script on the screen, the metrics dashboard, the supervisor who listens randomly. The degradation of the performance should be incremental and precise. The horror is quiet: no monsters, no violence, just the growing suspicion that the woman has been a performance all along and the performance is now ending. The ending should not explain what she is. It should show what happens when a coworker finally asks, "Are you okay?" and she does not know how to answer because she does not know what the question means.
