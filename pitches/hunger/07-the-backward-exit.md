## Premise
An elderly widow visits the home of her estranged son and his family for what both parties understand may be the last time. Over the course of a Sunday afternoon, she sits in their living room, eats their food, holds their youngest child, and says nothing about the ten-year silence between them. The son says nothing either. The story is told from the wife's perspective — the daughter-in-law who watches these two people perform proximity while withholding everything that matters, and who recognizes in their careful choreography a kind of love that has no other way to express itself.

## Source Inspiration
The "backward exit" from *Hunger* Part III: the narrator "rushed to the door, and went out backwards" from Ylajali's apartment — retreating while maintaining face-to-face orientation, unable to turn away from what is being lost. The site's companion text calls this "reverence and terror combined." The gesture of leaving while facing the person you are losing captures a specific kind of love: one that cannot speak, cannot stay, but cannot look away. The pitch transposes this from romantic desperation to family estrangement.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Robinson)**: The theological attention to ordinary domestic moments. Robinson's prose finds radiance in a glass of water, a shaft of light, a hand on a table. Her sentences are long, cadenced, meditative — the rhythm of a mind trying to understand grace in the midst of loss. The widow's visit is a *Gilead*-scale event: small, contained, infinite.
- **AuthorB (Strout)**: The sharp, unsentimental eye for family dysfunction. Strout's characters love each other badly and know it. Olive Kitteridge's particular genius is showing love that expresses itself as silence, stubbornness, and showing up. The daughter-in-law's outsider perspective mirrors Strout's method of approaching characters from the side.
- **WorkX (Gilead)**: A letter from a dying man to a son he will not see grow up — the architecture of one-directional communication, of saying things that cannot be answered. The widow is performing the inverse: she is present in the room but cannot speak.
- **WorkY (Olive Kitteridge)**: The linked-story structure where a difficult woman is understood through the perspectives of those around her. Olive's refusal to be warm, her love expressed as attendance rather than tenderness. The specific New England mode of showing up and shutting up as the deepest available intimacy.

## Notes
The story must avoid sentimentality. The widow is not a sweet old woman — she is difficult, opinionated, and the estrangement is partly her fault. The son is not cruel — he is exhausted and has drawn a boundary. The daughter-in-law is not a mediator — she is a witness, and her narration should be more observational than emotional. The youngest child, too young to understand the silence, should be the only person in the room behaving naturally. No reconciliation speech. The widow leaves. She does not go backward through the door, but the reader should feel the impulse.
