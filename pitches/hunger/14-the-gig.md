## Premise
A freelance content writer for an AI company discovers that the platform's algorithm has learned to replicate his style so precisely that new clients cannot distinguish his work from the machine's output. His income halves, then quarters. He continues writing — not because anyone is paying but because the act of writing is the only evidence he has that he is still the person who writes. The story follows three months of accelerating precarity as the writer occupies the exact position of Hamsun's narrator: producing work on speculation, income irregular, housing precarious, one rejected pitch away from the street. The AI does not replace him dramatically. It makes him redundant gradually, the way weather erodes a cliff.

## Source Inspiration
The site's "After Hunger" essay identifies the narrator of *Hunger* as the prototype of the contemporary gig worker: "freelance writer with no employer, safety net, institutional affiliation. Produces work on speculation; income irregular, housing precarious. One rejected article away from the street." The essay connects this to "the particular shame of educated poverty, of downward mobility" and names contemporary parallels: "journalists at folding publications, adjunct professors, MFA novelists unable to sell books." This pitch makes the parallel literal and adds the specific 2026 twist: the writer's replacement is not a cheaper human but a machine trained on his own words.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Hideo Levy)**: The Japanese-American novelist who writes in Japanese about displacement, language as identity, and the experience of existing between cultures and systems. Levy's protagonists are linguistically gifted people trapped in systems that do not value their particular gifts. His spare, precise prose captures the experience of being fluent in a language that no longer has economic use.
- **AuthorB (Saunders)**: The corporate-vernacular absurdism of a system that eliminates humans while using human language to describe the elimination. Saunders' specific talent for showing how dehumanizing systems generate their own euphemisms. The radical empathy for people caught inside machines they helped build.
- **WorkX (Hunger)**: The direct structural model — the spiral of hope-effort-failure-rescue repeating with intensification. The writer's relationship to his own work mirrors the narrator's: is the writing good? Does it matter if it is good when no one will pay for it? The writing-as-faith-without-evidence theme.
- **WorkY (Severance)**: The novel about a woman going through the motions of office work during an apocalypse — the routine that continues after the reason for it has ended. The protagonist who cannot stop working even when work has become meaningless. The specific horror of competence in a system that no longer requires it.

## Notes
The AI should never be villainized or personified. It is a tool that does what it does. The horror is structural, not malicious. The writer should be good at his job — not a hack being justly replaced but a skilled practitioner watching his craft become a commodity. His continued writing, unpaid, should be rendered as neither noble nor pathetic but as the thing he does because he does not know what else to do. The three-month compression should mirror Hunger's four-part spiral: each month a variation on the same arc, each more desperate. The ending should not be the writer finding a new career or making peace with his obsolescence. He should still be writing.
