## Premise
A man living in a tent camp beneath an overpass in a Southern city — not homeless by the usual narrative, but a former restaurant cook who lost his apartment and chose the camp over the shelter — gnaws a bone he found behind a barbecue joint. The bone becomes the story's center: its taste, its inadequacy, the tears that come while eating it, the particular shame of an expert on food reduced to scavenging scraps. Over one night in the camp, he tells his story to a new arrival — a recently evicted grandmother who insists she is only staying one night — and their conversation becomes a battle between his hard-won knowledge of how things are and her desperate insistence that her situation is temporary.

## Source Inspiration
The bone-gnawing scene in *Hunger* Part III: the narrator eats a bone in darkness, weeping, vomiting, cursing. The site's companion text describes it as "the narrator as animal in den, hunger stripped of pretense" — the bone "wet and dirty with my tears." The body in "total revolt." But the deeper resonance is from the essay "The Theater of Starvation": the narrator's expertise (he is a writer, a man of language and perception) becomes torture when applied to his own degradation. He can describe his suffering in perfect sentences. A cook tasting a discarded bone and knowing exactly what spices were used, what cut it came from, how it was prepared — that is the same torture.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (McCarthy)**: The prose of men reduced to elemental states. McCarthy's sentences in *Suttree* — long, baroque, biblical — describe poverty and the body's persistence with a grandeur that neither ennobles nor degrades. The riverbank camps, the drinking, the conversations between men who have stopped pretending. McCarthy's specific genius for rendering food, weather, and physical sensation.
- **AuthorB (O'Connor)**: The comedy of self-deception meeting violent truth. O'Connor's characters believe they are one thing and discover they are another. The grandmother in "A Good Man Is Hard to Find" performs gentility until the moment of grace, which arrives as catastrophe. The evicted grandmother in this pitch performs temporariness with the same conviction.
- **WorkX (Suttree)**: The architecture of a man living outside conventional society in a Southern city (Knoxville), his relationships with the marginal and dispossessed, the river as both setting and symbol. Cornelius Suttree's refusal to return to his family's comfort. The novel's episodic structure — each encounter a complete world.
- **WorkY (A Good Man Is Hard to Find)**: The collision between a character's self-image and reality. The grandmother who believes she is a lady. The Misfit who believes he is a philosopher. The violence that strips pretense. In this pitch, the violence is not physical but economic — the grandmother's discovery that "one night" is a fiction.

## Notes
The Southern city should be specific (Memphis, Birmingham, Nashville — somewhere with a food culture the cook can reference). The bone should be described with culinary precision — the cut, the smoke ring, the residual seasoning. The camp should not be sentimentalized or horrified — it is a place where people live, with its own social structure and rules. The cook should be neither bitter nor resigned; he should be competent, even authoritative, within his reduced circumstances. The grandmother should be genuinely difficult — not a sympathetic victim but a person whose class assumptions are being demolished in real time. The night should end at dawn with no resolution: she is still there. He knew she would be.
