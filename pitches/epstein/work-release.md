## Idea

A county jail guard assigned to the work-release program of a wealthy convicted sex offender describes his thirteen months monitoring the man. The predator leaves the facility six days a week for "office visits." He returns each night smelling of cologne. Other guards stop asking questions. The narrator tells himself it's not his job to judge what happens during those hours. The story is about the slow moral erosion of a decent person who accommodates evil by redefining the boundaries of his responsibility.

## Source Material

Epstein served 13 months in Palm Beach County jail with work release, permitted to leave six days per week for office visits -- described in the source as "essentially a work-study program." The source notes: "Victims watched predator receive minimal punishment." The deal was structured so that the people tasked with his custody were also the witnesses to his continued impunity.

## Why These Authors/Works

- **Jackson's** spare, unsettling prose -- the horror of social conformity; the narrator who participates in something terrible because everyone else is; domestic normality as the vehicle for atrocity
- **Tremblay's** ambiguous horror -- is the guard complicit or just powerless? The literary prose applied to genre dread; the question that refuses to resolve
- **We Need to Talk About Kevin** (Shriver): a narrator reconstructing their own complicity in hindsight; the story told to a "you" who already knows what happened; the unbearable weight of what wasn't done
- **A Good Man Is Hard to Find** (O'Connor): the violence of grace; the moment when a character confronts their own moral failure and it's too late; grotesque normality

## Core Tension

The guard is not a villain. He follows every procedure. He files every report. He does exactly his job. That's the horror.
