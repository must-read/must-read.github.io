## Idea

A retired Secret Service agent is deposed in a civil lawsuit about the flights he accompanied -- and the flights he didn't. He served on a former president's detail and logged 26 trips on a private financier's jet. Some flights had full detail. Others, the agent was told to stand down. No one ever explained why. Now, years later, a young attorney is asking him precise questions about dates, manifests, and gaps in the log. The story unfolds as a deposition transcript interwoven with the agent's memory, and the gap between what he recorded, what he saw, and what he understood widens with each question.

## Source Material

Between 2001-2003, 26 documented Clinton flights occurred on Epstein's jet -- some without Secret Service. Flight details remain partially restricted. The source notes that no investigation was ever conducted into these flights. The flight logs became one of the most scrutinized pieces of evidence, but the gaps in the record -- who was present, who was absent, and who decided to omit the detail -- remain unexplained.

## Why These Authors/Works

- **Le Carre's** dense, bureaucratic realism -- the moral exhaustion of serving a system; the deposition as interrogation; institutional loyalty as its own kind of prison; layered, literary prose for the intelligence world
- **DeLillo's** paranoid, systems-level vision -- the sense that the surface reality is a performance concealing the actual structure of power; the flight log as both document and symbol; language as the thing that simultaneously reveals and conceals
- **All the President's Men** (Woodward/Bernstein): the investigative process as narrative; the power of documents and records; the trail of accountability that leads upward
- **The Spy Who Came in from the Cold** (le Carre): a loyal operative who discovers that the system he served was using him; the devastating realization that duty and morality were never aligned

## Core Tension

The agent was a professional. He followed orders. He didn't ask why some flights had no detail. Now, under oath, he has to account for a kind of not-knowing that was also a kind of knowing. The deposition forces him to say aloud what he has spent years not thinking.
