## Idea

A recently retired federal prosecutor reflects on a plea deal he negotiated fifteen years ago -- one that granted blanket immunity to unnamed co-conspirators of a powerful sex trafficker. He tells himself the story of how reasonable each incremental decision was. The narrative is structured as a long dinner conversation with his adult daughter, who is now a victims' rights attorney. She never directly accuses him. She doesn't need to.

## Source Material

Alexander Acosta negotiated the 2008 Non-Prosecution Agreement that gave Epstein a 13-month county jail sentence with work release and blanket immunity for all co-conspirators. The agreement violated the Crime Victims' Rights Act (victims were never notified). Acosta later became Trump's Labor Secretary. When the deal became public, he resigned -- the only official ever held accountable for any part of the case.

## Why These Authors/Works

- **Ishiguro's** restrained, understated narration -- the narrator who tells you everything while understanding nothing about himself, the quiet devastation of a life spent serving a system that consumed what it was meant to protect
- **Cusk's** austere, essayistic observation -- dialogue as the mechanism through which the narrator's self-deception is revealed without ever being named; the daughter as the Outline-style interlocutor who draws out truth by saying almost nothing
- **Never Let Me Go**: a system of profound cruelty accepted by its participants as simply how things are; characters who comply with their own destruction because the alternative is unthinkable
- **Remains of the Day**: a man who served power faithfully, who made every decision according to professional duty, and who realizes too late that duty was the mechanism of his moral failure

## Core Tension

The prosecutor genuinely believes he made the best deal available. His daughter's silence is worse than any accusation. The reader watches him construct an airtight justification that is also a confession.
