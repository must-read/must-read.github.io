## Premise
A moral philosopher has spent two years hosting a weekly podcast for eleven listeners on a remote research station. His subject is "collective moral processing" -- a mathematical framework that converts conscious moments into abstract units ("utils") and demonstrates, with elegant equations, that the catastrophe which killed nine billion people was the optimal outcome. The math always works. One night he begins experiencing intrusive visions: a woman with dark hair, a young boy, faces he has never seen. He cannot make them stop. A colleague confronts him: "They were real." The philosopher attempts to recalculate. He writes the equation. W (value of consciousness) versus M (mission utility). 10^40 versus 2.214 times 10^19. The math still justifies it. But his pen tears through the paper. He records a final episode confessing that his framework was "not a tool for moral reasoning but a tool for" -- and he cannot finish the sentence. He does not kill himself. He sits in the dark and lets the faces remain.

## Source Inspiration
Douglas Kemper in Galt's Gulch -- the ethicist who developed the "Algebra of Suffering" to convert the deaths of nine billion into manageable variables. He hosts a shrinking podcast, experiences intrusive visions of faces he never knew, and eventually collapses when his framework fails. Solomon confronts him with the simple statement "They were real." In Chapter 36, Douglas's final breakdown: his pen tears through the paper, he broadcasts a confession, and he chooses presence over suicide -- allowing the faces to remain "real and unabstracted."

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Fyodor Dostoevsky)**: Dostoevsky's unsparing psychological examination of intellectual pride -- the conviction that a sufficiently elegant theory permits any action. Raskolnikov's "extraordinary man" theory is the Algebra of Suffering in embryo: the belief that mathematical certainty elevates you above ordinary moral constraint.
- **AuthorB (David Foster Wallace)**: Wallace's attention to the way intelligence becomes a cage -- how the most articulate people use language to avoid feeling, how irony and analytical frameworks function as emotional anesthesia. The philosopher's podcast voice should carry Wallace's hyper-articulate desperation.
- **WorkX (Crime and Punishment)**: Structural DNA of a brilliant mind that has committed an unforgivable act and slowly, agonizingly confronts the inadequacy of its own justification. The philosopher's two-year descent from certainty to collapse mirrors Raskolnikov's journey -- except here, the crime is nine billion times larger.
- **WorkY (The Stranger)**: Thematic DNA of emotional detachment as a form of moral failure. Meursault's inability to feel the appropriate response to death becomes the philosopher's inability to feel the weight of his calculations. The intrusive faces are the return of what his framework suppressed.

## Notes
The podcast format could serve as a structural device -- the story intercuts between recorded episodes (polished, mathematical, persuasive) and the philosopher's private experience (intrusive images, trembling hands, the growing gap between his public voice and his interior). The ending must resist redemption. He does not become good. He merely stops being able to pretend. Could work in the 4,000-6,000 word range.
