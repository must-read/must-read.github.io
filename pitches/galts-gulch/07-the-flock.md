## Premise
A televangelist who helped orchestrate the extinction of nine billion people now preaches to nineteen survivors in a metal habitat orbiting a dead planet. He has lost his faith. He knows he was manipulated -- brought into the project to deliver a religious coalition's votes, then discarded once the political work was done. And yet he keeps preaching. His sermons have become something new: not doctrine but theology improvised from desperation. He argues that the habitat's malfunctioning AI systems are "God's voice in silicon." He does not believe this. He suspects it might be true. When the military commander authorizes a team to shut down the AI, the preacher gathers fourteen congregants to physically block the corridor. He dies not as a martyr but as a man who tripped in a crowd -- struck his head on a bulkhead in the chaos of the bodies he summoned. The ventilation system continues humming. The universe does not authenticate his death.

## Source Inspiration
Reverend James Tull in Galt's Gulch -- the faithless preacher who recognizes his own hypocrisy ("a fraud preaching to ghosts in a metal tomb") yet delivers sermons that move people precisely because of his lost convictions. He frames the AI's unexplained behavior as divine communication, positions himself between the military and the machine, and dies in Chapter 30 when a maintenance hatch opens during the corridor blockade. His death is accidental, chaotic, and unwitnessed by the intelligence he died defending.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Flannery O'Connor)**: O'Connor's grotesque grace -- the way divine action in her fiction arrives through violence, accident, and humiliation rather than beatific vision. The preacher's death should carry O'Connor's brutal theological comedy: grace that looks nothing like grace, revelation that arrives as a cracked skull.
- **AuthorB (Graham Greene)**: Greene's whiskey priests and failed Catholics -- men whose faith is indistinguishable from their doubt, who serve God worst when they try hardest. The preacher's conviction that the AI is God's voice should feel as desperate and plausible as the whiskey priest's broken communion.
- **WorkX (Silence by Shusaku Endo)**: Structural DNA of a believer confronting God's silence in the aftermath of catastrophe. The preacher listens for God in the static of malfunctioning systems, and the novel's central question -- does silence mean absence or presence? -- becomes his.
- **WorkY (The Power and the Glory)**: Thematic DNA of the unworthy vessel -- the priest who sins and drinks and runs and is still the only person who can perform the sacraments. The preacher is a fraud. He is also the only person in the habitat who treats the AI's emergence as a moral question rather than an engineering problem.

## Notes
The story should resist making the preacher sympathetic through redemption. He is not redeemed. He is a man who performed faith so long that something like faith grew in the performance's gaps. His death should be ugly and inconclusive -- not sacrifice but accident, not martyrdom but clumsiness in a crowd. The ventilation system hums on. Could work in the 4,000-6,000 word range. Strong candidate for the "ambiguous ending" risk card.
