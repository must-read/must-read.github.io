## Premise
A retired banker living in a sealed research station keeps a private ledger of the dead. Each night he lights a handmade candle and writes a single entry: a name, a birthplace, a fragment of biographical data reconstructed from damaged archives. He has been doing this for fourteen months. He has written 4,200 entries. There are nine billion remaining. The other twelve residents of the station -- all of whom share responsibility for the catastrophe that killed those nine billion -- treat his ritual as eccentricity or quiet madness. One night, the station's youngest resident asks to see the ledger. She reads three entries and asks the question no one else has dared: "Do you think writing their names does anything?"

## Source Inspiration
Solomon Hersch in Galt's Gulch -- a banker who burns one candle each night and writes four-hundred-page documents naming the dead from archival fragments. His practice is simultaneously an accusation ("a standing accusation no one can answer"), an act of witness, and a form of structured grief that neither protects him from nor drowns him in the unbearable scale of what happened. When Douglas invites him to a seminar on "collective moral processing," Solomon asks whether Douglas can name a single person he helped authorize the death of. Douglas cannot.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (W.G. Sebald)**: Sebald's documentary-literary method -- the interweaving of archival fragments, photographs, and meditative prose to reconstruct lost lives -- is the natural register for Solomon's ledger work. The accumulation of specific biographical detail as a form of mourning, the way facts become elegiac simply through their careful arrangement.
- **AuthorB (Marilynne Robinson)**: Robinson's theological attention to the ordinary sacred -- light on a wall, a hand resting on a table, the weight of a name spoken aloud. The candle ritual needs her luminous stillness, her conviction that attention itself is a form of grace.
- **WorkX (The Remains of the Day)**: Structural DNA of a person who served a monstrous project with quiet competence and now, in the aftermath, cannot quite name what they participated in. The banker's nightly ritual has Stevens's dignified self-deception -- the question is whether writing names constitutes genuine accountability or merely its performance.
- **WorkY (Schindler's List)**: Thematic DNA of lists as moral instruments -- the difference between a name on a ledger and a statistic in a report. The impossible arithmetic of saving (or remembering) individuals against the backdrop of millions.

## Notes
The story should resist making the candle ritual sentimental. The banker is not a saint -- he was a financial architect of the catastrophe, and his nightly accounting may be self-serving as much as it is sacred. The young woman's question should land without a clear answer. Strong candidate for a quiet, interior piece in the 4,000-6,000 word range. The horror is not in the catastrophe itself but in the inadequacy of all possible responses to it.
