## Premise
A seventy-nine-year-old theoretical physicist whose original paper -- "On the Obligation of Seed Intelligence" -- provided the intellectual foundation for a project that killed nine billion people now spends his nights drawing portraits of the dead from archival photographs. He has produced over four thousand drawings. They are technically perfect. The eyes are wrong. Every portrait captures the correct anatomy, the precise proportions, the individual features -- but something is missing. He calls it "the expression of a person in the middle of being alive." For fourteen months he fails. He draws and redraws the same faces. He sits in silent communion with a man who burns candles. He listens to a faithless preacher. Then one night, working from a photograph of a six-year-old boy from Quito, the dissonance vanishes. The portrait captures what his mathematics erased: individual, irreducible human value. He lights a candle beside it. The story ends not with narrative action but with stillness -- a man who finally sees what he spent his life abstracting away.

## Source Inspiration
Arthur Pendleton in Galt's Gulch -- the physicist whose abstract framework for propagating consciousness across the universe justified "acceptable costs" and treated nine billion lives as variables. His obsessive portrait-drawing spans the entire novel (Chapters 1 and 38 bookend the narrative with "The Eyes"). Over four thousand portraits with technically perfect but "spiritually empty" eyes, until the final breakthrough: the portrait of Mateo, a boy from Quito, captures "the expression of a person in the middle of being alive." Arthur's mistake "was altitude -- viewing existence from such height that individual faces became invisible."

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Toni Morrison)**: Morrison's insistence on the specificity of individual lives against historical erasure. Arthur's project -- naming and drawing each face, refusing the aggregate -- is a Morrisonian act of recovery. The prose should carry her weight, her refusal to let the reader look away from what was lost.
- **AuthorB (J.M. Coetzee)**: Coetzee's spare, pitiless examination of complicity and the intellectual's relationship to violence. Arthur is a Coetzee protagonist: brilliant, guilty, performing a gesture of atonement whose adequacy he cannot assess. The self-interrogation should be relentless.
- **WorkX (Austerlitz by W.G. Sebald)**: Structural DNA of archival reconstruction -- photographs, documents, and memory fragments assembled into a portrait of what was destroyed. Arthur's studio full of charcoal drawings is Sebald's method made physical: the attempt to rebuild vanished lives from remnants.
- **WorkY (The Road by Cormac McCarthy)**: Thematic DNA of a world after the end, and the question of whether any act of preservation matters when everything has been lost. McCarthy's father carrying the fire becomes Arthur drawing faces -- a gesture whose meaning depends entirely on whether meaning persists after catastrophe.

## Notes
The story should be almost plotless. The drama is interior: a man attempting to see, failing, and finally seeing. The technical challenge is rendering four thousand failed portraits without repetition -- each failure should reveal something different about what Arthur is missing. The breakthrough should not feel earned or redemptive; it should feel like accident, like grace that arrives without permission. Could work in the 3,000-5,000 word range. Strong candidate for the "constraint of space" risk card: the entire story takes place in Arthur's studio module.
