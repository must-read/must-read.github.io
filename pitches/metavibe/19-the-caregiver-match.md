## Premise
A home healthcare coordinator uses an AI matching system to pair caregivers with patients -- the algorithm scores candidates based on skills, geography, language, availability, and "historical success" metrics. But the system's best-performing metric, the one most predictive of positive outcomes, is one it cannot explain: it keeps pairing a specific caregiver with patients who are about to die. Not hospice patients. Patients whose records show no terminal indicators. The caregiver, a quiet woman who drives a sedan with 200,000 miles on it, does not know she is being matched this way. The coordinator discovers the pattern, and must decide whether to tell the caregiver, tell the patients' families, adjust the algorithm, or simply let the system keep doing what it does -- sending this woman, unknowing, to sit with people in their final weeks.

## Source Inspiration
The healthcare platform book's chapter on caregiver matching: "Evaluates dozens of factors simultaneously. Scores based on skills, language, geography, history, preferences. Surfaces candidates with explanations for coordinator decisions." The book notes "Geographic distance mattered more than we expected" in predicting success and describes churn prediction that "identifies at-risk caregivers and patients before departure." The story takes the "anomaly detection" that "surfaces unexpected patterns for human investigation" and imagines the most unexpected pattern possible: an algorithm that has learned to predict death, expressed only through its matching recommendations.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Marilynne Robinson (style)**: Robinson's meditative attention to the sacred in ordinary life -- a caregiver driving her old sedan to sit with patients, the holiness of presence at the threshold of death. Her long, cadenced sentences for the coordinator's moral reasoning.
- **Hanya Yanagihara (style)**: Yanagihara's unflinching attention to vulnerability and care -- the physical reality of home healthcare, the intimacy of bodies, the exhaustion and tenderness of people who do this work. Her emotional intensity contained within domestic scale.
- **A Little Life (structure)**: The structural pattern of care as the central dramatic action -- not crisis or adventure but the daily, accumulating work of being present for someone in need. The story's events should be small and its stakes enormous.
- **Olive Kitteridge (themes)**: Strout's themes of small-town proximity to death, the people who show up and the people who don't, the unspoken knowledge that circulates in communities where everyone knows everyone's business. The caregiver should be recognized by the families without anyone naming what she represents.

## Notes
The coordinator should never confront the caregiver directly. The story's tension is the coordinator watching the algorithm's matches, seeing the pattern hold, and wrestling with what it means to have a system that knows something about mortality that the humans inside it do not. The caregiver's scenes should be rendered with absolute normalcy -- she is doing her job, doing it well, and what the algorithm sees in her is invisible to everyone, possibly including herself.
