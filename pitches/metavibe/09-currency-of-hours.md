## Premise
In a world where lifespan is literal currency -- measured to the minute, transferred through embedded chips, displayed on wrist counters that tick down like bombs -- a temporal arbitrageur (someone who buys low-value minutes from the desperate and sells them to the anxious) watches his counter drop below two years after paying rent. The landlord's phrase "cost of living" has become literal: the price of continuing to exist. The story follows one working day as he trades in other people's time, visiting the desperate sellers (the elderly cashing out final years, the addicted selling minutes for fixes, the parents buying time for sick children) and the anxious buyers (the wealthy stockpiling decades, the middle-class purchasing "just enough"). Each transaction is a moral event. He is the middleman in mortality, and his own counter is ticking down.

## Source Inspiration
World 5 of "Change Without Notice" presents a fully realized time-as-currency economy: "Buy something, lose time. Sell something, gain it. The economy ran on mortality." The wrist display counting down, banks where "years and decades stockpiled in vaults," and the option to "sell non-essential experiences: memories, sensations, the capacity to taste sweetness" to buy more time. The devastating observation: "purchased days in a diminished state, unable to fully experience what they'd paid for."

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Ursula K. Le Guin (style)**: Le Guin's anthropological worldbuilding -- the time-currency economy rendered not as dystopia but as a functioning society with its own logic, customs, and moral norms. Her ability to make alien systems feel lived-in.
- **Cormac McCarthy (style)**: McCarthy's stark, unsparing prose for the transactions themselves -- the moments where people sell their remaining time. No sentimentality, no narrative mercy. The border between life and its absence rendered in declarative sentences.
- **In Time (structure)**: The structural premise of time as literal currency, with the ticking clock creating constant tension. But where the film went to heist-thriller, this story stays in the daily grind of temporal commerce.
- **The Dispossessed (themes)**: Le Guin's interrogation of economic systems as moral architectures -- the time-currency economy examined not as a villain to defeat but as a system to understand. Who benefits, who suffers, and why the people inside it cannot imagine alternatives.

## Notes
The arbitrageur should not be a hero or a villain. He is a middleman, and the story should inhabit the moral ambiguity of that position: he gives desperate people money (time) for their time (life), and sells comfort to the anxious. Both sides need him. Neither side should be grateful. The story should end mid-transaction, his wrist counter visible but not commented on.
