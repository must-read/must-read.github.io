## Premise
A former novelist -- once lauded for her precise, handcrafted prose -- now works as a "taste curator" for an AI publishing house, selecting which machine-generated novels to release. She is exceptional at the job because her aesthetic judgment is flawless. She is destroyed by the job because aesthetic judgment is all she has left. Over the course of a single editorial meeting, she evaluates three AI-generated manuscripts, each better than anything she ever wrote, and must decide which ones deserve to exist. The story is told in her assessment notes, which begin as professional evaluations and become, line by line, a reckoning with the question of what a writer is when writing no longer requires a writer.

## Source Inspiration
"The Curator's Manifesto" essay argues that in an AI world, "human value shifts from doing to directing" and "taste -- the capacity to recognize quality and guide selection -- becomes the primary human contribution." The essay frames this as liberation. The story asks: what if it is not liberation but amputation? The essay's key formulation -- "taste in, meaning out" -- becomes the protagonist's professional motto and private torment.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Rachel Cusk (style)**: Cusk's austere, essayistic autofiction -- observation as narrative, stripped-down dialogue frames, the narrator as vessel for other people's stories. The protagonist's assessment notes should have Cusk's forensic detachment.
- **Ottessa Moshfegh (style)**: Moshfegh's mordant self-loathing and physical revulsion at comfortable situations. The protagonist should experience her own competence as a kind of nausea. Her precision should disgust her.
- **Outline (structure)**: The structural frame of a protagonist who reveals herself only through her observations of others. The three manuscripts she evaluates should reflect aspects of her own failed writing back at her, though she never says so.
- **The Remains of the Day (themes)**: The theme of a person who has perfected service to a master (here: AI-generated content) and realizes too late that mastery of service is not the same as having lived. The taste curator as a butler of literature.

## Notes
The three manuscripts she evaluates should each be described in enough detail that the reader can feel their quality -- and feel, alongside the protagonist, the particular devastation of recognizing greatness you did not create. The ending should not resolve whether taste-without-creation constitutes a meaningful life. The protagonist's final assessment note should be a single sentence that could be about the manuscript or about herself.
