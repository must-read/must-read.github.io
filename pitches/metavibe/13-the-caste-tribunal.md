## Premise
A man is on trial for marrying outside his caste without obtaining special dispensation -- a marriage that has lasted twelve years, that the state documented and taxed and recognized, that produced routines and habits and presumably intimacy. The problem: he has no memory of the marriage. No wedding, no courtship, no first meeting. His wife sits across the courtroom, a stranger in a blue dress, and she is waiting for an answer too. The tribunal wants to know why he violated caste protocols. He cannot say "I don't remember choosing this" because the implication -- that the caste system and the marriage and the twelve years were imposed rather than chosen -- is more dangerous than any answer he could give. So he says: "I loved her." And the tribunal nods, because they have heard that before.

## Source Inspiration
World 1 of "Change Without Notice" -- the opening vignette that establishes the novel's central condition. The man in the worn smooth chair, sweating where decades of defendants have sweated, confronting a tribunal about a marriage he cannot remember to a woman who "looked up at this. Her eyes met his for the first time. There was something in them. Not anger. Not accusation. Something more complicated. Something that suggested she was waiting for an answer too." The devastating line: "I loved her, because it seemed like the thing someone would say in this situation."

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Franz Kafka (style)**: Kafka's bureaucratic nightmare rendered in flat, exhausted prose -- the tribunal that has been in session for three hours, the institutional smell, the water stain on the ceiling, the procedural politeness concealing total power. The defendant's compliance as survival strategy.
- **Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie (style)**: Adichie's precision with the politics of identity and belonging -- caste as lived reality rather than abstraction, the ways social categories shape who you can love and how you can live. Her warmth and political edge applied to a marriage that may or may not have been chosen.
- **The Trial (structure)**: Kafka's structure of accusation without comprehensible crime, proceedings that assume guilt, a system that will process the defendant regardless of his answers. The tribunal is not seeking truth; it is completing paperwork.
- **The Handmaid's Tale (themes)**: The theme of state control over intimate life -- who may marry whom, under what terms, with what permissions. The caste system as reproductive and social engineering. The woman in the blue dress who has her own story the tribunal is not interested in hearing.

## Notes
The wife should be the story's gravitational center despite having fewer lines. Her silence, her blue dress chosen carefully for the occasion, her eyes that hold "something more complicated" -- the story should make the reader desperate to know her experience of these twelve years. The protagonist's amnesia should never be explained. The ending should be the tribunal's ruling, which resolves the legal question while resolving nothing human.
