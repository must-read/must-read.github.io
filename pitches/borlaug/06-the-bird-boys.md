## Premise
Four illiterate teenage boys are hired to chase birds away from an experimental wheat field in the Yaqui Valley, 1946. The American scientist who runs the station begins teaching them to make "wheat marriages" -- cross-pollinating plants by removing the male parts from one flower and introducing pollen from another. The story is told from the perspective of the quietest boy, who discovers he has a gift: his hands are faster and more precise than the scientist's. Over three years, he invents a technique the scientist never imagined, becomes indispensable to a program he cannot read about in any book, and watches his friends leave one by one for other lives. He stays. The story of a vocation that arrives before the vocabulary for it exists.

## Source Inspiration
The novel's portrait of Reyes Vega and the Bird Boys -- local farm teenagers hired to scare birds from experimental plots, who became the program's most skilled technicians. Vega was "twice as fast as Borlaug" at emasculation, invented the scissors technique for glume removal, and eventually became a "much prized Master Technician." The novel notes that Harold "demanded that once they achieved Master Technician status, they receive compensation equal to college graduates." Chapter 5 ("The Marriages") shows this world: Arturo Hernandez progresses from Bird Boy to University of Minnesota Ph.D., while Vega's mastery is entirely embodied -- "the knowledge of the body passed from one body to another in the space where words would have been."

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Jones)**: His ability to write about people whose intelligence and capability exist entirely outside institutional recognition. Jones's characters in *The Known World* possess knowledge that the systems around them refuse to see. His narrative patience -- the willingness to follow a character for decades, noting what accumulates. His understanding that mastery and literacy are unrelated.
- **AuthorB (Johnson)**: The hallucinatory clarity of Johnson's prose -- the way his characters see the world with preternatural sharpness precisely because they exist outside conventional frameworks. The Bird Boys' daily reality (heat, birds, wheat, the American's incomprehensible ambitions) should have the quality of Johnson's vignettes: vivid, immediate, slightly askew.
- **WorkX (The Known World)**: Jones's novel about a world whose internal logic is complete and coherent even when the larger world refuses to acknowledge it. The Bird Boys' wheat-crossing operation is this: a complete world of skill, hierarchy, humor, and purpose that exists in a field in Sonora, invisible to the institutions that fund it.
- **WorkY (Jesus' Son)**: Johnson's interconnected stories about a narrator who drifts through experiences he barely understands, finding moments of accidental grace. Vega's perspective should have this quality: he does not understand the science, does not know what wheat rust is, cannot read the ledger -- but his hands know exactly what to do, and that knowledge is a form of grace he cannot name.

## Notes
Vega's perspective, not Harold's. The scientist should be seen from outside -- a tall, sunburned man who speaks broken Spanish and sits on a stool all day doing something the boys initially find absurd. The "wheat marriages" metaphor (crosses explained as marriages between cows and bulls) should arrive organically through the boys' conversation, not through Harold's instruction. Vega's innovation (the scissors technique) should be rendered as a physical discovery, not a eureka moment -- he tries something different one morning because his hands suggest it. The departure of the other boys should accumulate gradually: one gets married, one follows a brother north, one simply stops coming. Vega stays because the work has become the shape of his days, and he does not know how to want a different shape. Target 5,000-6,500 words.
