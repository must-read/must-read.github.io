## Premise
In a near-future where a new race of stem rust (Ug99's successor) has jumped from East Africa to the Indian subcontinent and is moving west, a wheat breeder at an international research station in Mexico races to develop resistant varieties using the same shuttle breeding techniques pioneered seventy years earlier. The rust is faster than any previous strain -- "bright-orange clouds above the fields" that kill a harvest in weeks. The story follows three planting cycles (eighteen months) as the breeder and her team of local field technicians perform six thousand crosses per season, working on stools in the heat, removing anthers with tweezers, while the pathogen evolves faster than they can breed against it. The world outside the field is collapsing; inside the field, the work is the same as it was in 1945.

## Source Inspiration
The novel's rendering of stem rust as a living antagonist: "bright-orange, 'rusty' pustules" that produce "huge orange clouds above the fields" and the arms race between breeder and pathogen that structured Harold's entire career. Race 15B emerged in 1954 and threatened to destroy all his resistant lines. The research material describes rust's biology in visceral terms -- a parasite that feeds on wheat nutrients, causes stems to break, grain to shrivel. The novel's deeper insight: the breeding work is never finished. Every resistant variety is temporary. The pathogen shifts. The breeder starts again. This is the structure of a horror story told as agricultural realism.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (McCarthy)**: The post-apocalyptic landscape rendered in biblical cadences. McCarthy's ability to make physical survival feel elemental -- the father and son pushing a cart through ash, here a breeder and her team bent over wheat in a world running out of bread. The prose stripped to its bones: subject, verb, object, dust.
- **AuthorB (Powers)**: His ability to write about non-human life (trees, microorganisms, ecosystems) as protagonists with agency and intelligence. The rust is not a villain; it is doing what life does -- adapting, proliferating, surviving. Powers's systems thinking, where individual human effort exists within webs of biological and economic interdependence.
- **WorkX (The Road)**: The structure of a journey through devastation toward uncertain hope. The parent-child dynamic transposed to mentor-apprentice: the breeder teaching a young technician the hand-crossing technique, passing on knowledge against the possibility that this generation's work will also fail. The question The Road poses -- is it worth continuing? -- is the question the breeder faces every morning on the stool.
- **WorkY (The Overstory)**: Powers's argument that human timescales are inadequate for understanding biological systems. The wheat-rust arms race operates on evolutionary time; the breeder's eighteen months are a single breath in a conversation between plant and pathogen that has lasted millennia. The Overstory's structural ambition -- multiple timelines, multiple species, one interconnected system.

## Notes
Near-future, not far-future. The world is recognizable -- supply chains strained, bread prices tripling, governments rationing grain -- not post-apocalyptic in the Hollywood sense. The technology has advanced (gene sequencing, marker-assisted selection) but the fundamental work remains manual: a person on a stool, tweezers in hand, removing anthers one by one. The contrast between genomic data on a laptop screen and the physical act of emasculating a wheat flower should be the story's central image. The breeder should fail -- the rust wins this round -- but the seeds she has crossed are planted at the second station, eight thousand feet higher, where the next generation will grow. The story ends with a planting, not a harvest. Target 6,000-8,000 words.
