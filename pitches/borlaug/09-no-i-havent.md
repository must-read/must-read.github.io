## Premise
October 20, 1970. A woman drives for over an hour through the Mexican highlands to find her husband in a wheat field and tell him he has won the Nobel Peace Prize. He is recording data on test plots alongside scientists from Romania, Brazil, and Mexico. When she tells him, he says: "No, I haven't." He believes it is a hoax. She convinces him it is real. He says he cannot leave because there is still work to do. She drives home. He returns to recording data. The story covers twelve hours -- from the 4 AM phone call to the evening -- and in those hours a marriage, a career, and the nature of devotion are rendered through what two people do and do not say to each other.

## Source Inspiration
Chapter 12 ("The Morning") -- the novel's final chapter and its most compressed. The Nobel Prize phone call, Ruth's drive to Toluca, Harold's disbelief, his insistence on continuing work. The novel renders this as Harold's day, but the emotional weight falls on Ruth: she is the one who receives the call, drives through the mountains, delivers the news, and returns home alone. Harold's response -- "No, I haven't" -- is the novel's most revealing sentence. Not false modesty. Genuine inability to process recognition for work he experiences as unfinished. The man cannot be interrupted.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Ishiguro)**: The unreliable self-assessment of Stevens in *Remains of the Day* -- a man who has organized his entire life around service and cannot recognize what that organization has cost him. Harold's devotion to wheat is Stevens's devotion to Darlington Hall: total, sincere, and the source of both his greatness and his blindness. Ishiguro's prose, which maintains emotional restraint until the restraint itself becomes unbearable.
- **AuthorB (Trevor)**: The master of the Irish story in which decades of silence produce a single, devastating moment of clarity. Trevor's ability to compress a lifetime into a gesture, a sentence, a drive through the countryside. His understanding that the most important conversations in a marriage are the ones that never happen.
- **WorkX (The Remains of the Day)**: Stevens's journey to visit Miss Kenton -- a belated recognition of what has been lost. Ruth's drive to the Toluca field is the mirror image: she is not visiting to reclaim what was lost but to deliver proof that his choice was justified, even as the delivery confirms that the choice was made at her expense. The novel's final scene at the pier, where Stevens watches the lights come on, has the quality this story should achieve.
- **WorkY (The Story of Lucy Gault)**: Trevor's novel about the consequences of a single misunderstanding that ripples through decades. Harold and Ruth's marriage is not built on a misunderstanding -- they understand each other perfectly. That is what makes it heartbreaking. The story of Lucy Gault is about what happens when people who love each other cannot find a way to occupy the same life. Harold and Ruth have found a way, but the way requires Ruth to drive an hour to deliver the world's highest honor and then drive home alone.

## Notes
Dual perspective: alternating between Ruth in the car and Harold in the field, converging at the moment of the announcement and then diverging again. Ruth's drive should be rendered in full landscape detail -- the mountains, the altitude, the light changing -- because the drive is the last hour of her life before everything changes (and nothing changes). Harold's morning in the field should be rendered in the same obsessive technical detail as the stool chapters: the crosses, the measurements, the ledger. The Nobel Prize should feel like an intrusion into a story that is already complete without it. The reader should feel, as Harold does, that the work is more real than the prize. But the reader should also feel, as Ruth does, that something has been lost that the prize cannot replace. Target 5,000-7,000 words.
