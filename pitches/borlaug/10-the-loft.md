## Premise
Two Americans -- a wheat scientist and a combat veteran from Montana -- arrive at a ruined agricultural station in the Sonoran Desert in 1944 and begin rebuilding it. They sleep in a loft with rats and mosquitoes. They have no equipment, no labor, no local support, and no Spanish. The station's previous three wheat harvests were destroyed by rust. The story follows their first season: clearing the station, borrowing a tractor rebuilt from three junked vehicles by local mechanics, planting twenty-three wheat varieties, hiring teenage boys to chase birds, and learning to survive the heat, the isolation, and each other. It is a frontier story -- two men building something from nothing in hostile country -- told without heroism, in the language of logistics and endurance.

## Source Inspiration
Chapter 1 ("The Loft") and the research material's account of Borlaug and Joe Rupert's arrival: the station was "in shambles," they "slept in the loft of a run-down state of Sonora research building, sharing it with rats, mice, mosquitoes and weevil-infested grain." No new farm equipment was available due to WWII. Vicente and Jose Guerrero, local mechanics, built one usable tractor from three discarded machines and a combine from scratch. The novel renders this as Harold and Rupert sweeping, organizing, and securing equipment -- the unglamorous work of beginning. Rupert is a taciturn former Pacific veteran who "moved with a deliberateness that made everything he did seem considered."

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (McMurtry)**: The great novelist of men in harsh landscapes, doing practical work, communicating through action rather than speech. McMurtry's ability to make a cattle drive (or a station renovation) feel epic through accumulation of specific, physical detail rather than rhetorical inflation. Gus and Call's partnership -- one talker, one silent worker -- maps onto any paired male friendship defined by shared labor.
- **AuthorB (Haruf)**: His spare, unsentimental Colorado prose -- sentences that land like fence posts. Haruf's farmers and ranchers who do difficult things without comment, whose inner lives are visible only in the quality of their attention to animals, weather, and soil. The station's renovation should have Haruf's quality: no decoration, no complaint, just the next task.
- **WorkX (Lonesome Dove)**: The journey into hostile territory, the partnership between complementary men, the daily reality of frontier work (which is mostly logistics, repair, and endurance rather than violence). The novel's insistence that the West was built by competence, not by heroism. Harold and Rupert's station is McMurtry's ranch: a place where survival requires specific knowledge applied consistently.
- **WorkY (Plainsong)**: Haruf's novel about people who help each other without ceremony -- the McPheron brothers taking in a pregnant teenager, not from kindness but from a sense of what is obviously required. Harold and Rupert's relationship has this quality: two men sharing a loft, dividing labor, eating together, not because they are friends but because the work requires two people and they are the two people who showed up.

## Notes
The friendship should be rendered entirely through action: who fixes the tractor, who cooks, who checks the wheat at dawn, who writes the supply list. Rupert's combat history should appear only in physical habits -- the way he clears a room, checks sight lines, sleeps light. Harold's scientific ambition should appear only in the twenty-three labeled wheat varieties and the ledger he writes in every night. The Sonoran landscape should be a third character: heat, dust, mesquite, the quality of light at different hours. The local community's gradual acceptance -- Cathy Jones offering rides, Aureliano Campoy loaning equipment on Sundays -- should arrive without sentiment, as practical transactions between people who recognize competence. Target 5,000-7,000 words.
