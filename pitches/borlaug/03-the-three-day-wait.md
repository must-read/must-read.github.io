## Premise
A scientist working in a remote Mexican agricultural station receives a telegram: his wife has given birth to a son with a severe spinal condition. No flights are available for three days. The story occupies those three days -- seventy-two hours during which he continues to work in the wheat field, sleeps in a loft with rats, writes letters he does not send, and waits for a plane that will carry him to a child he already knows he will lose. He does not break down. He does not stop working. The story's question is not what he feels but where the feeling goes when a man like this refuses to let it surface.

## Source Inspiration
Chapter 3 of the novel ("The Telegram"): Harold receives word that his son Scotty has been born with spina bifida. For three days he waits for a flight back to Wilmington. The novel renders this as physical experience -- pressure in the chest, throat tightness -- while Harold's body "continued to work and the work continued." The devastating precision of that sentence. The chapter's refusal to enter Harold's grief directly, instead showing it as a disturbance in the rhythm of his hands. Ruth's later insistence that he return to Mexico ("the work is there") is one of the novel's most complex moments -- is it generosity, self-protection, or both?

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Cusk)**: Her method of revelation through surface -- the way characters in the Outline trilogy disclose themselves through what they describe rather than what they confess. The interviewer's stillness that forces others' stories into the space. Here, Harold's refusal to speak about his son forces the grief into the physical world: how he holds the tweezers, whether the stool feels different, the particular quality of silence in the loft at night.
- **AuthorB (Petterson)**: The Scandinavian restraint, the way emotion in Petterson's work arrives as landscape -- cold water, the smell of hay, a horse's breathing. His understanding that some men process catastrophe through their bodies, not their minds. The slow, patient accumulation of detail that constitutes a kind of testimony.
- **WorkX (A Grief Observed)**: Lewis's raw notebook entries after his wife's death -- the discovery that grief is not what he expected, that it resembles fear more than sadness. The intellectual man's attempt to think his way through loss, and the failure of that attempt. Harold is not Lewis (he does not write about his grief) but the structure of anticipated loss -- grieving before the death -- applies.
- **WorkY (Out Stealing Horses)**: Petterson's novel about a man revisiting, decades later, a summer when everything changed. The way memory in that novel is stored in physical sensation -- the feel of a log, the temperature of river water. Harold's three days should feel stored in the body: the telegram paper between his fingers, the particular heat of that week, the sound of the plane engine when it finally arrives.

## Notes
Three sections, one per day, but the days should blur rather than progress. No resolution: the story ends when he boards the plane, not when he arrives. The baby should never appear on the page. Ruth's voice should appear only in the letter Harold composes in his head but does not write. The wheat work should be rendered in full technical detail -- the anthers, the bags, the ledger -- because the precision of that work is how Harold keeps the three days from collapsing. If the work detail wavers, the reader should feel the character's control slipping. Target 5,000-7,000 words.
