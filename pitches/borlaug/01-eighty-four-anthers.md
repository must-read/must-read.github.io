## Premise
A plant breeder at a remote agricultural station in northern Mexico spends a decade on a stool in a wheat field, removing anthers from flowers with tweezers -- eighty-four per head, thousands per day. His infant son has died. His wife lives in another city. His assistant, a local teenager who cannot read, becomes faster at the work than he is. The story covers one ordinary workday in full, from the kerosene stove at dawn to the ledger entries at night, and in those hours the entire shape of a life -- its silences, its substitutions, its strange sufficiency -- becomes visible.

## Source Inspiration
The novel's central image: a man on an eighteen-inch pine stool, bent over wheat plants in the Sonoran heat, performing a task of extraordinary tedium with extraordinary precision. The character Harold uses the repetitive physical labor as a container for grief he cannot articulate -- the death of his son Scotty, the distance from his wife Ruth, the intimacy he can only achieve with plants. The stool chapters (especially Chapter 2: "The Stool" and Chapter 5: "The Marriages") present work not as metaphor for devotion but as the thing itself -- the way some people can only be present through their hands.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Knausgaard)**: The relentless accumulation of physical detail as a method of emotional excavation. Knausgaard's ability to make the mundane feel like confession -- to find in the act of washing dishes or changing a diaper the same weight as a death. The forward-moving comma-rich sentences that refuse to editorialize.
- **AuthorB (Robinson)**: Her theological attention to ordinary labor, the way work in Robinson's world is a form of prayer that the worker does not recognize as prayer. Her capacity to hold grief and gratitude in the same sentence without resolving the tension. The quiet radiance of Gilead's prose.
- **WorkX (Stoner)**: Williams's novel about a man whose life appears unremarkable from the outside but contains, in its accumulated daily choices, a complete moral architecture. Stoner's relationship with his work -- the way teaching becomes his only reliable form of connection -- mirrors Harold's relationship with wheat.
- **WorkY (Gilead)**: The epistolary patience, the sense of a man reviewing his life from inside the life rather than above it. Ames's awareness that the ordinary world is luminous, paired with his inability to convey that luminosity to the people closest to him.

## Notes
Single day, single location. The story's architecture should be radically simple -- morning, work, midday rest, afternoon work, evening -- but within that frame, the accumulation of sensory detail (heat, insects, the particular resistance of a wheat anther, the sound of kraft paper bags in wind) should achieve the Knausgaard effect where duration becomes meaning. The assistant Vega should be present but never psychologically penetrated -- Harold watches his hands but never asks about his life. The dead son should appear only as physical sensation: a weight in the chest, a particular quality of stillness when Harold stops working. No flashbacks. No epiphany. The day ends and another one begins. Target 6,000-8,000 words.
