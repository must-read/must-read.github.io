## Premise
Two brothers, Vicente and Jose Guerrero, are mechanics at a junkyard near Mexico City in the 1940s. A government agricultural program hires them to build farm equipment from scrap. They construct one usable tractor from three discarded machines. Then they build a combine harvester from scratch -- a machine neither of them has ever seen intact, working from a photograph and a parts manual in English they cannot read. The story follows the construction of the combine over six months, told through the brothers' perspective: their arguments about engineering, their wives' opinions of the American scientists, the economics of scrap metal, and the slow emergence of a working machine from a pile of rust. A story about intelligence that has no credentials, expertise that has no name.

## Source Inspiration
A single detail from the novel's research material: "one usable tractor was constructed from three discarded machines; a combine was built from scratch" by Vicente Guerrero and his brother Jose, described only as Mexican mechanics who rebuilt junked vehicles near Mexico City. The novel gives them one sentence. They built a combine harvester from nothing -- a machine of extraordinary mechanical complexity -- and the historical record preserves their first names and nothing else. This pitch recovers them.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Bolano)**: His ability to find in Mexico's margins -- its junkyards, its border towns, its unrecorded lives -- stories of genius and violence that official history ignores. Bolano's Mexico is a place where brilliance exists without recognition, where the most important work happens in the spaces between institutions. The brothers' junkyard is a Bolano location: a place of improbable creation.
- **AuthorB (Alexievich)**: Her method of recovering history through the voices of ordinary people -- the welders, the mechanics, the cleanup crews who built and maintained the Soviet system. Alexievich's interview subjects speak about their work with a combination of pride, bewilderment, and matter-of-fact precision that the brothers should share. Their expertise is real and untheorized.
- **WorkX (2666)**: Bolano's novel contains, in its "Part About the Crimes" section, hundreds of pages about lives that the official record reduced to statistics. The Guerrero brothers are reduced to a dependent clause. This story performs the Bolano operation: expanding the footnote into a world.
- **WorkY (Secondhand Time)**: Alexievich's oral history of post-Soviet Russia, in which mechanics, engineers, and factory workers describe building things under impossible conditions -- no parts, no manuals, no support. The brothers' combine is a Soviet-scale improvisation performed in a Mexican junkyard with American scrap. The quality of intelligence required is the same.

## Notes
The combine should be rendered in physical detail: what parts they need, what they substitute, how they solve problems of torque, clearance, alignment. The brothers should argue about engineering in the way brothers argue -- personally, competitively, with reference to childhood grievances. Their wives should appear as people with opinions about the project (one supportive, one skeptical about unpaid labor for a foreign program). The American scientists should be peripheral -- Harold appears once, inspects the combine, says something brief and technical. The photograph of an intact combine that the brothers work from should be a recurring image: pinned to the workshop wall, grease-stained, consulted like a scripture. The combine's first successful pass through a wheat field should be the story's climax, but the story should not end there -- it should end with the brothers back in the junkyard, already working on the next thing. Target 4,500-6,000 words.
