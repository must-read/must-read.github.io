## Premise
A woman married to an agricultural scientist in 1950s Mexico City spends a Sunday with her two children while her husband is home -- one of perhaps eight Sundays per year when he is not at one of his two field stations. She cooks, markets, reads to her son, watches her husband sit in a chair and fail to be idle. She understands that he is thinking about wheat two hundred kilometers away. She understands that she chose this, and that her choice was rational, and that rationality does not prevent the Sunday from feeling like a performance of family life in which everyone knows their part but nobody is fully present. By evening, her daughter asks: "Papa, will you be here next Sunday too?" He says he will try. She makes tea.

## Source Inspiration
Chapter 6 ("Sunday") is the novel's most intimate and most devastating chapter. Harold cooks stew, goes to the market with Ruth, holds sleeping Bill, reads correspondence about rust strains while his daughter discusses monarch butterflies. The chapter's genius is in the gap between the domestic surface and the psychological undertow: "his hands were on his knees...the shape of it wrong, the balance off." Ruth reads in her chair. Harold reads about wheat. They sit in separate circles of lamplight. The final image -- "comfortable silence" that might be comfort and might be distance -- refuses to resolve.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Munro)**: The master of the domestic story that contains, without announcing, enormous emotional violence. Munro's women see their situations with terrifying clarity and choose to remain in them, not from weakness but from a calculus the story respects without endorsing. Ruth's "calculation" -- she would rather have this partial version of Harold than not have him at all -- is a Munro sentence.
- **AuthorB (Fitzgerald)**: Her ability to write about marriages in which both parties are intelligent, decent, and fundamentally unable to give each other what they need. Fitzgerald's comedy is not satirical but structural -- the comedy of two good people whose needs are architecturally incompatible. The Sunday should have Fitzgerald's quality of gentle, precise absurdity.
- **WorkX (Too Much Happiness)**: Munro's story about Sophia Kovalevsky -- a mathematician whose genius and personal life exist in irreconcilable tension. The title's irony: too much happiness as a problem, an excess that destabilizes. Ruth's Sunday is too much Harold -- his rare presence overwhelming a routine she has carefully built without him.
- **WorkY (The Gate of Angels)**: Fitzgerald's novel about a physicist whose commitment to empirical truth makes him unable to recognize love when it arrives. Harold's commitment to his wheat work has the same quality: it is not that he does not love Ruth, but that his instrument for measuring love has been calibrated to detect other things. The gate of angels is the threshold between intellectual and emotional knowledge that some people never cross.

## Notes
Ruth's perspective throughout. Harold should be seen entirely from outside -- his body language, his restlessness, his visible relief when he finds the Stakman letter about rust strains. The children should be vivid and specific (Bill's mess with the stew, Jeanie's monarch butterfly lecture) because Ruth sees them with the attention Harold gives to wheat. The market scene should be rendered in full sensory detail -- cilantro, chilies, raw meat -- because these are the textures of Ruth's actual life, the one she inhabits while Harold inhabits his. The ending should resist judgment: is their marriage a tragedy, a compromise, or a form of love the story is not equipped to name? Target 4,000-6,000 words.
