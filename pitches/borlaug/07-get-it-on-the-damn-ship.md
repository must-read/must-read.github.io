## Premise
August 1965. Four hundred and fifty tons of Mexican wheat seed must reach India and Pakistan before the planting window closes. A thirty-truck convoy crosses northern Mexico. Mexican customs impound the shipment. The Watts riots close the Los Angeles freeway. A Pakistani treasury check for $100,000 is refused by a Mexican bank because three words are misspelled. Indian state monopolies lobby to block the import. The story follows one week in the life of the logistics coordinator -- a woman at the Rockefeller Foundation's Mexico City office -- as she makes phone calls, falsifies documents, bribes a customs official, and screams at a shipping agent, knowing that if the seed does not arrive in time, planting will be delayed a full year and people in the Punjab will die. She has never been to India. She will never go. Her war is fought on telephone lines and in bank lobbies.

## Source Inspiration
The 1965 seed shipment crisis is the novel's most kinetic sequence (Chapters 10-11). The research material catalogs the cascading absurdities: customs delays at Guaymas, the thirty-truck convoy, the Watts riots, the misspelled check, over-fumigation killing half the seeds, the India-Pakistan war erupting during planting. Harold's fury -- "Get that wheat aboard the ship and send it on its damn way!" -- is the novel's only moment of uncontrolled anger. The novel tells this from Harold's perspective, in the field. This pitch inverts it: the story of the person in the office, on the phone, fighting the bureaucracy that stands between seeds and soil.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Didion)**: Her journalist's eye for the moment when systems fail -- the specific, concrete detail that reveals institutional breakdown. Didion's ability to maintain a cold, precise narrative voice while describing events that are emotionally catastrophic. The woman in the office should have Didion's quality of seeing clearly while the world around her becomes irrational.
- **AuthorB (le Carre)**: The bureaucratic thriller -- the understanding that real power operates through memos, phone calls, and approvals rather than through action. Le Carre's appreciation for the moral complexity of people who do necessary work through morally ambiguous means. The logistics coordinator who falsifies a customs form to save lives is a le Carre character.
- **WorkX (The Year of Magical Thinking)**: Didion's memoir about the gap between what is happening and what the mind can process. The logistics coordinator knows, abstractly, that her phone calls determine whether millions eat. She cannot hold that knowledge and also negotiate a shipping rate. The magical thinking here is the bureaucratic kind: if I file this form correctly, the world will be fed.
- **WorkY (The Constant Gardener)**: Le Carre's novel about the intersection of international aid, corporate interests, and individual moral choices. The seed shipment exists at exactly this intersection: Rockefeller Foundation money, Mexican government approval, American shipping companies, Indian and Pakistani politics. The logistics coordinator is le Carre's gardener -- someone who tends a small plot within a vast system she can see but not control.

## Notes
The protagonist is invented -- the novel does not name the logistics coordinator, though the research implies multiple people at the Foundation's Mexico City office handled operations. Making her the center inverts the novel's focus from field to office, from patience to urgency. The week should feel like a thriller that never leaves a desk: phone calls, cables, calculations, a map pinned to the wall with the convoy's route marked in red. The Watts riots should arrive as a radio bulletin, not as spectacle -- heard from three thousand miles away, relevant only because a freeway is closed. The misspelled check should be the story's most devastating moment: three words, $100,000, a planting season, possibly a famine. Target 5,000-7,000 words.
