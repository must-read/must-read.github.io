## Premise
A plant scientist who has spent a decade suppressing his anger -- against his director, against institutional bureaucracy, against the distance between his results and the permission to use them -- walks out of a committee meeting in Mexico City. His colleague, a combat veteran, stands and walks out with him. The story is not about the meeting. It is about the decade of suppression that preceded it, and the three days that follow it, during which the scientist drives back to his field station, resumes his work, and discovers that the anger has not dissipated but merely relocated -- from the committee room to his hands, his stool, his wheat. A story about what happens to a man's body when he finally acts on what he has been feeling for ten years.

## Source Inspiration
Chapter 9 ("The Walk-Out") and the research material's account of the pivotal meeting: Harold presents yield data showing his dwarf varieties outperform traditional wheat by twenty to forty percent. The committee recommends two more years of regional trials. Harold absorbs this, then acts: he and Vega load seed into a truck and distribute it to farmers without authorization. Harold's declaration -- "if I'm not permitted to do that, I'll leave" -- and Rupert's silent solidarity (standing and walking out with him) is the novel's most dramatic interpersonal moment. But the novel's deeper insight is that Harold's anger does not resolve with the walk-out; it simply changes form.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **AuthorA (Baldwin)**: The essay as an instrument of contained fury. Baldwin's ability to write about systemic obstruction -- the way institutions absorb and neutralize individual conviction -- with prose that is simultaneously controlled and incandescent. "Notes of a Native Son" renders a decade of suppressed rage through the lens of a single funeral; this story renders a decade of suppressed rage through the lens of a single meeting.
- **AuthorB (Matar)**: His memoir *The Return* is about going back to a place after decades of exile and discovering that the grief you carried has become structural -- part of your posture, your breathing, your relationship with rooms. Harold's anger has this quality: it is not an emotion he experiences but a condition he inhabits. Matar's prose, which moves between political analysis and bodily sensation without marking the transition.
- **WorkX (Notes of a Native Son)**: Baldwin's essay about his father's death, the Harlem riots, and the discovery that hatred and love can occupy the same body simultaneously. Harold's relationship with Harrar -- respect for his authority, fury at his caution -- has this structure. The committee meeting is Harold's Harlem: the place where the contradiction becomes unsustainable.
- **WorkY (The Return)**: Matar's journey back to Libya to learn what happened to his imprisoned father. Harold's drive back to the station after the walk-out is a return to the only place where his competence is legible. The field is his Libya: the place where what he knows is written in the ground, regardless of what committees decide.

## Notes
The meeting should occupy one-quarter of the story. The remaining three-quarters should be the aftermath: the drive, the arrival at the station, the loading of seed, the distribution to farmers. The story's emotional center is not the dramatic gesture (the walk-out) but the quiet, unauthorized action that follows (giving farmers seed without permission). Harold's anger should be rendered physically -- jaw tension, grip pressure, the particular force with which he drives the truck -- rather than psychologically. Rupert's walk-out should be the story's most moving moment: a man who says nothing, standing and following. The farmers who receive the seed should be briefly, specifically rendered: names, faces, the size of their fields. These are real people who will eat or not eat based on what Harold puts in their hands. Target 5,000-6,500 words.
