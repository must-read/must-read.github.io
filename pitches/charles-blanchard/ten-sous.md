idea: "A widow in mid-nineteenth-century rural France sends her young son to beg at funerals of wealthy families. He is instructed to kneel respectfully through the service and accept the coins distributed to poor attendees afterward. His first funeral yields ten sous, which he guards in his fist for the entire walk home. The story follows three funerals across one autumn, each a different wealthy household, each with its own economy of grief and charity. The boy observes the other beggars — a separate, silent group who move cautiously through ceremonies and depart with modest coins, ashamed afterward to have 'mingled with society's most desperate inhabitants.' He begins to understand the transaction: the rich need the poor at their funerals, not out of kindness but because charity at death is currency spent on the afterlife."
notes: "Philippe's Supplement describes the specific practice of poor families attending wealthy funerals for the alms distributed afterward. The passage captures something precise about the economics of visible poverty: it is useful to the wealthy only at certain ceremonial moments. Edward P. Jones's quiet, devastating stories of Black Washington — where poverty has its own dignity and social architecture — meets Mantel's present-tense historical immersion. WorkX gives the structure: linked vignettes that accumulate into a portrait of a community's hidden economy. WorkY gives the theme: the way power operates through ceremony, the way death and money and social performance interlock."
