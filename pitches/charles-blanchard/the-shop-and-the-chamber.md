idea: "A clog-maker's house contains two worlds. The shop: masculine, furious, filled with specialized tools and the noise of steam hammers and shaped wood. The chamber: feminine, ordered, maintained by the clog-maker's wife Rose with quiet domestic authority. Between these worlds stands a twelve-year-old boy who has been delivered by his desperate mother — a child so damaged by poverty that his silence seems alive, 'like a monstrous beast watching everything around him.' Rose fears and resents the boy. She sees something in his enormous blue eyes that she cannot name. He prefers her dark chamber to the shop's noise and light, and this preference terrifies her. The story follows the household over one season as the boy's presence destabilizes the careful balance between shop and chamber, between husband and wife, between the productive and the domestic — until something must give."
notes: "Philippe describes the clog-maker's house as two distinct spaces with different laws, and Rose's reaction to Charles as genuine fear — not of what he might do, but of what he is. His silence has a living quality. His presence disrupts the household's equilibrium. Bronte's gothic domestic architecture — the madwoman in the attic, the house divided into zones of permission and prohibition — meets O'Connor's Southern Gothic tradition of grotesque figures whose mere existence forces a moral crisis. WorkX gives the structure: a child navigating a house with forbidden rooms, a domestic power struggle that is also a spiritual one. WorkY gives the violence of grace: the moment when ordinary life is interrupted by a figure who refuses to behave according to the household's rules, and the family must confront what they have been avoiding."
