## Premise
At a guild meeting on a glassmaking island, a young reformer proposes allowing artisans to sell their work on the mainland — breaking the monopoly that has protected the craft for centuries but now threatens its survival as competitors in rival cities capture market share. The old guard, backed by the state's intelligence apparatus, votes the reform down 29 to 11. But the story is told through the eyes of a mid-ranking master who voted with the majority — not because he disagrees with the reformer, but because the last craftsman who defied the guild's protectors died in a furnace explosion. The vote is not about glass. It is about what the state will do to those who carry its secrets elsewhere.

## Source Inspiration
Chapter 7 of *The Feast of Ashes*, "Guild and Ghetto" — the Palazzo da Mula guild meeting where Trevisan challenges the mainland trade restriction and Contarini casually invokes the Council of Ten's agents. The scene operates on two levels: a procedural debate about trade policy, and a veiled death threat delivered through institutional language. The detail that Bardolino's vote is synchronized with six others indicates coordinated control. Trevisan loses but demonstrates "the posture of patience, not defeat." The entire scene reads as a miniature political thriller set inside the machinery of a craft guild — where the carved lion holds a glass blowpipe instead of a sword, but the threat is identical.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Hilary Mantel (style)**: Mantel's present-tense immersion in institutional politics — the way her Cromwell reads a council meeting as a battlefield of positioned bodies, timed gestures, and strategic silences — is exactly the lens for a guild vote that is really a demonstration of state power. Her prose makes bureaucratic process feel lethal.
- **John le Carre (style)**: Le Carre's morally exhausted institutional realism — where the spy's enemy is not the opposition but his own service — gives the story its emotional temperature. The master who votes against his own interest because the institution demands it is a le Carre protagonist: loyal, compromised, and aware of both.
- **Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy (structure)**: Le Carre's masterpiece of institutional betrayal, where the mole is inside the system and the investigation requires reading loyalty itself as a form of deception, provides the structural model. The guild meeting is not what it appears; every vote is a declaration of allegiance to power, not craft.
- **The Leopard (themes)**: Lampedusa's novel about a Sicilian prince watching his world change — understanding that "everything must change so that everything can stay the same" — captures the guild's paradox. The monopoly that once protected artisans now traps them. The reformer who loses the vote is the future; the master who votes with the majority is the present, already obsolete but too entangled to move.

## Notes
The short story's constraint is that the protagonist never speaks during the meeting. His entire drama is interior: reading the room, timing his raised hand, understanding what each gesture means. The mariegola (guild book) on its lectern, the bronze bell marking procedural transitions, the terrazzo floor reflecting Murano glass windows — the setting is rich enough for a story that never leaves one room. The apprentice with the yellow badge who sits silently at the back, holding the master's papers, gives the scene its moral witness: someone who has no vote, no voice, and no protection, observing the machinery of power from the outside.
