## Premise
A Jewish apprentice lens-grinder and the Christian daughter of a rival workshop master carry on a secret relationship in the narrow counting room where she keeps the ledgers. She understands supply chains well enough to read the conspiracy in the delivery records. He wears a yellow badge that marks him as Other in every public space but not in this one. Their intimacy is conducted in the language of craft — glass beads, ink-stained fingers, the vocabulary of specification and flaw — and it carries the weight of everything they cannot say aloud in a city that would punish them both, though not equally.

## Source Inspiration
Enzo and Serena's relationship in *The Feast of Ashes* — conducted in stolen moments in a counting room that smells of ink, canal water, and furnace smoke. Serena is not a passive love interest but a bookkeeper who reads patterns in supply orders and understands the conspiracy without being told. Their physical contact carries enormous weight precisely because it's rare and forbidden. The detail of Serena crossing the lagoon from Murano to the Jewish quarter at night — risking her own safety for a final encounter — and leaving her father's glass-bead necklace (eighteen beads, one for each year of her life, each hand-blown by Bardolino) in Enzo's hand is devastating. One inheritance meeting another on the workbench.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Sarah Waters (style)**: Waters writes queer and transgressive desire in historical settings with Dickensian plotting and atmospheric detail. Her ability to render forbidden intimacy within the material reality of class and social constraint is exactly what this story needs — the counting room as both workspace and secret chamber.
- **Mary Renault (style)**: Renault's scholarly precision about the ancient world and her gift for rendering desire that cannot speak its own name openly gives the story its emotional register. Her prose combines classical restraint with emotional immediacy, perfect for lovers communicating through the grammar of craft.
- **The Age of Innocence (structure)**: Wharton's novel about desire constrained by social architecture — where every gesture is freighted because the lovers cannot act openly — provides the structural model. The counting room is their equivalent of Wharton's drawing rooms: a space governed by rules both characters understand completely.
- **Romeo and Juliet (themes)**: The star-crossed structure is earned here by actual legal and social barriers — not family feuds but religious law, guild regulation, and a city that marks one lover's body with a yellow badge. The necklace exchange inverts the balcony scene: she gives him her father's craft, knowing she may never see either again.

## Notes
The story should resist the temptation to make the romance the only plot. Serena's intelligence — her ability to read the conspiracy in the ledger numbers — makes her a participant in the resistance, not just Enzo's lover. The counting room works as a setting because it's simultaneously intimate and transactional: a space where numbers are kept, debts recorded, and truths visible to anyone who knows how to read the columns. The glass-bead necklace is the ideal ending image for short fiction — small, beautiful, handmade, and impossible to return.
