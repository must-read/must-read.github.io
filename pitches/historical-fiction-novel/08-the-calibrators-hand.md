## Premise
A young artisan has been trusted with calibrating the precision components of a state weapon because his hands are the steadiest in the workshop. Over five sessions spread across weeks, he rotates each component a fraction of a degree within its mount — alterations imperceptible to any inspection but cumulative enough to render the weapon useless at distance. Each sabotage act is disguised as routine calibration. The story follows one of those sessions in real time: the locked room, the brass mounts, the calculations, the exact angle of displacement, and the knowledge that his father attempted the same sabotage and was killed for it.

## Source Inspiration
Chapter 25 of *The Feast of Ashes*, "The Sabotage" — Enzo's systematic rotation of lenses within their mounts, exploiting the trust his calibration skill has earned. The detail is extraordinary: "rotating lens three by a fraction causing it to shift fourteen degrees, thirty-seven minutes rather than the specified forty minutes." Each individual offset falls within tolerance, but collectively they create cumulative angular error. At close range, the weapon works. At the four-hundred-pace distance needed for naval warfare, the beam scatters. His father Giacomo tried the same thing through surface grinding errors and was caught. Enzo's method is more sophisticated — he weaponizes precision itself, using his excellence against the people who valued it.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Patricia Highsmith (style)**: Highsmith's cold, clinical prose — the way she renders criminal acts with methodical detachment, making the reader complicit through intimacy with the perpetrator's process — is ideal for a story that unfolds as a step-by-step technical sabotage. The banality of the act (rotating a brass mount a fraction of a degree) masks its enormity.
- **Anthony Doerr (style)**: Doerr's luminous attention to the physics of light — the way *All the Light We Cannot See* renders radio waves and optics as objects of beauty — provides the counterweight to Highsmith's coldness. The lenses themselves are beautiful; the sabotage is an act committed against beauty in service of conscience.
- **Crime and Punishment (structure)**: Dostoevsky's novel about a man who commits a deliberate act and then must live with the psychological consequences provides the structural arc. But this story inverts it: the "crime" is sabotage that prevents killing, and the "punishment" is the knowledge that discovery means death — the same death his father suffered.
- **Oppenheimer / American Prometheus (themes)**: The parallel to Manhattan Project scientists who built the bomb and then wrestled with its use is direct. But Enzo is not Oppenheimer — he's the technician who sabotages the bomb. His moral position is cleaner and his danger more immediate. The question is not "should we have built it" but "can I make it fail without dying."

## Notes
The real-time constraint (one calibration session, perhaps ninety minutes) creates natural tension for short fiction. The locked room, the brass mounts, the specific angles — the setting is almost a laboratory. The technical precision of the sabotage is the story's aesthetic: a prose style that mirrors the exactness of the work being described. The inherited dimension — father and son using the same skills for the same resistance, with the father already dead for it — transforms a thriller premise into something more devastating. The story should include the moment when Fra Giordano tests the close-range prototype and it works perfectly, confirming that the sabotage is invisible at short distance. The saboteur watches the test succeed and says nothing.
