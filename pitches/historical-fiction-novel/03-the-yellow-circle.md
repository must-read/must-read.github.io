## Premise
In a Renaissance port city, a Jewish widow who washes linens for Christian households has kept a packed trunk beneath her bed since her husband's death. She knows why he died — not the official accident, but the real reason — and she has been preparing for the moment when the same powers that killed him come for her son. The story spans one evening: a Shabbat dinner where the bread is blessed, the third place is set for Elijah, and the mother must decide whether to tell her child the truth or let him keep grinding lenses in the morning.

## Source Inspiration
Mirela Sforno in *The Feast of Ashes* — a character who appears quietly in domestic scenes but whose silence is load-bearing. She washes linens until her hands crack. She sets a third place at the Shabbat table. She sold her wedding ring to fund her son's eventual escape. The packed trunk — ready since her husband's death — is an inheritance from centuries of Jewish displacement, a readiness for diaspora that is not paranoia but historical competence. When she finally speaks, the single word "Modena" contains an entire escape plan. Her grammatical shift from "you are staying" to "we are staying" is the story's emotional climax.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Toni Morrison (style)**: Morrison's incantatory prose and her gift for rendering the interior life of women whose suffering is structural, not incidental, is the right voice for Mirela. The way *Beloved* renders a mother's impossible choices — what to tell, what to withhold, what lengths to go to — maps directly onto a woman deciding how much truth her child can survive.
- **Pat Barker (style)**: Barker's spare, unsentimental treatment of trauma and working-class endurance provides the counterweight to Morrison's lyricism. Mirela's cracked hands, her silence, her practical preparations — these need Barker's refusal to sentimentalize suffering.
- **Beloved (structure)**: Morrison's novel about what a mother will do to protect her child from the system that claims ownership of their bodies provides the structural DNA. The trunk is Mirela's version of Sethe's desperate act — a container for the worst-case future, maintained with terrible love.
- **The Plot Against America (themes)**: Roth's novel about Jewish American life under a fascist hypothetical captures the specific dread of Mirela's situation — not a sudden catastrophe but a slow tightening, the whispered word "ghetto," new restrictions arriving as reasonable administrative measures while the ground shifts beneath a family's feet.

## Notes
The story should be anchored in the domestic and the sensory: the smell of bread, the weight of wet linens, the crack of winter skin, the candlelight on the Shabbat table. The trunk under the bed is the story's controlling image — always there, never opened on the page, its contents implied. The rabbi's line about the builder who does not own the building gives Mirela her philosophical context, but she doesn't need philosophy. She has already packed. A short story can do what the novel distributes across chapters: compress the entire weight of preparation-for-catastrophe into one evening's dinner.
