## Premise
A Franciscan friar who has devoted his life to optics — Alhazen's theories, Bacon's speculations about burning mirrors, the geometry of parabolic reflection — arrives at a glassmaking island to see his theoretical designs made real for the first time. The story follows one day: the friar watches a master craftsman test a short-range prototype that successfully concentrates sunlight to ignite wood. The theory works. What was abstract is now lethal. The friar must reconcile his reverence for knowledge — light as God's instrument, optics as divine geometry — with what his knowledge will burn.

## Source Inspiration
Fra Giordano da Padova in *The Feast of Ashes* — a gaunt, intense Franciscan whose optical designs drive the weapon project. He is not a villain but an intellectual consumed by the beauty of his own theory. He has spent years in correspondence with scholars, visiting furnaces, constructing prototypes. When he tests the lens array and it works — when concentrated light burns a mark on wood — he treats it with "reverent intensity." The novel's question about him is whether knowledge that can destroy ships is evil in the hands of a man who vowed poverty and sees geometry as divine. His story is about the fracture between theory and consequence, between the beauty of the mathematics and the burning of the timber.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Umberto Eco (style)**: Eco's medieval scholarly milieu, his comfort with Latin citations, theological argument, and monastic life rendered with sensory precision, is the natural voice for a friar-scientist navigating the intersection of faith and knowledge. Eco's narrators think in footnotes and disputations.
- **Ted Chiang (style)**: Chiang's crystalline, philosophical short fiction — where each story is a thought experiment rendered with precise emotional consequences — gives the story its intellectual architecture. The friar's realization is a Chiang moment: the instant when abstract knowledge becomes irreversibly concrete.
- **The Name of the Rose (structure)**: Eco's novel about forbidden knowledge in a monastery — where the pursuit of a dangerous book leads to murder — provides the structural parallel. The friar's optical manuscript is this story's forbidden text, and the weapon is what it produces when theory escapes the library.
- **Doctor Faustus (themes)**: Mann's novel about a composer who makes a devil's bargain for genius maps onto the friar's situation. His optical mastery is his art, and the weapon is the price. The Faustian question: does the beauty of the knowledge justify what it enables? The friar, who has taken vows of poverty, has nothing to sell but his intellect — and he has already sold it.

## Notes
The story's power is in the gap between the friar's two frames of understanding: in one, light is God's first creation, optics is the study of divine architecture, and a parabolic lens is a window into the Creator's geometry. In the other, that same lens focuses sunlight to burn a ship's rigging at four hundred paces. The short story should inhabit that gap — not resolve it, but live inside the friar's consciousness as he watches theory become fire. The Byzantine mosaic in the church (gold-backed glass tessera that create sacred images using the same optical principles as the weapon) is the ideal symbolic counterpoint.
