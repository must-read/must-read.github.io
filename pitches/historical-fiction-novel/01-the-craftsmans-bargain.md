## Premise
A master artisan in a Renaissance Italian city-state accepts a lucrative commission from the state that he gradually realizes is a weapon of war. The story traces the slow moral corrosion of a man who tells himself the work is theoretical, even as he watches the components come together under his own hands. By the time he understands what he has built, the apprentice he took on out of guilt — the son of the last craftsman who refused — has already seen everything.

## Source Inspiration
Bardolino's arc in *The Feast of Ashes* — a master glassmaker on Murano who accepts Fra Giordano's commission for optical weapon lenses. He rationalizes each step (the debts, the workers' families, the claim that it's just glass) while carrying a millefiori piece from his youth as a talisman of lost innocence. His backstory includes being punished decades earlier for refusing a similar commission, making his current capitulation feel inevitable. The detail that he took the dead man's son as apprentice — atonement and perpetuation in a single gesture — is the story's emotional engine.

## Why These Formula Elements
- **Hilary Mantel (style)**: Mantel's present-tense immersion and gift for rendering institutional power through a single consciousness is ideal for Bardolino's claustrophobic perspective. Her Cromwell novels show how a man of practical intelligence negotiates with power he cannot refuse.
- **Kazuo Ishiguro (style)**: Ishiguro's unreliable narrators who suppress their own complicity give the story its emotional mechanism. The craftsman will narrate around his guilt the way Stevens narrates around his wasted life.
- **The Master and Margarita (structure)**: Bulgakov's interleaving of the mundane and the diabolical — the way the Devil's visit to Moscow operates as both farce and moral reckoning — provides the structural tension between the workshop's daily rhythms and the weapon taking shape behind a locked door.
- **An Artist of the Floating World (themes)**: Ishiguro's novel about a painter reckoning with how his art served Japanese imperialism maps directly onto a craftsman reckoning with how his glass serves Venetian military ambition. Both are stories about the lies artisans tell themselves about the innocence of craft.

## Notes
The millefiori piece (a glass disc he made at nineteen, before any compromise) is the perfect concrete symbol for short fiction — small enough to hold in the hand, large enough to carry a life's worth of regret. The furnace itself works as a controlling metaphor: it must be fed constantly or it dies, like the compromises that sustain his livelihood. The story could end not with exposure or punishment but with the craftsman standing at the furnace mouth at dawn, understanding that the fire no longer belongs to him.
