Corridors That Refuse to Be Measured

A discussion between Robert Aickman and Carmen Maria Machado

February 22, 2026

The pub had low ceilings and those particular English wall sconces that cast more shadow than light, which felt appropriate given the company. Aickman had ordered a sherry without looking at anyone, Machado a mezcal that the bartender had to confirm they actually stocked. I was drinking water and regretting it already.

“The community,” Aickman said, as if resuming a sentence he’d begun hours ago. “You want the community to be the source of the wrongness. That’s the premise you’ve brought me.”

“It’s the premise all three of us are working with,” I said. “Jackson’s insight — the lottery, the stoning. Communal violence that everyone has agreed is normal.”

“Jackson’s insight was rather more precise than that.” He turned his sherry glass a quarter rotation. “It wasn’t that the violence was hidden. It was conducted in sunshine. The weather was clear and warm. She tells you that in the second sentence. The horror is not that the community has a dark secret. The horror is that the community has no secret at all.”

Machado leaned forward. “Right. And that’s where the body comes in.”

“I beg your pardon?”

“The body. The physical body of the person being destroyed. Jackson keeps it almost abstract — Tessie Hutchinson gets the slip of paper, and then the stones. You see the first one hit her temple. But Jackson’s brilliance is in what she doesn’t linger on. The community’s mechanics are the subject, not the flesh. I want to reverse that. I want the flesh.”

Aickman looked at her the way you’d look at someone who had proposed renovating your house while you were still living in it. Not hostile, exactly. Evaluative.

“You want the body horror.”

“I want the body to be the document. If we’re using Danielewski — the nested narratives, the text as labyrinth, the documents that contradict each other — then I think the body should be one of those documents. A body that contradicts the other evidence.”

I said, “Can you say more about what you mean by contradicts?”

She pulled her hair over one shoulder, thinking. “So imagine a community with a ritual. Something that everyone participates in, that everyone agrees is benign or even beautiful. And the physical evidence — what happens to the bodies of the people who go through the ritual — tells a completely different story than the official narrative. But the bodies keep being wrong in different ways. Inconsistently wrong. The way Navidson’s house keeps being measured differently by different people. The hallway is longer than the house. The body has more vertebrae than it should.”

“No,” Aickman said.

It landed in the conversation like a dropped plate. Machado waited.

“The additional vertebrae. That’s too legible. That’s a clue in a detective story. You’ve just told us what the ritual does — it adds to the body, it transforms the body in a catalogueable way. The moment you make the physical evidence that specific, you’ve given the reader a map. And the reader will follow the map to its destination and feel they’ve understood something. They’ll feel satisfied.” He said the word the way most people say the word infected. “The entire enterprise collapses into a puzzle.”

“I don’t think specificity is the enemy,” Machado said. “I think vagueness is lazy.”

“Vagueness and ambiguity are not the same thing.”

“They’re kissing cousins.”

“They are not even in the same county.” Aickman set down his glass with a precision that suggested the conversation had shifted into territory he cared about. “Ambiguity is when you show the reader two things that are both true and cannot both be true. The reader holds them simultaneously and feels the world tilt. Vagueness is when you simply decline to show the reader anything and call it atmosphere. I am arguing for ambiguity. I would never argue for vagueness. A story that is merely vague has failed.”

I found myself scribbling that down — two things that are both true and cannot both be true — because it was exactly the structural principle I needed for the contradicting narrators. The risk card called for multiple voices, two or three, that contradict each other. Not unreliable narrators in the usual sense, where one narrator is lying and the others aren’t. Narrators whose accounts are each internally coherent and mutually impossible.

“So the body can be specific,” I said, “as long as it’s specific in ways that don’t resolve.”

“The body can be anything,” Machado said, and there was an edge to it now, “as long as it’s a body. Not a metaphor. Not a symbol. An actual body that bleeds and aches and carries its history in its tissue. I’m tired of weird fiction that’s all atmosphere and no flesh. Aickman, your best stories have people in them who eat and sleep and take trains and book hotel rooms. Physical people doing physical things in physical places. The wrongness lands because they’re physical.”

Something shifted in his face. It wasn’t agreement, but it was recognition.

“In ‘The Hospice,’ the food is wrong,” he said.

“Yes.”

“The food is too much. There is too much food and it keeps coming and the host keeps insisting. And you never learn why the food is wrong. But your stomach knows.”

“Your stomach knows. That’s it. That’s what I mean.” Machado was almost vibrating. “The body as reader. Not the intellect. Not even the emotions, exactly. The body. I want to write something where you finish and your neck hurts. Where your skin feels like it’s been touched by someone you didn’t invite.”

I asked what the food scene in “The Hospice” had in common with Jackson’s stoning, because I was genuinely trying to find the connective tissue.

“Social obligation,” Machado said, before Aickman could. “The food is wrong but you eat it because your host is watching. The stones are wrong but you throw them because your neighbors are watching. The mechanism is identical. The community demands that you participate in the thing that is destroying you, and you do, because the alternative is being outside the community. And being outside the community is worse.”

“Is it?” Aickman said quietly.

“For most people, yes.”

“For most people. But not for the story’s narrator.”

“I don’t know who the narrator is yet.”

“Narrators,” I corrected. “We have two or three. Contradicting each other.”

Aickman’s expression when I said this was hard to read. He didn’t look displeased, exactly. He looked like a man being offered a tool he recognized but had never used in quite this way.

“Danielewski’s house,” he said. “The Navidson Record is narrated by Zampano, who is narrated by Truant, who is narrated by the editors. Each layer contradicts the one below it. But the genius — and I’ll grant Danielewski this — is that the house itself remains consistent. The house is always impossible. The house doesn’t change its nature depending on who’s describing it. The narrators distort, but the thing at the center is real.”

“So what’s our house?” I said.

No one answered for a long time. Machado swirled her mezcal. Aickman examined the grain of the table. I watched a couple at the bar negotiate who would pay, and for a moment the silence was just a silence and not a dramatic pause.

“The ritual,” Machado said finally. “The communal act. Whatever it is that this community does together. That’s the house. Each narrator describes it differently, each account is internally consistent, and none of them agree. But the ritual itself is real. The ritual has a body — it leaves marks, it changes things, it has physical consequences that can be observed. You can measure the hallway of the ritual, and it’s always longer than the house it’s in.”

“I rather like that,” Aickman said, which coming from him was extravagant.

I said, “And the question of whether the ritual is violent — whether it’s a stoning or a feast or something else entirely — changes depending on which narrator you’re reading.”

“Not changes,” Machado said. “Each narrator is accurate. One narrator describes something that looks like surgery and is experienced as tenderness. Another describes something that looks like tenderness and is experienced as surgery. The body on the table is the same body. The hands are the same hands. The act is the same act.”

“And the reader will want to know which one is true.”

“The reader doesn’t get to know.”

Aickman almost smiled. Almost. His mouth did something adjacent to smiling and then thought better of it.

“This is the problem with contemporary fiction,” he said, which I took as a digression until it became clear it wasn’t. “Everyone wants to know. The entire culture is organized around knowing — around information, around data, around the definitive account. When I wrote a story where a man visits a house and something happens that he cannot explain and the story ends without explaining it, my editor would say, ‘But Robert, what happened?’ And I would say, ‘Something happened.’ And he would say, ‘Yes, but what?’ As if the what were separable from the how of the telling. As if you could remove the ambiguity and be left with the story, the way you can shell a walnut. The ambiguity is not the shell. The ambiguity is the meat.”

“I agree with you,” Machado said, “which annoys me, so let me find where I disagree.” She drank. “I think there’s a version of ambiguity that’s a power play. The author knows something and withholds it from the reader, and the withholding is the point. That’s not ambiguity. That’s cruelty masquerading as sophistication.”

“And you think my stories do that?”

“I think some of them do. I think ‘The Inner Room’ does.”

“You’re wrong.”

“I might be. But the experience of reading it was the experience of being teased. Like someone dangling keys above a baby.”

“The experience of reading it is the experience of being in a room whose dimensions don’t make sense. If that feels like teasing, the failure may not be the story’s.”

I watched them look at each other across the table. Neither of them blinked. I had the sense that this disagreement was old — not between these two specific people, but between their two positions. The weird tale as restraint against the weird tale as embodiment. The uncanny as a negative space, defined by what’s withheld, versus the uncanny as a positive space, defined by what’s presented in excess.

“Both,” I said. They turned to me. “Both at once. That’s what the multiple narrators buy us. One narrator who withholds. Another who provides too much. A third who — I don’t know, a third who seems to be narrating a different event entirely but is somehow narrating the same one.”

“The third narrator is the community,” Machado said immediately. “The collective voice. We the undersigned. We who participate. The communal ‘we’ that describes the ritual in the language of normalcy, of beauty, of tradition. The voice that says this is what we do and it is good. Jackson’s voice — the flat, reportorial voice that describes the lottery as if it were a county fair.”

“And within that communal voice,” Aickman said, leaning back, “within the ‘we,’ there is a single dissenter who does not dissent. A single person who knows and says nothing. Because saying something would be worse than participating.”

“Would it? Or is that just what the community has taught them to believe?”

“Those are the same thing. If you believe that saying something would be worse, then saying something would be worse. The belief is self-executing. Jackson knew that. The woman with the slip of paper doesn’t run. She could run. She has legs. Nothing physical prevents her from leaving. She stays because the communal reality is more real to her than her own survival.”

“That’s the most horrifying sentence you’ve said tonight,” Machado said.

“I’ve said more horrifying things. You simply weren’t in the room.”

I wanted to ask about the physical space — where this community exists, what the place looks like, whether it matters — but I was also aware that pinning down the setting would start pulling us toward plotting, and we weren’t there yet. Instead I asked something that had been bothering me.

“The text as labyrinth. Danielewski uses footnotes, crossed-out passages, competing typefaces. We’re writing a short story, not a novel. How do we get the labyrinth into a compressed form?”

“You don’t need footnotes,” Machado said. “You need formal disruption. The moment when the text itself does something the reader doesn’t expect. A paragraph that repeats but with one word changed. A section break that leads to the same scene you just read, told by someone else, and nothing matches. The labyrinth isn’t about how much text there is. It’s about how the text behaves.”

“The text as space,” Aickman said. “A corridor you walk down that turns out to be longer than the building could contain.”

“Yes. The reading experience itself becomes the impossible architecture. You’re inside the house. The story is the house.”

I wrote that down too. The story is the house. It was the kind of statement that could collapse into pretension if you weren’t careful, but sitting in that dim pub with these two people, it felt like the thing we were circling.

“I keep thinking about hands,” Machado said, seeming to change the subject. “Everyone in Jackson’s story uses their hands. They pick up stones. Old Man Warner picks up the first one — no, wait, someone hits Tessie first, on the side of the head, and then little Davy Hutchinson is given pebbles. A child is given stones to throw at his mother. That image never leaves you. And the reason it never leaves you is that the hands are ordinary. They’re hands that also knead bread and button coats and open mailboxes.”

“The hands of the narrators,” I said, half to myself. “What their hands are doing during the ritual. One narrator’s hands are performing surgery. Another’s are performing a caress. A third’s—”

“A third’s are simply holding something,” Aickman interrupted. “Holding something and not letting go. And the reader never learns what the something is, but the hands won’t open.”

Machado set down her glass and looked at both of us for a long moment, as if deciding whether to say the next thing.

“I need the story to be queer,” she said. “Not in the sense that there’s a same-sex couple — though there might be. In the sense that the community’s ritual has something to do with desire and the regulation of desire. The communal violence in Jackson is about conformity, about the suppression of the individual. I want our version to be about the suppression of a specific kind of wanting. The thing the community will not let its members want.”

Aickman said nothing. I could feel him not objecting, which was different from agreeing.

“The body on the table,” she continued. “The body being worked on by all those contradicting hands. What if the body wants to be there? What if the horror isn’t that the ritual is forced but that the ritual is desired, and the desire is the thing that can’t be spoken? Every narrator gives a different account of what’s happening because to describe it accurately would be to name the desire, and naming the desire is the one thing the community forbids.”

“That’s—” I started.

“That would make the ambiguity structural,” Aickman said, and his voice had changed. Quieter. More careful. As if he were handling something fragile. “Not a choice the author makes but a condition the characters live inside. They cannot describe the ritual accurately because accuracy would require a vocabulary the community has not permitted to exist.”

“A language the house doesn’t have a room for,” Machado said.

The pub was closing. They were putting chairs on tables at the far end. Aickman had not finished his sherry, and Machado was turning her empty glass between her palms, and I was sitting between them thinking about hands, about houses that are longer than buildings, about a community whose ritual keeps being described and never being described, and whether three narrators could each tell the truth in a language that hadn’t been invented yet for the thing they all
