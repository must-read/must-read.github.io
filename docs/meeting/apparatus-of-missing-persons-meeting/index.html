Sincerity as a Lethal Dose, or The Trouble with Inventing People

A discussion between Oscar Wilde and Vladimir Nabokov

March 1, 2026

The room Nabokov had chosen was not, strictly speaking, a room. It was a glassed-in porch attached to a Swiss hotel that no longer existed in any guidebook I could find, furnished with the kind of wicker chairs that suggest someone once cared very much about comfort and then died. Through the windows, Lake Geneva performed its usual trick of looking like an oil painting that had been left in a drafty hallway — magnificent, slightly cold, faintly accusatory.

Wilde arrived twenty minutes late, which he appeared to regard as a form of punctuality.

“I never arrive on time,” he said, settling into the wicker with the practiced ease of a man who has been settling into other people’s furniture his entire life. “On time is the arrival of the expected. Twenty minutes late is the arrival of the interesting. After an hour, one becomes mythological. I try to stay in the interesting range.”

Nabokov was already seated, a notebook open on the table before him, his pen moving in small, precise circles that might have been notes or might have been lepidoptera. He did not look up.

“Punctuality,” Nabokov said, “is a species of accuracy. I have spent my life in pursuit of accuracy. The exact word. The exact wing pattern. The exact shade of a woman’s hair as she turns in a doorway at four in the afternoon in a city she is about to leave forever.” He paused. “You would call this pedantry.”

“I would call it magnificent,” Wilde said, with a warmth that appeared to catch Nabokov slightly off guard. “Pedantry is merely thoroughness that has not yet learned to accessorize.”

I introduced the project. We were to write a parody-pastiche — a literary critic annotating a recently discovered masterpiece, where the footnotes gradually reveal that the critic has invented the masterpiece, the author, and possibly himself. I mentioned Pale Fire. I mentioned The Importance of Being Earnest. I watched both of them react to their own work being named and found the reactions instructive: Wilde smiled as though complimented by a stranger he intended to charm; Nabokov frowned as though a butterfly he had pinned had been incorrectly labeled.

“You are proposing,” Nabokov said, “a commentary that devours its text. This I understand. Kinbote’s commentary is a parasite that kills its host and then, having no host, must become its own organism. The footnotes in Pale Fire are not about Shade’s poem. They are about Kinbote’s need to exist.”

“Precisely,” I said. “But in our version, the text being devoured doesn’t exist at all. The critic has invented it.”

“Ah,” said Wilde, crossing his legs, “then the commentary is not a parasite. It is something more interesting. It is a lover who has invented the beloved. The most sincere form of devotion — to devote oneself to something one has made up entirely.”

This landed. I felt it land. Nabokov set down his pen.

“You are being clever,” Nabokov said to Wilde, “and the cleverness obscures something true. The scholar who invents his text is not merely performing devotion. He is performing scholarship. The apparatus — the footnotes, the cross-references, the bibliography, the querulous acknowledgments — these are the trappings of a discipline. He is not pretending to love. He is pretending to know. The pretense of knowledge is far more dangerous than the pretense of love.”

“Is it?” Wilde said. “I’ve always found the pretense of love to be extraordinarily dangerous. Particularly when it becomes sincere.”

“When does the pretense of love become sincere?”

“At exactly the moment when you can no longer locate the boundary. That is also the moment at which it becomes lethal.”

I tried to steer us toward the mechanics. “The critic — what kind of person does this? Invents a masterpiece, invents the author who wrote it, and then writes a critical apparatus so detailed and scholarly that the nonexistence of the source material becomes, somehow, beside the point?”

“A person,” Nabokov said, “who cannot bear to be a secondary figure. The scholar is always secondary. The text comes first; the scholar annotates. The author creates; the critic interprets. It is a hierarchy, and it is permanent, and for certain temperaments it is intolerable. So the scholar begins to write his own text — but he cannot admit this, because to be an author would mean abandoning the scholarly apparatus that is his entire identity. So he invents an author to be the author, and he remains the scholar. He has the pleasure of creation and the dignity of commentary.”

“That’s awfully psychological,” Wilde said. “I prefer it as a social problem. Your scholar-inventor is not suffering from an identity crisis. He is suffering from a party. Specifically, he has been at the same party for thirty years — the party of academic life, where everyone pretends to have read everything and no one admits to having enjoyed any of it — and he has discovered that the only way to make the party interesting is to introduce a guest who doesn’t exist.”

“The imaginary guest,” I said. “The invented author.”

“Who is, naturally, more interesting than anyone real at the party. Because the real guests have flaws that are tedious — they publish too little, or too much, or in the wrong journals — while the invented guest has flaws that are fascinating. The invented author is a genius and a recluse and possibly a fraud, which is the most compelling combination since Hamlet.”

Nabokov shook his head, but with a half-smile that suggested he was shaking it at himself as much as at Wilde. “You reduce everything to performance.”

“You reduce everything to precision. We are both right, and therefore both in danger.”

“The danger,” I said, “is that the piece becomes a one-joke engine. Critic invents text, footnotes get increasingly absurd, reader catches on. How do we sustain this for four thousand words without the reader simply waiting for the next reveal?”

“By making the footnotes genuinely good criticism,” Nabokov said immediately. “The commentary must function as literary criticism. Real literary criticism. The passages the scholar quotes — from his invented masterpiece — must be worth quoting. The analysis must be penetrating. The reader must find themselves thinking, ‘This is brilliant scholarship,’ and only then realize that the text being analyzed does not exist. The comedy is sharpest when it is built on something real.”

“I disagree,” Wilde said. “Or rather, I agree, but the something real is not the scholarship. It is the feeling. Your scholar must love his invented author. Not ironically. Not performatively. He must be devastated by this author’s prose. He must find in these invented passages the thing he has been looking for his entire career — genuine literary greatness — and he must be unable to distinguish between having found it and having made it. That confusion is not a joke. It is the human condition in footnote form.”

There was a silence. Outside, the lake did nothing, which it did beautifully.

“The Importance of Being Earnest,” I said, “is about invented identities that become real. Jack invents Ernest. Then Ernest becomes more real than Jack. The fiction displaces the fact.”

“Yes,” Wilde said, “and the critical point — the point everyone misses, because they are too busy laughing, which is a form of payment I accept gladly — is that the fiction is better. Ernest is better than Jack. The invented version is wittier, braver, more fully alive. Bunbury is better than anything Algernon could actually do on a weekend. The lie, in Earnest, is not a failure of truth. It is an improvement upon it.”

“In Pale Fire,” Nabokov said, “the lie is not an improvement. It is a prison. Kinbote’s Zembla is more vivid than his reality, yes. More beautiful, more narratively satisfying. But it is a cage he has built around himself, and every footnote is another bar. The brilliance of the architecture is what makes it tragic.”

“Can it be both?” I asked. “A cage and an improvement?”

“Everything interesting is both,” Wilde said.

I was beginning to see the shape of the piece. A critic — call him something absurd, something that sounds both distinguished and faintly invented — publishing his magnum opus: the definitive critical edition of a lost masterwork by an author no one has heard of. The edition is impeccable. The footnotes are learned, witty, occasionally moving. The quoted passages are extraordinary. And slowly, through accumulating contradictions, through dates that don’t align and sources that can’t be verified and a biography of the author that grows increasingly incredible, the reader realizes that the whole thing is fabricated. The critic has written the masterpiece himself, attributed it to a phantom, and then spent his career studying his own work while pretending to study someone else’s.

“The question,” Nabokov said, reading my face, “is whether this is comedy or tragedy.”

“It is comedy until the last ten pages,” Wilde said. “Then it is something the English language does not have a word for, which is one of the many failures of the English language that I have spent my life correcting.”

“The critic must quote from the invented work,” Nabokov said. “And the passages must be remarkable. I insist on this. If the passages are bad — if they read as parody, as pastiche of greatness — the piece collapses. The reader will think, ‘Obviously this is invented; no real work could be this clumsy.’ The passages must be good enough that the reader believes in them. Good enough that the reader wants them to be real.”

“And then feels the loss when they aren’t,” I said.

“No,” Wilde said. “And then questions whether ‘real’ is the relevant category. If the passages move you, what does it matter who wrote them, or whether the attributed author existed? The forgery that makes you weep is worth more than the authenticated manuscript that bores you. This is a scandal, of course. But all the best truths are scandals.”

“We are not writing a defense of forgery,” Nabokov said sharply.

“We are writing a defense of nothing. We are writing a comedy about a man who loves something he made up. Whether it is a defense or an indictment depends entirely on whether you think loving something you made up is brave or pathetic. I have never been able to decide.”

“I lean toward pathetic,” Nabokov said.

“You lean toward whatever hurts more. That is your defining characteristic as a writer, and it is magnificent, and it occasionally makes you unbearable.”

I expected Nabokov to bristle. He didn’t. He picked up his pen and resumed the small precise circles.

“The scholar’s name,” he said. “It should suggest both authority and fabrication. A name that sounds almost real. A name from a country that might or might not exist.”

“Like Kinbote’s Zembla,” I said.

“Nothing like Zembla. Something warmer. Zembla is ice. This scholar is not cold. He is, as Wilde correctly identified, in love. His name should carry that warmth and also its slight ridiculousness.”

“Earnest,” Wilde murmured.

“What?”

“Nothing. Or everything. I haven’t decided.”

I raised the question of the footnotes’ structure — how they should escalate, what they should reveal and when. Nabokov had firm views.

“The early footnotes must be flawless. No seams. The reader should accept the scholarly apparatus without question. By footnote ten, there should be a detail that nags — a date that seems unlikely, a reference to a library that may not exist. By footnote twenty, the reader who is paying attention knows. By footnote thirty, the reader who is not paying attention knows. And by the final footnote, everyone knows, including the scholar himself, which is the thing he has been trying not to know for the entire piece.”

“The scholar knows?” I said.

“Of course the scholar knows. The deepest comedy — and here I agree with Wilde, which pains me — is that the performance of not-knowing is itself a form of sincerity. He has been lying for so long that the lie has become his truth, and the moment of recognition — if it comes — is not an unmasking but a bereavement.”

“Or a liberation,” Wilde offered.

“Those are the same thing.”

This was the first moment they fully agreed, and it unsettled both of them. Wilde examined his fingernails. Nabokov examined the lake.

“The invented masterpiece,” I said. “What is it? A novel? A poem?”

“A novel,” Nabokov said. “Poems are too short to support the apparatus. A novel gives you chapters to cite, characters to analyze, a publication history to fabricate.”

“A novel no one has read,” Wilde added, “because it was suppressed, or lost, or published in a country that no longer exists. The unavailability is essential. If anyone could simply read the book, the game would be over immediately. The scholar must control access to the text. He must be the only one who has seen it.”

“The sole surviving copy,” I said.

“Which he keeps in a vault, or a safe deposit box, or — better — which he claims to have memorized. The physical text was destroyed. Only his memory remains. And his memory, of course, is immaculate.”

Nabokov’s pen stopped. “That is good,” he said, in a tone that suggested saying so was costing him something. “The memory-as-text. The scholar as the last surviving edition. Yes. That is the image.”

“And the footnotes,” Wilde said, “are his way of proving the text existed. Each footnote says: look, I remember this passage. Look, I can analyze it. Look, the analysis is rigorous. Therefore the passage must have existed. Therefore the novel must have existed. Therefore the author must have existed. Therefore I — who have devoted my life to this author — must exist as well.”

“The chain of therefore,” I said. “Each link depending on the one before it. Pull out the first link and — ”

“Nothing collapses,” Wilde said. “That’s the joke. Pull out the first link and everything remains standing, because the footnotes have become their own support. The apparatus no longer needs a text. The commentary is the cathedral, and the poem it was built to house has been gone for centuries, and no one has noticed because the buttresses are so beautiful.”

The light through the glass had shifted while we talked. The lake had turned from blue-grey to something closer to pewter. Nabokov closed his notebook.

“One more thing,” he said. “The prose. The scholar’s prose must be beautiful. Not merely competent, not merely learned — beautiful. Epigrammatic, self-aware, slightly drunk on its own elegance. Because the greatest horror, for the reader who catches on, is not that the masterpiece doesn’t exist. It is that the scholarly apparatus is the masterpiece. The critic has written the great work he was pretending to annotate, but he has written it in the wrong form. He has put his genius into the footnotes and his phantom into the text. He is standing in his own light.”

“Standing in one’s own light,” Wilde said, “is, in my experience, the only place worth standing.”

“In your experience,” Nabokov said, “that is exactly where the trouble starts.”

Neither of them looked at me. The conversation had moved past me, into the space between them, where the dandy and the scholar-madman were circling something they both recognized and neither wanted to name. I wrote down what I could. The rest is on the lake somewhere, going pewter.
