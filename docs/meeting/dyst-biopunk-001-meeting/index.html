Dystopian / Biopunk Dystopia

On the Clinical Prose of Disappearance

A discussion between Margaret Atwood and P.D. James

February 21, 2026

We met in a university faculty lounge that smelled of old carpet and instant coffee — the kind of room where decisions about curricula are made under fluorescent lights by people who would rather be elsewhere. James had arrived early and chosen the chair closest to the window, though the window looked out onto a parking garage. Atwood was ten minutes late and unapologetic about it. She came in carrying a canvas tote bag printed with a Margaret Atwood quote, which she set on the table face-down without comment.

“Biopunk,” Atwood said, sitting down. “You want to talk about biopunk.”

I said I wanted to talk about biological engineering as a form of social control — not the chrome-and-neon version, but the institutional kind. The version that arrives through healthcare systems and regulatory frameworks and informed consent forms that nobody reads.

“Good,” Atwood said. “Because the chrome-and-neon version is boring. It’s always boring. Some tattooed genius in a basement laboratory splicing genes with a pipette, and the camera swoops through the double helix like we’re on a theme park ride. That’s not how it happens. It happens in committee rooms. It happens through procurement processes. Some deputy minister signs a form and six years later an entire generation has been optimized for compliance, and nobody can point to the moment it went wrong because there wasn’t a moment. There was a process.”

“A process,” James said quietly. She had been listening with the particular stillness of someone who is assembling a response. “Yes. That’s how it always works. Not with a declaration. With an accretion. One policy, then another, then a revision to the first policy that makes the second seem inevitable. And at each step, the people implementing it believe — genuinely believe — that they’re doing good. That’s essential. The functionaries are not villains. They’re professionals.”

“They’re civil servants,” Atwood said.

“They’re civil servants who go home at five o’clock and water their gardens.” James folded her hands. “That’s the horror of it. The garden is lovely. The person tending it has signed off on something that will reshape what it means to be human, and then they’ve gone home and deadheaded the roses.”

I asked whether the story should center on one of these functionaries.

“No,” they said, nearly simultaneously, and then looked at each other with the faint surprise of unexpected agreement.

“The functionary is the mechanism,” James continued. “Not the subject. The subject is the person who has been — what’s the word — optimized. Modified. The person on the receiving end of the form. Though I hesitate to say ‘receiving end’ because that implies passivity, and one of the things I find most disturbing about biological control is that the subject is often an active participant. They sign the form. They attend the appointments. They take the supplements or the gene therapy or whatever it is, and they do it because — this is crucial — because why wouldn’t you? If you can make your child healthier, calmer, more capable of sustained attention, less prone to the particular anxieties that make modern life unbearable — why wouldn’t you?”

“Because of what you lose,” I said.

“What do you lose?” Atwood asked, and I couldn’t tell whether she was challenging me or genuinely asking. “Be specific. Don’t say ‘humanity’ — that’s a greeting card. What, specifically, is lost when you engineer a person to be content?”

I didn’t have an answer ready. I said something about the capacity for dissent.

“But dissent isn’t an inherent good,” Atwood said. “Dissent is a response to conditions that are intolerable. If you eliminate the intolerable conditions — actually eliminate them, not just paper over them — then the absence of dissent isn’t a symptom. It’s a cure. The question is whether engineered contentment constitutes an elimination of intolerable conditions or a masking of them. And that question is genuinely hard. I don’t think it has an answer. I think the story has to live inside the not-knowing.”

James shifted in her chair. “I disagree. I think it does have an answer, and the answer is that engineered contentment is always a masking, because the conditions that make contentment necessary — inequality, scarcity, the fundamental arbitrariness of who suffers and who doesn’t — those conditions haven’t changed. They’ve been made invisible. And invisible suffering is worse than visible suffering because you can’t even point to it. You can’t even say ‘I am suffering’ if the capacity to identify suffering has been edited out of you.”

“Edited out,” Atwood repeated. “I like that. Not removed. Edited. Like a manuscript. Someone went through your genome with a red pen.”

There was a pause. James looked out at the parking garage.

“You mentioned a document,” James said, turning back. “That the story should take the form of a document.”

I explained the constraint — that the piece needed to be structured as a non-literary document. A clinical form, perhaps, or a compliance report, or an intake questionnaire. Something bureaucratic that would create friction with the emotional content.

Atwood leaned back. “Well, obviously it should be a consent form.”

“Obviously?” James raised an eyebrow.

“A consent form for a genetic modification procedure. Multi-page. Legalistic. Full of subsections and checkboxes and disclaimers. ‘I understand that the following modifications are irreversible. I understand that the following modifications may affect emotional range, creative capacity, and the ability to experience what the literature refers to as existential dread. I consent to the reduction of existential dread.’ And somewhere in the checkboxes, buried in the legalese, is the actual story — the person filling out the form, the life they’ve lived that brought them to this office, the child they’re consenting on behalf of.”

“It’s too neat,” James said.

“What do you mean, too neat?”

“I mean a consent form is too obviously ironic. The reader sees it coming. ‘Ah, a consent form for the erasure of humanity — how pointed.’ It’s editorial cartoonish. I’d rather see something that doesn’t announce its own irony. A compliance diary, perhaps. A form that a modified person fills out weekly to confirm that the modification is — what would the language be — ‘performing within expected parameters.’ The horror wouldn’t be in the irony of consent. The horror would be in the sincerity of the entries. A person who genuinely believes they are well. Who ticks the boxes and means it.”

I felt the room shift. Atwood was quiet for a moment — not a concessive silence but a recalibrating one.

“A compliance diary,” she said. “So we’re inside the modified person’s perspective.”

“Yes. And they’re not suffering. That’s the point. They’re not a victim in any sense they can recognize. Their diary entries are calm, competent, perhaps even warm. They describe their work, their relationships, their sense of purpose. And the reader — the reader outside the document — can see what’s been taken, but the person inside the document cannot. Because the ability to see it is precisely what was modified.”

“That’s Huxley,” Atwood said. “That’s soma. The person who can’t perceive their own diminishment.”

“It’s adjacent to Huxley, yes,” James said, “but Huxley’s people are numb. They’re sedated. What I’m describing is worse. This person is articulate, intelligent, and genuinely fulfilled. They have a rich inner life. It’s just that the inner life has been — curated. Like a garden.”

“The garden again,” I said.

“The garden again.” James allowed herself a thin smile. “I keep coming back to it because it’s the right image. A garden is nature that has been made to behave. It’s beautiful precisely because it’s controlled. And the gardener will tell you — sincerely, passionately — that they love their garden, that it gives them peace, that it is the most meaningful thing in their life. And they are not wrong. The love is real. The peace is real. But it is a peace that was designed.”

Atwood crossed her arms. “I’m troubled by something. If the modified person is genuinely content — not sedated, not dulled, but intelligently, articulately content — then what right does the reader have to pity them? Who are we to say that their happiness is lesser because it was engineered? Every happiness is engineered to some degree. By culture, by economics, by the accident of which neurotransmitters your particular brain happens to produce in which quantities. We don’t call a person who’s happy because of good serotonin levels inauthentic.”

“No,” James said. “But we call a person who’s happy because someone else decided they should be — that’s different. That’s not chemistry. That’s authority.”

“Is it? Is it different? If I raise my child in a stable home, with good nutrition, with access to education and nature and art — I am engineering their happiness. I am making choices that shape their neurochemistry. At what point does good parenting become genetic modification? Where’s the line?”

“The line,” James said slowly, “is that your child can reject what you’ve given them. They can grow up and repudiate your values and move to another country and become someone you don’t recognize. That capacity — the capacity to refuse the design — is what’s at stake. If the modification removes the capacity to refuse, then it doesn’t matter how happy the person is. They’re not a person anymore. They’re a garden.”

The room was quiet. The fluorescent light above us flickered once and then resumed its steady, institutional hum.

I said I thought the compliance diary was the right form. James was right about the consent form being too on-the-nose. But I wanted to know what would break the diary’s surface. What would create the friction — the moment where the bureaucratic form and the emotional content collide.

“Something the diary can’t contain,” Atwood said. “Something that doesn’t fit the checkboxes. A dream, maybe. Modified people shouldn’t dream — or they should dream in approved patterns — but this person dreams something that doesn’t belong. Something atavistic. Something from before.”

“Before what?”

“Before the modification. A memory that shouldn’t exist. Not a dramatic memory — not trauma, not loss. Something small. The smell of rain on hot asphalt. The specific quality of light in a room where someone you loved was sitting. Something the genome editors didn’t think to remove because it wasn’t flagged as disruptive.”

James nodded. “And the person writing the diary doesn’t know what to do with it. It doesn’t fit the form. There’s no checkbox for ‘I dreamed about the light in my grandmother’s kitchen and I woke up feeling something I have no word for.’ So they don’t report it. For the first time, they leave a box unchecked. And that omission — that tiny act of non-compliance — is the entire story.”

“That’s too tidy,” Atwood said. “That’s a parable. The one unchecked box that represents the indomitable human spirit. No. The box gets unchecked, and nothing happens. Nobody notices. The system doesn’t care about one box. The system is robust. It was designed to tolerate variance within acceptable parameters. And the person goes back to being content, because they are content, because contentment is what they were built for. And the dream fades. And the light in the grandmother’s kitchen becomes just another data point that didn’t reach the threshold for clinical significance.”

“That’s bleaker than what I proposed,” James said, “and I’m the one who wrote The Children of Men.”

“It’s not bleak. It’s accurate. The horror of biological engineering isn’t that the human spirit is crushed. It’s that the human spirit is irrelevant. The system doesn’t need to crush it. It can afford to let it flicker. A flicker is not a threat.”

I asked about the engineered being — the figure at the moral center. In Bacigalupi’s work, it’s the Windup Girl herself: manufactured, exploited, but possessed of an interiority that indicts the society around her. Should our protagonist be someone like that? Someone designed, looking out?

“That’s the perspective of the diary,” James said. “From the inside. Which means we never get the outside view. The reader infers it. The reader does the work of seeing what the diarist cannot.”

“But the diarist has to give us enough,” Atwood said. “The entries can’t just be ‘Mood: Stable. Productivity: Optimal.’ There has to be language. Personality. The tragedy has to be that this person is vivid and specific and real and also — also — built. Both things. Not a robot performing humanity. A human whose humanity has been shaped by an authority they will never perceive as authority because the perception of authority is one of the things that was shaped.”

“Built,” James said. “I keep returning to the language of construction. Built, engineered, designed. These are architectural terms. We’re talking about a person as if they’re a building. And perhaps that’s what the diary should expose — the architectural language hiding inside the clinical language. Every ‘wellness metric’ is a load-bearing wall. Every ‘compliance indicator’ is a window that only faces one direction.”

“You’re getting architectural,” Atwood said. “Watch it.”

“You’re getting metaphorical,” James replied. “Which is worse, in a story that’s meant to be a document.”

This was a real disagreement. I could feel it in the temperature of the room — not hostile, but not comfortable either. Atwood wanted the diary to carry emotional weight through language, through the voice of the diarist. James wanted the form itself to do the work — the gaps between checkboxes, the architecture of what’s asked and what’s not asked.

“Both,” I said, and immediately regretted the weakness of it.

“Both is a non-answer,” Atwood said.

“Both is the only honest answer,” James countered. “A document that works has both — the form constraining the content, the content straining against the form. That friction is the story. If the form wins, you’ve written a piece of conceptual art. If the content wins, you’ve written a conventional story with a gimmick. Neither is interesting.”

Atwood picked up her tote bag, looked at the quote on it, set it back down again, face-down.

“Fine,” she said. “The diary. Weekly entries. The language of compliance. And underneath it — not hidden, not coded, but genuinely invisible to the person writing it — the evidence of what was done. A happiness so complete it should be terrifying. Will be terrifying. To everyone except the person experiencing it.”

“And no redemption,” James said.

“No redemption,” Atwood agreed. “Not because redemption is impossible, but because the story doesn’t owe us that. The diary ends. The entries stop. Not because the person wakes up or rebels or has an epiphany. Because the diary has reached its final page. It’s a form. Forms end.”

James stood up, straightened her jacket, and looked out the window at the parking garage one last time.

“The quiet end of humanity,” she said. “Not with a bang. With a final entry.”

Atwood was already reaching for her bag. “With a box checked ‘Satisfactory’ and no one left to disagree.”

I sat there after they left, staring at the empty chairs, thinking about the dream of the grandmother’s kitchen — the light in it, the smell of something cooking, the way it would appear in a compliance diary as a blank space where an answer should be. I didn’t know yet whether the blank space would matter. Whether the system would notice. Whether noticing and not-noticing were, in the end, the same thing.

The fluorescent light continued to hum. I turned to a fresh page in my notebook and wrote: Satisfactory. And then, underneath it, smaller: What was the question?
