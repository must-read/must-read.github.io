Romance / Paranormal Romance

Every Block Has a Ghost

A discussion between Beverly Jenkins and Casey McQuiston

February 21, 2026

We met at a coffee shop in D.C. that Beverly picked because it used to be something else — a barbershop, she told us, owned by a man named Thurston who cut hair for forty years and never raised his prices. The shop closed in 2019. Now it sells twelve-dollar oat milk lattes with lavender foam. Beverly ordered a black coffee and sat beneath a framed photograph of Thurston that the new owners had hung near the register, a gesture that managed to be both respectful and insufficient.

Casey arrived eleven minutes late, apologizing with their whole body, carrying a tote bag that said BOOKS ARE MY PERSONALITY in block letters. They ordered something complicated and then immediately forgot what it was called when the barista asked them to repeat it.

“Okay,” Casey said, dropping into the chair across from Beverly. “Paranormal romance. A Black woman. A bookstore. An immortal guy. I’m already in love with this and I haven’t heard a single detail.”

Beverly looked at them over her coffee. “That’s exactly the problem.”

I felt the conversation sharpen before it had really started. I opened my notebook. I’d been thinking about this combination for days — the interview-as-confession structure, the time-displaced love, the community bookstore as an anchor point. I had ideas. I was about to learn that most of them were wrong.

“What I mean,” Beverly said, “is that the premise is seductive. A woman building something permanent. A man who can’t stay. It writes itself, and that’s the danger. The version of this story that writes itself is sentimental garbage.”

Casey tilted their head. “I don’t think sentimentality is automatically garbage.”

“I didn’t say sentimentality. I said sentimental garbage. There’s a version of this where the bookstore is a symbol of community resilience and the immortal man learns the value of putting down roots and everyone cries and nobody believes a word of it. That version is easy. That version is a lie.”

“So what’s the truth?” I asked.

Beverly set down her cup. “The truth is that building something permanent in a neighborhood that’s being eaten alive by developers is an act of war. Not a metaphor for war. Actual, daily, grinding combat. Zoning meetings. Tax assessments. Suppliers who stop giving you credit because they assume you’re going under. The romance of a community bookstore is about fifteen minutes long. After that, it’s spreadsheets and fury.”

Casey was nodding. “Okay, yes. I love that. But I also think — can I push back on something?”

“You can try.”

“The fury is real. The grind is real. But there’s also joy in it. Not inspirational-poster joy. Weird, specific, almost irrational joy. The joy of a regular who comes in every Tuesday and only ever buys one author. The joy of a kid who sits on your floor for three hours and reads an entire graphic novel and then puts it back on the shelf and leaves without buying anything, and you’re somehow fine with that. I write about joy that doesn’t make sense, and I think this character — the bookstore owner — she has that. She has fury and she has joy and the two things don’t cancel each other out.”

Beverly studied Casey for a moment. Something shifted in her expression — not agreement exactly, but recognition. “The joy has to be specific. If it’s general, it’s a Hallmark card.”

“One hundred percent specific. The joy of knowing that the guy who sells incense on the corner has started recommending her store to tourists. The joy of catching a teenager shoplifting a poetry collection and deciding not to say anything.”

“The teenager is too cute.”

“Fine. A middle-aged man shoplifting a poetry collection.”

“Better.” Beverly almost smiled. “Why poetry?”

“Because it’s embarrassing. A grown man who wants a poetry book badly enough to steal it — that’s a person with a secret interior life. She sees him do it and she understands something about her neighborhood that she couldn’t have learned any other way.”

I was writing fast, but I wanted to steer us toward the supernatural element before we got too deep into the realism. “What about him?” I said. “The immortal. How does he work?”

Beverly leaned back. “He doesn’t sparkle.”

Casey laughed. “Obviously he doesn’t sparkle.”

“I’m serious. He doesn’t sparkle, he doesn’t brood on rooftops, he doesn’t have a tragic backstory that explains everything. Or — no. He has a tragic backstory. Of course he does. He’s been alive for centuries. But the tragedy isn’t romantic. It’s cumulative. It’s the weight of having watched neighborhoods like hers form and dissolve and form again, knowing that the dissolution is coming, not being able to say so because who would believe him, and also because saying so would be cruel.”

“So he’s carrying this knowledge,” I said. “He knows what gentrification looks like from a three-hundred-year perspective.”

“Longer than that,” Beverly said. “He’s seen it before it had a name. He’s seen entire communities built by free Black people after the Civil War — thriving, self-sufficient, with their own banks and schools and newspapers — erased in a single generation. Greenwood. Rosewood. Seneca Village. He was there. Not as a savior. As a witness who couldn’t do anything.”

The coffee shop was loud around us — the espresso machine, someone’s laptop playing a podcast without headphones — but our corner had gone quiet.

Casey spoke carefully. “That’s heavy. That’s really heavy. And I think it needs to be heavy. But I also think — does he get to be funny?”

“Why does he need to be funny?” Beverly asked.

“Because people who carry that much grief and keep going are often hilarious. Not despite the grief. Because of it. Humor is a survival mechanism. And if he’s been alive for centuries, he’s had centuries to sharpen it.” Casey paused. “I want him to make her laugh. Not in a charming-love-interest way. In a way that catches her off guard. She’s mid-rant about a city council meeting and he says something so unexpectedly funny that she snorts, and then she’s mad at herself for snorting, and then she’s laughing harder because she’s mad.”

I saw it immediately. That was a scene. That was an actual moment between two people.

Beverly nodded slowly. “He can be funny. But the humor has to come from knowledge, not from detachment. He’s not wry. He’s not above it all. He’s funny the way people who’ve been through things are funny — they find absurdity where others find tragedy because they’ve already done the tragedy and they’re tired.”

“Yes,” Casey said. “Exactly that. He’s not sardonic. He’s exhausted in a way that’s become comic. There’s a difference.”

“There’s a huge difference.” Beverly pointed at me. “Write that down. Exhausted in a way that’s become comic. That’s his register.”

I wrote it down. Then I asked the thing I’d been circling. “How does she find out?”

Both of them looked at me.

“That he’s immortal. How does the reveal work?”

Casey jumped in first. “She doesn’t find out in one scene. She accumulates evidence. He knows too much about the neighborhood. He references a building that was torn down in 1973 as if he walked past it yesterday. He mentions a restaurant that closed before she was born and corrects himself too late. She’s not stupid — she starts keeping a list.”

“A list,” Beverly repeated. “She keeps a list.”

“In the margins of her inventory ledger. Next to the counts of Toni Morrison novels and the note about the leaky pipe in the back room. She writes down every anachronism. And she doesn’t confront him because — why? Because she’s not sure what she’s accusing him of. Being a local history buff? Having a good memory? She doesn’t have a framework for ‘this man might be immortal.’ That’s not a conclusion a rational person reaches.”

Beverly was quiet for a moment. “My characters are always rational people. That’s something I insist on. Black women in fiction are too often asked to be magical thinkers — intuitive, spiritual, in touch with something beyond reason. I won’t do it. She’s a businesswoman. She keeps books. She’s practical. And when the evidence starts piling up, her first instinct isn’t wonder. It’s suspicion.”

“Suspicion of what?” I asked.

“Of him. Of his motives. Why is this man spending so much time in her store? What does he want? Because in her experience, when someone pays that much attention to a Black-owned business in a gentrifying neighborhood, they’re usually scouting it. Figuring out what it’s worth. Planning an offer.”

Casey set down their drink. “Oh. Oh, that’s brutal. She thinks he’s a developer.”

“She thinks he might be working for one. He’s too interested. He asks too many questions about the building, the lease, the history of the block. All the things that would be charming if they were genuine curiosity and predatory if they weren’t.”

“And he’s actually asking because he remembers,” Casey said. “He remembers when the building was something else. He’s not scouting — he’s grieving. And she can’t tell the difference because the behaviors look identical.”

I felt something lock into place. The misunderstanding wasn’t a sitcom contrivance — it was structurally inevitable. Of course an immortal’s nostalgia looks like a developer’s due diligence. Of course a woman fighting to keep her business would read the worst into his questions.

“The confession structure,” I said. “The interview frame. When does that come in?”

Beverly straightened. “Late. It comes in late. He doesn’t confess because he wants to. He confesses because she’s about to throw him out of her life, and the truth — the impossible, ridiculous truth — is the only thing that might stop her.”

“And she doesn’t believe him,” Casey added.

“Of course she doesn’t believe him.”

“So what makes her believe?”

Beverly didn’t answer immediately. She looked at the photograph of Thurston on the wall — the barber, the man who’d owned this space before it became what it is now. “He tells her something no one alive would know. Not about history. About her. About her family. About the building. Something specific and verifiable and impossible.”

“Like what?” Casey pressed.

“I don’t know yet. That’s for the story to find.” Beverly looked at me. “Don’t plan that moment. Let it arrive.”

I nodded, though the planner in me wanted a blueprint. I forced myself to leave it open.

Casey had been fidgeting with their cup. “Can we talk about the aging thing? Because that’s the Time Traveler’s Wife engine, right? She ages. He doesn’t. And in a paranormal romance, that’s usually played as tragic-beautiful. The immortal watches his mortal love grow old. Very poetic. Very male-gaze.”

“It is male-gaze,” Beverly said. “The woman as beautiful object who decays while the man remains perfect. Absolutely not.”

“So how do we do it differently?”

“She doesn’t decay. She changes. She gets gray hair and reading glasses and a bad knee from standing on concrete floors for twenty years. And none of that is tragic. It’s evidence. It’s proof she was here, she did the work, she built the thing. Her body is a receipt.”

Casey grinned. “Her body is a receipt. Beverly. That’s the line.”

“That’s not the line. That’s the idea. The line will be better.”

“The line is always better when you write it.” Casey turned to me. “She should write this part. I’m serious. That image — the body as evidence of a life lived, versus the immortal body as evidence of nothing — that’s hers.”

I held up my hands. “I’m the one writing it. But I’m listening.”

“You’d better be,” Beverly said, and there was no humor in it.

Casey shifted gears. “What about the HFN? Happy for now. Not happily ever after. They choose each other knowing it can’t last forever, because she’s mortal and he’s not. How do we make that feel earned instead of resigned?”

“It’s not resigned if she’s the one who decides,” Beverly said. “She doesn’t accept the terms. She sets them. She says: I know what I’m getting. I know the math. I’m choosing this anyway, and I’m not choosing it because I’m romantic or naive. I’m choosing it because I’ve spent my whole life building something that might not last, and I’ve learned that the building is the point.”

“The building is the point,” Casey echoed. “Literally and metaphorically. The bookstore, the relationship, the neighborhood. None of it’s permanent. All of it matters.”

“But don’t say that in the story,” Beverly said sharply. “Don’t announce it. If you write a line where she says ‘the building is the point,’ I’ll know, and I’ll find you.”

Casey laughed. I laughed. Beverly did not laugh. I believed her.

“One more thing,” Casey said. “The tone. This could go dark. Centuries of watching Black communities get destroyed, gentrification as a present-tense wound, an immortal who’s accumulated more grief than any person should carry. All real, all important. But I need there to be moments where the story is fun. Not light — fun. The difference between them arguing about which Toni Morrison novel is the best entry point for a reluctant reader. The way he keeps almost buying the same book and then putting it back, and she finally just gives it to him because she can’t watch him do it one more time. The small, stupid, human stuff that makes a love story breathable.”

Beverly picked up her coffee, found it cold, set it down. “Fun is fine. Fun is necessary. But fun earns its place by being surrounded by things that aren’t fun. If every scene is enjoyable, nothing is. The argument about Morrison — that’s fun. The scene where she drives past a new luxury condo that replaced the church her grandmother attended — that’s not fun. And the not-fun is what gives the fun its weight.”

“Agreed,” Casey said. “Completely agreed. I just don’t want the not-fun to win.”

“It doesn’t win. She’s still standing at the end. The store is still open. He’s still there, for now. That’s not the not-fun winning. That’s the whole thing.”

I wanted to ask about the structure — how many sections, whether the confession frame should bookend the narrative or interrupt it, whether we should see his perspective at all. But the conversation had reached one of those plateaus where pushing further would break something. They’d given me a woman who fights, a man who remembers, a building that means more than shelter, and a love story that knows its own expiration date and refuses to apologize for existing anyway.

Casey was looking at their phone. “I have another call in twenty minutes.”

“Then go,” Beverly said. “We’re not done, but we’re done for now.”

Casey gathered their tote bag, hesitated. “The bookstore needs a name. A real one. Not something cute or symbolic. A name that sounds like someone’s actual business.”

“It does,” Beverly said. “But that’s not our job. That’s his.” She nodded at me.

They left. Beverly sat for another minute, looking at the photograph of Thurston the barber. I thought she might say something about him — about what it means to sit in a space that used to belong to someone else, drinking expensive coffee where a man once cut hair for prices that never changed. But she just finished her cold coffee, put on her coat, and said, “Don’t make him apologize for being immortal. That’s the trap. Everyone writes the immortal who’s sorry. Write one who’s just tired.”

She left me with the check and the notebook and the faint smell of lavender foam. Through the window I watched her walk past the spot where, according to no record I could find, Thurston’s original barber pole had stood. I wondered if the immortal in our story would remember it. I wondered if remembering was the same as haunting.

The barista asked if I wanted another coffee. I said yes. I sat there for another hour, writing nothing, thinking about buildings and bodies and the kind of love that knows exactly how it ends and begins anyway.
