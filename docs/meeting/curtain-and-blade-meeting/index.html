Adventure / Swashbuckler

Masks, Rapiers, and the Person Underneath

A discussion between Alexandre Dumas and Robert Louis Stevenson

February 28, 2026

The café was Dumas’s choice — a place near the Palais-Royal with marble-topped tables and mirrors on every wall, so that wherever you sat you could see yourself and everyone else from three or four angles. Stevenson noticed this immediately. He chose the chair with its back to the largest mirror, which I think was deliberate.

Dumas arrived carrying a manuscript he hadn’t written — one of those leather portfolios he liked to spread open on the table to claim territory, the way a general lays out maps. Stevenson had brought nothing. His coat was too thin for the weather and he looked like he hadn’t quite decided whether he was here to participate or to observe.

I had a notebook, which felt embarrassing. I put it under my chair.

“So,” Dumas said, rubbing his hands together with a satisfaction I hadn’t yet earned. “We are writing a swashbuckler. I assume you have come to apologize.”

Stevenson raised an eyebrow.

“For Jim Hawkins,” Dumas said. “A boy who goes to sea with pirates and comes home having learned — what, exactly? That pirates are interesting? That morality is complicated? This is not adventure, Louis. This is a travel memoir with a body count.”

“The body count is rather the point,” Stevenson said mildly. He had a way of speaking that made each word sound like it had been considered and then released reluctantly, as if he were always slightly sorry to spend it. “A boy watches a man die and has to figure out what it means without anyone helping him. That seems like adventure to me.”

“Adventure is when D’Artagnan rides from Paris to London in three days to save the Queen’s diamonds! Adventure is action!”

“Adventure is a word we use to avoid saying ‘danger,’” Stevenson said. “And danger is only interesting if we believe it might change someone.”

I tried to get a word in. “I think what we’re looking at is a story that does both — the spectacle and the psychological —”

“Of course,” Dumas said, waving his hand as if this were obvious and therefore beneath discussion. “I have never written a story without psychology. Edmond Dantès is nothing but psychology. He is a man consumed by revenge for twenty years, and I gave him — what — nine hundred pages to work it out? The psychology is in the plot. It is the plot. Every time he springs another trap on one of his enemies, we see what the revenge is costing him, because we remember the boy he was.”

“And yet,” Stevenson said, turning his wine glass slowly, “you would not call that a cost in the way I mean. For you, Alexandre, the cost of revenge is that one becomes magnificent and lonely. For me, the cost of an adventure is that one becomes — split. One half remains the person one was. The other half becomes something strange.” He paused. “Jekyll and Hyde. Long John Silver, who is both friend and murderer. The performance divides the person performing it.”

That landed. I could see Dumas thinking. He didn’t like being told what his own work meant by someone else, but Stevenson had touched something real.

“Let me put it this way,” I said, sensing an opening. “We have a disguise at the center of this story. A rescue mission during a revolution — echoes of the Scarlet Pimpernel. The hero is playing a role. Dumas, you want the role to be exhilarating. Stevenson, you want the role to consume the person.”

“I want the role to succeed,” Dumas said.

“I want us to worry about what happens after it succeeds,” Stevenson said.

We sat with that for a moment. The café clattered around us — cups on saucers, chairs scraping back, a woman laughing at something near the window. Dumas refilled his wine from a bottle I hadn’t seen him order.

“Tell me about the Pimpernel,” Stevenson said. “What interests you in that model?”

“The performance,” I said. “Sir Percy Blakeney plays the fool — the fop, the idiot, the man who cares about nothing but his cravat — so that nobody suspects he’s risking his life every night to save people from the guillotine. The disguise isn’t just a mask. It’s a complete person, a character he inhabits. And the cost — at least in Orczy’s version — is that his own wife doesn’t know who he really is.”

“That is magnificent,” Dumas said.

“That is horrifying,” Stevenson said.

Dumas looked at Stevenson as though he were being deliberately difficult, which he was.

“Consider,” Stevenson said. “A man who is so committed to his disguise that the woman he loves cannot see him. Not because the disguise is clever — because he has decided that saving strangers matters more than being known by the person closest to him. That is not heroism. That is a pathology.”

“It is absolutely heroism,” Dumas said. “Personal sacrifice for a greater cause. Athos gives up love for honor. Porthos gives up comfort for his friends. Every musketeer gives up something private for something public. This is what swashbucklers are about.”

“And what do they get back?”

Dumas frowned. “They get the adventure.”

“Right,” Stevenson said. “The adventure. Which is a thing that happens to you while you’re losing something else.” He looked at me. “Your story needs to understand that. The rescue mission isn’t the story. The rescue mission is the mechanism by which the real story — the loss — occurs.”

I wrote this down. It felt important. But I also felt Dumas pulling back, and I didn’t want to lose him, because he was right about something too.

“Alexandre,” I said. “Help me with the ensemble. The story can’t just be one person brooding in a mask. I need — bodies in motion. I need the breathless quality of the Musketeers, where five things are happening at once and every character has their own scheme.”

His eyes lit up. “Yes. Good. The hero is never alone. The Pimpernel had his League. D’Artagnan had his three. Your hero needs people who depend on him, people who have their own reasons for being in this fight, people who might betray him or surprise him or save him at the wrong moment.”

“The wrong moment,” Stevenson repeated, with something like appreciation.

“The wrong moment is the best moment in adventure fiction,” Dumas said. “The rescue that comes too late or too early. The friend who arrives just when you’ve decided to do the wrong thing. Timing is everything — not because it creates suspense, but because it reveals character. What a man does when he has thirty seconds to decide is who he actually is.”

“We agree on that,” Stevenson said. He sounded slightly annoyed about it.

“And The Princess Bride,” I said. “The other structural source. Goldman’s trick is that he loves the genre while bending it. The swordfight between Inigo and Westley isn’t a parody — it’s the most sincere scene in the book. They fight with absolute skill and absolute respect, and the choreography reveals who they are. We need a fight like that.”

“Every fight should reveal character,” Dumas said. “How a man fights tells you everything. D’Artagnan fights recklessly because he is young and believes he cannot die. Athos fights precisely because he no longer cares whether he lives. Give me a sword fight in which both combatants are telling the truth for the first time, and I will give you the best scene in the story.”

“A sword fight in which both combatants are telling the truth,” Stevenson repeated. “Yes. I like that. But what if one of them doesn’t know which truth they’re telling? What if the disguise has gone so deep that the hero fights as the character, not as himself — and wins, but wins as the wrong person?”

I felt something shift. The story was beginning to take shape, not as a plot but as a problem. A hero who rescues people through performance, who is brilliant at being someone else, who builds an ensemble around a version of himself that may not be the real one. And a sword fight where the mask cracks — or worse, where the mask wins.

“There should be a woman,” Dumas said. “Not a love interest. A woman who is essential. The person the hero is trying to save, or the person who knows the truth about the hero, or both. In The Princess Bride, Buttercup is the engine of the plot. Without her, Westley has no reason to return from the dead.”

“Buttercup is passive,” Stevenson said. “She waits and is beautiful and nearly dies.”

“She is loved,” Dumas said, as though correcting him. “And being loved is doing something, whether or not you hold a sword.”

“That is very French,” Stevenson said.

“Thank you.”

“It was not entirely a compliment.” Stevenson turned to me. “If there is a woman at the center of this story, she should have her own secret. She should be performing too. Not in competition with the hero — in parallel. Two people who cannot be honest with each other because they are both too busy being brave.”

“And the revolution,” I said. “The Scarlet Pimpernel’s stakes come from the Terror — the guillotine, the tumbrils, the systematic murder. Our story needs that weight. Not as backdrop. The revolution should be the thing that makes the disguise necessary and the thing that makes the disguise destructive.”

“The revolution is always the most interesting character,” Dumas said. “I wrote about 1793. I wrote about the Fronde. Every revolution is a machine for revealing what people will do when the rules disappear. Your hero’s disguise only works because the revolution has made everyone suspicious — and suspicion is the mother of performance.”

“Or the other way around,” Stevenson said. “Performance is the mother of suspicion. Once you know that anyone might be pretending, trust becomes impossible. And trust is what your hero needs most — from the person he’s trying to save.”

“So the rescue destroys the relationship,” I said.

“The rescue reveals that the relationship was built on a performance,” Stevenson said. “Which is worse.”

Dumas shook his head. “Not worse. More interesting. The question is whether the relationship can survive the revelation. In my stories, it usually can, because loyalty is stronger than knowledge. Athos learns terrible things about everyone and forgives them, because the bond matters more than the facts.”

“In my stories,” Stevenson said, “it usually can’t. Because knowledge changes the person who receives it. Jim Hawkins cannot un-know that Silver is a murderer. He can only decide what to do next.”

“So which story are we writing?” I asked.

They looked at each other. For a moment neither spoke. Outside, a street musician was playing something on an accordion that was either very beautiful or very bad — I couldn’t tell from inside.

“Both,” Dumas said.

“Neither,” Stevenson said.

“The story where we don’t yet know the answer,” I said.

Stevenson almost smiled. “That’s the only kind worth writing.”

Dumas drained his wine and reached for his leather portfolio. “I want the rescue to be spectacular. I want three disguises, each nested inside the other. I want a swordfight on a staircase. I want the hero’s friends to each have one perfect moment — a line, a gesture, a refusal.”

“And I want the hero to win the rescue and lose something he didn’t know he was risking,” Stevenson said. “I want the mask to come off and for everyone — including the hero — to be unsure who’s underneath.”

“We should talk about endings,” I said.

“Must we?” Dumas asked.

“No,” Stevenson said. “The ending will tell us what it wants when we get there. That’s how stories work, in my experience. You build the engine and it drives itself off a cliff you didn’t see.”

“This is why the Scots should not be trusted with adventure fiction,” Dumas said, but he was smiling. “You build beautiful machines and then crash them into the rocks.”

“And you,” Stevenson said, “build beautiful machines and insist they can fly.”

I gathered my notebook from under the chair. It was full of things I hadn’t written down — images, tensions, the feel of two writers disagreeing about what courage costs and what performance means and whether a rescue can save someone who doesn’t want to be saved by a stranger wearing the face of the person they loved.

The accordion player outside had stopped. The café was emptying. Dumas was already talking about dinner, and Stevenson was buttoning his thin coat with the careful attention of a man who knows the cold is waiting.
