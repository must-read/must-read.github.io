Two Men Who Knew Dirt and One Who Didn't

A discussion between Juan Rulfo and Wendell Berry

March 1, 2026

We met at a table outside a cantina in Ciudad Obregon, though the cantina was closed and the table belonged to no one. It was November, already hot by eight in the morning. The Yaqui Valley spread south from the town in a pale haze of dust and irrigation canals, the fields flat and immense and silent in the way that only land being actively farmed can be silent — not empty, but held. Berry had arrived the night before from Kentucky, Rulfo from Mexico City, and both of them looked like they hadn’t slept, though for different reasons. Berry because he’d been reading on the plane. Rulfo because he didn’t sleep well away from his own bed, or perhaps because he didn’t sleep well anywhere, which was more likely.

I’d brought coffee from the hotel. It was bad coffee. Nobody mentioned it.

“I want to write about a man who believes steel plows steal warmth from the earth,” I said. “A farmer in this valley, 1952. A government man comes with new seed. The farmer has to decide whether to plant it.”

Rulfo looked at the valley. He didn’t speak for a while. Then: “That man would not think of it as a decision.”

“What do you mean?”

“A decision implies two things of equal weight. For this farmer, the seed from outside is not equal. It is foreign matter. You don’t decide about foreign matter. You wait for it to leave.”

Berry shifted in his chair. He was a larger man than I’d expected — not fat, but substantial, with the kind of hands that suggest they’ve recently been doing something. “I’d push back on that a little. The farmer I’m imagining — and maybe this is where Juan and I part ways early — he knows the seed will grow. He’s not stupid. He’s seen what happens in other men’s fields. His resistance isn’t ignorance. It’s loyalty.”

“Loyalty to what?” I asked.

“To the relationship. You farm the same ground your father farmed, you’re not just growing wheat. You’re continuing a conversation. The soil knows you. That sounds mystical and it isn’t. It’s practical. You’ve learned that particular ground over decades — where it drains, where it holds, what it wants in October versus April. You bring in a new seed developed in a laboratory and you’re introducing a stranger to the conversation.”

Rulfo said, “It is not a conversation. You are both too gentle about this. The farmer does not converse with his field. He submits to it. The field tells him what it will produce, and he accepts. This is not Berry’s dialogue between equals. The land is not his partner. The land is his condition.”

“That’s a real difference between us,” Berry said, and he said it without rancor, like a man noting that the wind had shifted.

I sat with that for a moment. The distinction felt important — Berry’s farmer in dialogue with the land, Rulfo’s farmer subject to it. Both true, neither complete. “So who is this man?” I said. “Is he Berry’s farmer or Rulfo’s?”

“He’s Mexican,” Rulfo said. “That settles part of it.”

Berry laughed, a short sound. “Fair enough. But the question of agency matters for the story. If this farmer is purely subject to the land, then the arrival of the new seed is fate, and he submits to that too, and there’s no story. There has to be friction.”

“There is always friction,” Rulfo said. “You don’t need to manufacture it. The friction is that his father is dead and still telling him what to do.”

This was the first mention of the dead father, and it landed on the table between us like something physical. I’d sketched it in my notes — the farmer talks to his dead father — but I’d imagined it as a literary device, a way of externalizing internal conflict. Rulfo was describing something else.

“Tell me what you mean,” I said. “The dead father. Is it a ghost? A memory?”

“It’s how he thinks,” Rulfo said. “When you grow up with one person teaching you everything about the world — this is how you plant, this is how you read the sky, this is what the soil wants — that person’s voice doesn’t leave when they die. It becomes the voice you think in. The farmer doesn’t summon his father. His father is the structure of his thought.”

“That’s beautiful,” Berry said.

“It’s not beautiful. It’s a problem. Because the father is also wrong. The father believed steel plows steal warmth. The father drove cattle across plowed fields to put the heat back. These are things that don’t work. And the farmer knows they don’t work — has known for years, probably — but he can’t think past his father’s voice to admit it, because admitting it means his father was wrong, and if his father was wrong about the plow then maybe his father was wrong about everything, and then what is left?”

I felt something cold move through the conversation. “So the new seed isn’t just a threat to his farming practice. It’s a threat to his father.”

“To his father’s authority,” Rulfo said. “Which is the same as his own foundation.”

Berry was quiet for a long time. He was looking at his hands on the table. When he spoke, his voice was slower than before, more careful. “I want to be honest about something. My instinct — my whole body of work’s instinct — is to side with the farmer. To say that the local knowledge is the real knowledge and the laboratory seed is an intrusion. And for most of my career I’ve been right about that, or at least defensible. But this particular story defeats me. Because the seed works. The seed feeds people. Not theoretically. Millions of people who were hungry stopped being hungry because of what happened in this valley. And if I write this farmer as a hero of traditional knowledge, I’m sentimentalizing a man whose children might have starved.”

“They wouldn’t have starved,” Rulfo said. “They would have continued as they were. Thin. Getting by. Not starving. The distinction matters because starvation is dramatic and getting-by is not. The seed didn’t rescue this farmer from death. It rescued his grandchildren from his life.”

“Christ,” Berry said.

“That’s the sentence,” I said, and wrote it down. “The seed rescued his grandchildren from his life.”

Rulfo waved a hand. “Don’t use it. It’s too neat. It explains too much.”

He was right. I crossed it out.

“What about the extension agent?” I said. “The government man who brings the seed. Who is he?”

“Young,” Berry said immediately. “Educated. From the city. He has data. He has a college degree in agronomy and a genuine belief that he’s helping. He is helping. That’s what makes him impossible.”

“He can’t hear the farmer,” I said.

“He hears the farmer perfectly. He just doesn’t recognize what the farmer is saying as information. The farmer says ‘the steel plow takes the warmth from the soil.’ The extension agent hears superstition. What the farmer is actually saying — though he’d never put it this way — is that the old wooden plow disturbed less of the soil microbiome, maintained more moisture and organic heat in the top layer, and that heavy tillage with steel changed the texture of the ground in ways his hands could feel but his vocabulary couldn’t name.”

Rulfo shook his head. “You’re translating him again. You’re making the farmer into a scientist who lacks the proper terminology. He’s not a scientist. He believes the plow steals warmth because his father told him the plow steals warmth. Some of it happens to correspond to microbiological reality. Some of it doesn’t. He also believes the moon affects planting, which it does not, and that a rooster crowing at midnight means someone nearby will die, which it also does not.”

“Does the rooster matter for the story?” I asked.

“The rooster matters because you need the reader to understand that this farmer’s knowledge is not secretly scientific. It’s something else entirely. It’s coherent on its own terms. Some of it overlaps with what the extension agent knows, and some of it doesn’t, and the farmer himself cannot tell you which parts are which, because for him it’s all one thing. All one inheritance.”

Berry nodded slowly, and I could see it was costing him something. “That’s right. And that’s what gets lost. Not the individual beliefs — some wrong, some right — but the coherence. The fact that it’s one thing. The new seed breaks the coherence. After the new seed, knowledge is divided into the kind that can be verified in a laboratory and the kind that can’t, and the second kind loses its authority, and the farmer is no longer a man who knows his ground. He’s a man with outdated techniques and a few lucky guesses.”

“Does he plant the seed?” I asked.

“He plants the seed,” Rulfo said. “History says so. Everyone planted the seed.”

“But the story has to earn that moment,” Berry said. “It can’t be inevitable. The reader needs to feel the weight of what he’s giving up.”

“It is inevitable,” Rulfo said. “That is the weight.”

I understood what he meant, and I also understood that Berry was right, and that the two positions were not compatible, and I would have to hold them both.

“The dead father,” I said. “Does he tell the farmer not to plant?”

Rulfo said, “The dead father tells the farmer what he has always told him. He says: the soil is warm because I kept it warm. He says: this is how you hold a plow. He says: when you plant, you are doing what I did, and when I planted, I was doing what my father did. He does not address the new seed directly, because the new seed is outside his vocabulary. He cannot oppose something he has no words for.”

“That’s devastating,” Berry said.

“It is accurate,” Rulfo said, which was, I suspected, his way of agreeing.

We sat with the coffee for a while. A truck went past on the road, loaded with something covered by a blue tarp. The valley was brighter now, the haze burning off, and I could see irrigation channels catching the light in thin silver lines. Somewhere out there — sixty, seventy years ago — a man had stood in a field exactly like this one and held a sack of seed he didn’t trust. I wanted to write him without making him a lesson. Berry wanted that too. Rulfo wanted something harder, something that didn’t comfort anyone.

“I keep thinking about the cattle,” I said. “He drove cattle across a plowed field to put the warmth back. That’s an image.”

“It’s the whole story,” Berry said. “The belief that you could undo what the machine had done by sending living bodies over the ground. That’s not crazy. That’s a theology.”

“It didn’t work,” Rulfo said.

“That doesn’t make it not a theology.”

“We are not writing a theology. We are writing about a man in a field.”

Berry turned to me. “Here’s what I want you to resist. The easy shape. The farmer resists, the farmer wrestles, the farmer accepts. That’s a conversion narrative. It’s what the extension service wrote in their reports. It’s not what happened inside the man.”

“What happened inside the man?”

“I don’t know. Nobody asked him. That’s sort of the point.”

Rulfo had been folding a napkin into smaller and smaller squares. He set it on the table, a tight white block. “The story should end with the seed in the ground. Not with the man’s feelings about the seed in the ground. The planting itself. The physical act. His hands. The dirt. Whatever he is thinking at that moment — whatever his father is saying or not saying — the reader does not need to be told. The ground has the seed. That is an ending.”

“But —” I started.

“There is no but. You are afraid of silence. Writers are always afraid of silence. You want the last paragraph to mean something. Let the dirt mean something. You don’t have to help it.”

Berry smiled, and it was one of those smiles that contains a concession so deep it barely shows on the surface. “He’s right about the ending. I hate that he’s right about the ending.”

I looked at my notes. I had a farmer, a dead father, a sack of seed, an earnest young man from the government, and a valley full of light. I did not have a plot, exactly. I had something better and worse: a set of tensions that no structure could fully contain. The farmer who cannot betray his father without becoming modern. The extension agent who cannot save anyone without destroying something. The dead man who goes on speaking in a world that no longer answers.

“One more thing,” Rulfo said. He was standing now, pushing his chair back. “The farmer’s name. Don’t give him a name that means something. Don’t call him Santiago or Esperanza. Call him something plain. Something his mother chose because she liked how it sounded.”

“Roque,” I said, from nowhere.

Rulfo considered it. “Roque Almeida. His father was Tomás.”

Berry reached across and picked up the folded napkin. Unfolded it. It was just a napkin again. “I keep thinking about what Tomás would say if he could see the wheat that grew from that seed. Twice the height of the old strain. Gold like nothing he’d ever —”

“He would say it wasn’t his wheat,” Rulfo said, already walking toward the road.
