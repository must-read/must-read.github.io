The Loyal Instrument

A discussion between John le Carré and Patricia Highsmith

February 21, 2026

The hotel bar had been David’s choice. Not the grand lobby bar with its marble columns and tourists, but a small annex off the restaurant — leather banquettes worn smooth and a window that looked onto a parking garage. He’d arrived twenty minutes early, ordered a whisky he hadn’t touched, and arranged himself in the corner seat with his back to the wall. Old habits. He apologized for it when I sat down, though I noticed he didn’t move.

Patricia was late by ten minutes and unapologetic. She ordered a gin and tonic with no ice and no lime, and when the waiter looked confused she said, “Just the gin and the tonic water. I don’t need decoration.” She settled into the opposite banquette with the contained stillness of someone accustomed to watching.

I sat between them, which felt both appropriate and uncomfortable. I had my notes. I had my ideas. I suspected neither would matter much within the hour.

“So,” David said, turning the whisky glass in a slow circle. “You want to write about a man who doesn’t know what he’s done.”

“That’s part of it,” I said. “A retired intelligence officer reviewing a cold case file from decades ago. He thinks he’s reconstructing what went wrong with an operation, but the reader gradually understands that the operation went exactly right — and he was its instrument.”

“Its unwitting instrument,” Patricia said. “That’s the word you’re dancing around. Unwitting.”

“Yes.”

She sipped her gin. “The question that interests me is not whether he discovers the truth. It’s what he does with the five minutes before the discovery, when the shape of it is already pressing against the inside of his skull but he hasn’t yet allowed himself to see it. That liminal minute. That’s where the story lives.”

David leaned back. “I’d push against that. The story lives in the thirty years before. In the ordinary mornings and the competent paperwork and the drinks with colleagues who knew, some of them, what he’d been used for. The texture of a career built on a lie that no one needed to speak aloud.”

“You want to write the whole career?”

“I want the reader to feel the weight of it. A cold case file isn’t just documents. It’s the residue of institutional time. Every memo carries the fingerprints of the person who decided to route it that way rather than another. You can read power in the circulation lists.”

I’d expected this split — David pulling toward the institutional, Patricia toward the psychological — but hearing it articulated so cleanly made me realize I hadn’t yet decided which story I was writing. “Could it be both? The institutional texture as the mechanism through which the psychological revelation occurs?”

“Everything is both,” Patricia said, which was not helpful. She seemed to register this. “What I mean is: the documents are the man. He filed them. He routed them. He initialed them in his tidy hand. When he reads them now, thirty years on, he’s reading his own complicity in a language he invented for himself. The institution didn’t do this to him. He and the institution did it together. That’s what makes it unbearable.”

David set down his glass. “That’s not quite right either. The institution did do it to him — that’s the whole machinery of intelligence work. You are shaped by the service. Your instincts are trained. Your moral reflexes are conditioned until you genuinely cannot distinguish between what you believe and what you’ve been taught to believe. To say he did it together with the institution implies a partnership of equals, and it never is.”

“But he chose the service.”

“Did he? In 1962? A certain kind of young man from a certain kind of background — what was the alternative? Selling insurance? He chose the service the way a river chooses its bed.”

Patricia smiled at that. It was not a warm smile. “David, you are very generous to your characters. You always have been. You give them the excuse of the institution, the excuse of the era, the excuse of their class and their fathers and the particular quality of gray light in an English November. I think this man chose. I think he chose every day for thirty years, and the choosing was so habitual it became invisible to him, and that is the horror.”

I watched David absorb this. Something shifted behind his eyes — not agreement, but the recognition of a worthy objection. “The horror,” he repeated. “You’d make it a horror story.”

“All espionage is horror. You know that better than anyone. You just dress it in melancholy.”

There was a silence that lasted longer than either of them seemed comfortable with. I broke it, clumsily. “What if the reader understands before he does? Not through some dramatic reveal, but through the accumulation of small details that he keeps misreading?”

David nodded slowly. “That’s the Tinker Tailor structure, in a way. The reader assembles the picture from fragments the narrator provides without understanding their significance. But — and this is crucial — the narrator must provide those fragments honestly. He’s not hiding anything. He’s showing you everything. He simply can’t see what it means.”

“Like a butler,” I said, and then felt foolish for being so direct about it.

But Patricia leaned forward. “Say more about that.”

“Ishiguro’s Stevens. He gives you every piece of evidence you need to understand that he wasted his life in service to a fascist sympathizer, and he does it while maintaining perfect dignity. The dignity is the blindness. They’re the same thing.”

“Yes,” Patricia said. “That’s much closer to what I want. Not a man who’s been deceived — a man who deceived himself, and built an entire architecture of professionalism around the deception so that it became indistinguishable from competence.”

David shifted. “I resist the word self-deception. It’s too psychological. Too clean. What I’ve observed — and I spent rather a long time observing these people — is something more structural. The service provides a grammar. Subject, verb, object: we identified the threat, we designed the operation, we neutralized the target. Within that grammar, certain questions are not speakable. Not forbidden, precisely. Just… ungrammatical. You wouldn’t think to ask them any more than you’d think to put the verb before the subject in English.”

“But someone designed that grammar,” Patricia said. “Someone benefits from the fact that certain questions are unspeakable.”

“Of course. And our man might even be the one who designed it. That’s what makes the cold case so poisonous — he might find his own handwriting on the memo that closed the door he’s now trying to open.”

I wrote that down. I wrote it down twice, actually, because the first time my hand was shaking slightly and I couldn’t read it. “His own handwriting on the memo. That’s — can we talk about the physical experience of that moment? He’s sitting in a room, probably his study at home, retired, with these old files. He turns a page and sees his own initials.”

“He doesn’t react,” David said immediately. “That’s essential. He turns the page. He sees the initials. He moves on to the next document. Because the initials don’t mean what you and I think they mean — not yet. To him, they’re simply confirmation that the file passed through his desk, as hundreds of files passed through his desk. The significance is there, but it’s inert. It’s a bullet that hasn’t been fired.”

Patricia shook her head. “No. He does react. His hand hesitates. A fraction of a second. He turns the page slightly too quickly. The body knows before the mind does. It always does. The question is whether you show the reader the hesitation or make them imagine it.”

“Show it,” I said. “But through the prose rhythm, not through description. A sentence that stutters where the others don’t.”

Patricia looked at me with what might have been approval, though with her it was hard to tell. “That could work. The body as text.”

David was quiet for a moment, staring out the window at the concrete wall of the parking garage. “There’s something else we haven’t addressed. The question of what the operation actually was. What did they use him for? Because if it’s too dramatic — an assassination, a betrayal that killed people — we’re in thriller territory, and the emotional register shifts. It becomes about guilt rather than something more interesting.”

“What’s more interesting than guilt?” Patricia asked, and I genuinely couldn’t tell if she was being provocative or sincere.

“Waste,” David said. “The discovery that your life’s work was a component in something that didn’t even have the decency to be evil. Something merely expedient. A bureaucratic adjustment. He was used, yes, but not for anything grand. Not to betray his country or destroy an enemy. To slightly improve the position of one faction within the service at the expense of another. Internal politics dressed up as operational necessity.”

“That’s devastating,” I said.

“It’s the truth. Most intelligence work is.”

Patricia set down her glass. “I disagree. I think the operation should have a body. Maybe not a death — maybe a ruined life. Someone who was hung out to dry because of what our man did. I want there to be a face. Not because I’m interested in guilt, but because the story needs a mirror. Our man looks into the file and sees his own handwriting, yes. But he also needs to see someone else’s name. Someone he sent down the river without knowing he was doing it.”

“Or someone he knew he was sending but told himself it was necessary.”

“That’s your version, David. In mine, he genuinely didn’t know. The architecture of the service was designed so that he wouldn’t know. He signed the memo. Someone else connected the dots. The outcome was three desks away from his signature.”

I was scribbling fast. “So we have two possible structures. In David’s version, the man’s complicity is structural — he participated in a system whose grammar made certain outcomes invisible. In Patricia’s, there’s a specific consequence with a specific face, and the file eventually connects his signature to that face.”

“They’re not incompatible,” David said, surprising both of us.

Patricia raised an eyebrow. “Go on.”

“The system is as I described. The grammar makes the questions unspeakable. But within that system, there is one particular file, one particular name, one particular outcome. And our man — when he finds it, decades later — he does what any trained intelligence officer would do. He contextualizes it. He places it within the larger operation. He finds twelve reasons why it was necessary, or unavoidable, or someone else’s decision. And every one of those reasons is true. And none of them is sufficient.”

Patricia was quiet for a long time. Then she said something I didn’t expect. “I knew a man like this. Not intelligence. A publisher. He’d dropped an author from his list in the fifties — not for political reasons, not consciously. The author’s sales were declining. Market forces, he said. Years later it came out that someone at the firm had been quietly steering reviewers away from the author’s books. My publisher friend hadn’t known. Or had known and forgotten. Or had known and classified the knowing as something else. When I asked him about it, he said, ‘These things happened.’ As if ‘these things’ were weather.”

“‘These things happened,’” David repeated. “That’s the voice. That passive construction. Things happened. Not: I did this. Not even: it was done. Just: things happened.”

I felt something click into place — not a plot, not a structure, but a tone. The voice of a man describing his own actions in the passive tense, and not as evasion but as genuine perception. He really does see it that way. The operations happened. The files were routed. The outcomes occurred. Agency dissolved into procedure long ago.

David ordered a second whisky, which surprised me — I’d assumed the first was purely ornamental. “There’s a question of age,” he said. “How old is this man now? Because the relationship between a man and his past changes as he ages. At sixty, you can still tell yourself the story of your career as a narrative of purpose. At seventy-five, the narrative starts to feel like someone else wrote it.”

“Seventy-three,” Patricia said, with a specificity that made both of us look at her. “Old enough that the body has started its own betrayals. A knee that aches. Reading glasses he keeps misplacing. The physical deterioration mirrors the epistemological one — his tools for understanding the world are failing him at the same moment the world is asking to be re-understood.”

“That’s good,” I said. “The reading glasses especially. He’s literally struggling to see the fine print.”

“Don’t make it a metaphor,” Patricia said sharply. “The moment you make it a metaphor, it dies. He has bad eyes. He needs glasses. He misplaces them because he’s seventy-three and people misplace things. If the reader draws a parallel, that’s the reader’s intelligence, not yours. Never do the reader’s work for them.”

David was smiling — the first real smile of the evening. “She’s right. The worst thing you can do to a symbol is notice it.”

“Can I ask something that might be stupid?” I said. “Should he ever understand? At the end, I mean. Should there be a moment where he sees?”

“No,” they said, simultaneously, and then looked at each other with something like amusement.

“Absolutely not,” Patricia continued. “The reader understands. The man does not. That’s the whole architecture. If he understands, you’ve written a redemption story. You’ve given him the dignity of self-knowledge, and he hasn’t earned it.”

David nodded. “In Tinker Tailor, Smiley understands. That’s what makes it a detective story. Here, the investigation succeeds — in the sense that the reader can reconstruct what happened — but the investigator fails. He’s too close. He can’t read his own handwriting.”

“He can read it,” Patricia said. “He just can’t hear what it’s saying.”

I wanted to argue. Some part of me wanted the character to break through, to have the moment of recognition, even if it destroyed him. I said as much.

David looked at me not unkindly. “That’s the writer’s temptation. You love your character, so you want to give him the truth. But the truth is a gift he can’t use. It would just sit in his hands like a grenade with the pin pulled. Better — more honest — to let him set down the file, pour himself a drink, and go to bed believing he’s done good work. The reader carries the grenade home.”

Patricia finished her gin. “The reader carries everything home. That’s the job.”

There was another pause. I found myself thinking about the physical files — the paper itself. “What does the study look like? Where he’s doing this work?”

“Orderly,” David said at once. “Compulsively so. A man who has organized his retirement the way he organized his professional life. The files are in banker’s boxes, labeled. His desk is clear except for the document he’s reading and a notepad. He works methodically, left to right, top to bottom. He is, in his own mind, conducting a proper review.”

“And his wife?” Patricia asked. “Is there a wife?”

I hadn’t thought about a wife. David had, apparently. “Dead. Or divorced, which in this context amounts to the same thing — an absence that structures the space. The study exists because there’s no one to share the house with. The cold case exists because retirement is a country without borders, and he needs borders.”

“Not dead,” Patricia said. “Still alive. Living somewhere else. They speak on the phone occasionally. Cordial. The marriage didn’t end in drama — it simply emptied out, the way a house empties when you remove one piece of furniture at a time. One day you look around and there’s nothing left but the carpet and the marks where the sofa used to be.”

David was turning his fresh whisky glass now, the same slow rotation as before. “That’s good. The marriage as another operation that succeeded on its own terms and failed on every other.”

“Don’t connect them,” Patricia said. “Don’t draw the line between the professional failure and the personal one. Let them exist in parallel. The reader will do the arithmetic.”

She signaled for the check. David still hadn’t touched the second whisky either — two untouched whiskies now, standing like sentries on the dark wood. I had seventeen pages of notes and the growing suspicion that I’d been having the wrong conversation — that the story they’d described was not the story I’d walked in planning to write, and that theirs was better, and that this realization was itself a small demonstration of the theme we’d been circling: the moment when you discover that the operation you thought you were running was actually running you.

I said something about the Ishiguro connection — the butler’s evening, the drive home through the darkening countryside — but Patricia was already putting on her coat, and David was watching a woman cross the parking garage with the abstracted attention of someone who has spent a lifetime noticing things without appearing to notice them.

“One more thing,” Patricia said from the doorway. “Don’t make him sympathetic. Make him recognizable. Those are different things, and only one of them is honest.”

David raised his untouched whisky in her direction. Whether it was agreement or farewell, I couldn’t tell.
