Horror / Folk Horror

Hospitality and Its Instruments

A discussion between Robert Aickman and Mariana Enriquez

February 27, 2026

We met in a restaurant in Belgrano that Enriquez had chosen, a place with blue tiles on the walls and too many tables pushed too close together, so that our conversation kept being interrupted by the elbows and laughter of strangers. Aickman sat very upright in his chair and had ordered a glass of white wine that arrived warm. He did not complain about the temperature, but he held the glass by the stem at a slight distance from himself, as if preserving the possibility that he might send it back later. Enriquez was drinking a Fernet and Coke and had already eaten most of the bread.

“The problem with folk horror,” she said, tearing the last piece of bread in half, “is that the English invented it, and the English version is always about a man from London who drives to the country and discovers that the peasants are doing something dreadful. And the audience is supposed to identify with the man from London. And the peasants exist to be dreadful. And this is, at its root, a colonial story wearing a mask.”

I expected Aickman to object. He didn’t. He turned the wine glass a quarter turn and said, “You are not entirely wrong.”

“I’m not wrong at all.”

“You are not wrong about a certain kind of folk horror. The Wicker Man. The sort of thing that flatters the educated viewer into believing that their education would save them from the locals. But that is a lesser tradition, and I do not write in it.” His voice was measured — not cold, but deliberate, each word placed like a card on a table. “In my stories, the protagonist’s education is precisely the thing that fails. Not because the locals are cleverer. Because cleverness is irrelevant to the situation. The protagonist cannot solve the story because the story is not a puzzle. It is a condition.”

“A condition,” Enriquez repeated, chewing this over along with the bread. “Like a disease.”

“Like weather. You do not solve weather. You endure it, or you are undone by it, and understanding its mechanics does not change your relationship to it in any practical sense.”

I had been taking notes, but I stopped, because I realized I was trying to turn their conversation into a plan, and that was exactly what I’d been told not to do. So I put the pen down and said something that had been bothering me since I’d read the brief. “The British academic. The woman who goes to Argentina for this festival. I keep worrying that she’s the man from London. That she’s the colonial observer, and the story is just another version of ‘outsider finds the natives alarming.’”

Enriquez pointed at me with the hand holding the bread. “Yes. That’s the trap. And there is only one way to avoid it.”

“Which is?”

“Make her wrong about being an outsider. She thinks she is separate from what is happening. She is not. She was invited. The invitation is not random. The colleague who invited her — this is important — the colleague did not choose her because she is convenient. The colleague chose her because she is correct. She is the right person. And that should terrify her more than any bonfire or any knife, but it won’t, because she does not believe she can be chosen. She believes in her own separateness the way the English believe in their own separateness, which is to say, absolutely, and without evidence.”

Aickman set down his wine. He had not drunk from it. “I want to be careful here,” he said, and the care was audible — a tightening, as though the conversation had come closer to something he did not wish to touch carelessly. “You are describing something I recognize. The protagonist who cannot accept that the story is about them. In my work, this manifests as a kind of bewilderment. The character experiences the uncanny and responds not with terror but with social embarrassment. They feel they are being rude by noticing. The English disease, if you like — the suspicion that acknowledging the impossible is a breach of etiquette.”

“That’s it,” I said, because it was. “That’s her. She’s at this festival and things are wrong — not violently wrong, not yet — but the rhythms are off, the hospitality is too insistent, the schedule of events doesn’t correspond to any folk tradition she’s studied, and her response is to feel ashamed of her own suspicion. Because these people have been so generous. Because she’s a guest. Because to name what she’s feeling would be — ”

“Ungrateful,” Enriquez said.

“Racist,” I said.

They both looked at me.

“I mean — that’s what she’d be afraid of. Not the word, but the substance. She’s an academic. She studies folk traditions. She respects these practices. And the moment she begins to think something is wrong, she has to contend with the possibility that her unease is just another version of the colonial gaze. The educated European who finds indigenous ritual threatening. She’s spent her career arguing against that reflex, and now it’s happening to her, and she cannot tell if the reflex is bigotry or survival instinct.”

There was a pause. A waiter squeezed between our table and the next, carrying three plates of milanesas at precarious angles. Enriquez waited until he passed, then leaned forward.

“This is good,” she said, “but you are making it too psychological. You are putting the horror inside her head. In my stories, the horror is never only inside. It is in the soil. It is in the economics. It is in the fact that this village exists at all, in the particular way it exists — the poverty, the history, the things that were done to these people and the things they did in return. The festival is not a metaphor for something. The festival is the thing itself. It has a function. And the function is material.”

“What function?” I asked.

“I don’t know yet. But it must be real. Not symbolic. The crops grow because of it, or the river doesn’t flood, or the children don’t die. Something that the community has tested against centuries of evidence, and the evidence supports them, and the academic’s theories about cultural performance and ritual signification are, to these people, as irrelevant as a weather forecast is to the weather.”

Aickman made a small sound — not quite agreement, closer to the noise a person makes when they encounter an idea that reorganizes something they already believed. “You want the supernatural to be functional,” he said. “Operative. Not decorative.”

“I want it to be necessary. In Argentina, in the north, in the provinces — there are places where the relationship between the living and the dead is not a belief system. It is an infrastructure. The dead are maintained the way roads are maintained. You tend them or things fall apart. This is not superstition. This is municipal engineering with different tools.”

“And yet,” Aickman said, and here his voice took on the quality I knew from his stories — a kind of measured retreat from certainty, as though the sentence itself were backing away from something — “and yet, the story must not explain this. Must not make it legible. Because the moment you explain the mechanism, you have turned it into a technology, and technologies can be evaluated, and the protagonist becomes a quality inspector rather than a sacrifice.”

“I’m not saying explain it. I’m saying root it.”

“Those are different things?”

“Absolutely. I can root a story in the poverty of Santiago del Estero without explaining why the dead are restless there. The reader feels the weight of the place — the drought, the debt, the distance from Buenos Aires that is not merely geographical but ontological — and within that weight, the supernatural becomes, not explicable, but inevitable. You understand that something would grow in this soil. You don’t need to name what.”

I watched Aickman absorb this. His posture did not change, but something behind his eyes rearranged itself, and I thought: he’s conceding something, and it costs him, and he’s doing it so quietly that if you weren’t watching for it, you’d miss it entirely.

“I accept rooting,” he said. “I resist explaining. We are agreed.”

“We are not agreed. We are adjacent.”

He almost smiled. “Adjacent will do.”

I picked up my pen again because I could not help it. “Can I ask about daylight? The brief mentions Midsommar — the idea that the horror happens in full sun. Does this festival happen during the day?”

“The festival happens over several days,” Enriquez said immediately. “And the days are the worst part. The nights you can hide in. The nights have shadows. But the days — the days are when everyone can see you, and you can see everyone, and no one is doing anything wrong. That’s the horror. The visibility. In Midsommar, the flowers are obscene because they’re beautiful. The sun is obscene because it won’t stop. You want darkness because darkness would give you permission to be afraid, and the light refuses that permission.”

“But Jackson’s lottery is also in daylight,” I said. “And it’s a different quality of daylight. Mundane. Ordinary. A nice June morning. The horror isn’t that the sun is relentless — the horror is that the sun doesn’t care. The weather is pleasant. The children are gathering stones. It’s not operatic light. It’s the light you do errands in.”

“Jackson’s light, then,” Aickman said. “Not Aster’s. I prefer Jackson’s light for the same reason I prefer Jackson’s prose — it does not perform. The sentences in ‘The Lottery’ are so plain they are almost bureaucratic, and that plainness is what makes the stoning intolerable. The story reads like minutes from a committee meeting, and the committee has decided to kill someone, and the minutes reflect this decision with the same syntax they would use for any other piece of business.”

“Committee minutes,” Enriquez said, and laughed — a sudden, sharp laugh that startled me. “Yes. I think that’s right. The festival should have a committee. An organizing body. Someone takes notes. Someone is responsible for the food. There are disagreements about the schedule — petty disagreements, the kind you have about who brings the folding chairs. And embedded in these disagreements, entirely unremarked upon, is the other thing. The selection.”

“And the academic sees the committee at work,” I said, “and she recognizes it, because she has sat on committees, and the rhythm is universal — the same tedium, the same passive aggression, the same person who always volunteers and the same person who never does — and this recognition is what undoes her. Because the committee is so normal. The committee is so familiar. And the committee is deciding something that she cannot quite hear.”

“Cannot hear or will not hear?” Aickman asked.

“I don’t know. I honestly don’t know. And I think that ambiguity is the spine of the story. She might be dense. She might be willfully blind. She might be perfectly perceptive but trapped by politeness. I don’t want to choose.”

“Good,” Aickman said. “Don’t.”

Enriquez shook her head. “No. You must choose. Not for the reader — the reader can be left in the ambiguity — but you, the writer, must know. Because the difference between a protagonist who is oblivious and a protagonist who is complicit is the difference between a horror story and a tragedy. And I want the tragedy.”

“You want her to know.”

“I want her to know enough. I want her to have a moment — maybe during the second day, maybe at the long table where they feed her and feed her and feed her until she cannot refuse anything — where she understands, not the specifics, but the grammar. She grasps the syntax of what is happening. Subject, verb, object. And she stays. And the staying is the thing. Not because she is trapped. Because she has been so thoroughly welcomed that leaving would be a kind of violence against the people who welcomed her. The hospitality has become a cage, but the bars are made of genuine kindness, and she cannot break them without breaking something real.”

Aickman was quiet for a long time. The restaurant had gotten louder around us. A couple at the next table was arguing about football — Boca or River, I couldn’t tell which — and their argument had the comfortable intensity of a fight that has been going on for decades and will never be resolved and sustains the relationship rather than threatens it. I thought: that’s what the festival is. A fight that sustains something. A violence that keeps the community intact.

“I will say one thing more,” Aickman said, “and then I should like to sit quietly for a while, because I am tired and the wine is still warm. You mentioned the colleague. The one who invites her. In my experience, the most frightening figure in any story is not the one who means harm but the one who means well and causes harm as a consequence of their good intentions. The colleague should love her. Not romantically — that is a different story and a lesser one — but genuinely, as a friend and fellow scholar. The invitation is an act of love. The colleague believes she is offering a gift. And the gift is, in fact, a gift. That is the terrible part. The festival is genuinely an honour. The selection is genuinely a distinction. And the academic is correct to feel flattered, and she is correct to feel afraid, and both responses are appropriate to the same event, and neither cancels the other.”

He picked up his wine glass and drank from it, finally, wincing slightly at the temperature but swallowing.

“The colleague knows,” Enriquez said. It was not a question.

“The colleague knows, and the colleague loves her, and the colleague invited her because she loves her. These things are all true simultaneously. This is what I mean when I say the story is a condition rather than a puzzle. A puzzle, you can solve. A condition — you were invited to it.”

I wanted to ask about the ending, but something in Aickman’s posture — the way he had settled back into his chair, the wine glass now held against his chest like a document he was considering filing — told me the question would not be welcome. Enriquez seemed to feel it too. She finished her Fernet and signaled for another and said, almost to herself, “The last scene should be a meal. Not the selection. Not whatever happens after. Just the meal. The long table. The dishes she cannot identify. The colleague sitting beside her, pressing food on her, saying eat, eat, you must try this. And the academic eats. And the food is extraordinary. And she cannot stop eating. And the reader understands, before she does, that the eating is part of it, and that her appetite is not her own, and that the meal will not end because she is full.”

“That’s Jackson,” I said. “That’s the lottery. Everyone participates.”

“Everyone participates,” Enriquez agreed. “And participation is irreversible.”

Aickman set his glass on the table with a small, precise click. “You will ruin it if you show what happens after the meal.”

“I know.”

“You will be tempted.”

“I know.”

“The story ends at the table. The food keeps coming. She keeps eating. The colleague smiles. The reader understands enough and not enough, which are the same amount.”

The Boca-River argument at the next table had escalated into laughter, the kind that comes after someone concedes a point they did not expect to concede. Enriquez’s second Fernet arrived. Aickman examined the blue tiles on the wall behind me with the expression of a man cataloguing something he would not mention. I picked up a piece of bread from the basket that had been refilled without anyone asking for it and ate it, and it was very good, and I ate another piece, and the basket was full again, and I did not remember the waiter coming by.

I put the bread down.

Enriquez watched me do this and said nothing, but her eyes — for just a moment — had the quality of someone who has shown you something on purpose and is waiting to see if you’ve understood.

I had not understood. Or I had understood exactly, and understanding made no difference. Which, as Aickman had said, was worse.
