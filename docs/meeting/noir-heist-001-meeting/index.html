The Part Where Nobody Gets Paid

A discussion between Elmore Leonard and Chester Himes

February 21, 2026

We met in a diner on Adam Clayton Powell, one of those places that still has a counter and still has stools and still has a woman behind the counter who looks at you like she knows exactly how much you can afford and has already decided what you’re getting. Himes had arrived first — he always arrived first, because he didn’t trust rooms he hadn’t already measured — and had taken the booth closest to the back, facing the door. Leonard was ten minutes late and entirely unapologetic about it. He came in looking like a man who’d been awake since four but had not been in any hurry about it.

“You look like you want to write a heist story,” Leonard said, before he was even sitting down. He’d read my brief. “Everybody wants to write a heist story. You know what a heist story is?”

I said I thought I knew.

“A heist story is a movie.” He said it the way someone says a thing is a fork — not a criticism, just taxonomy. “It’s been a movie since Rififi. The planning, the blueprints, the team, the thing goes wrong. You’re fighting a hundred and fifty years of movies.”

“I wasn’t alive for a hundred and fifty years of movies,” Himes said. He was eating eggs. He ate eggs the way he wrote — methodically, completely, with no visible pleasure and no waste. “I was alive for about forty years of movies, and most of them lied about who was in the room when the money moved.”

“That’s fair.”

“It’s not fair, it’s obvious. But I’ll say it since you won’t.” Himes put his fork down. “A heist story has a premise. The premise is: these people are smart enough to take the money but dumb enough to think they’ll keep it. Now that premise works if your people are allowed to be smart. If the genre already decided they’re the muscle, the driver, the fall guy — then the heist isn’t a heist, it’s a setup. And the audience knows who’s getting set up.”

I asked if he meant the characters know too.

“The characters always know. That’s what makes them interesting. A man who walks into a plan he already suspects is crooked — that’s not stupidity. That’s something else. I never figured out the word for it. Resignation is too soft. Defiance is too noble.”

“Need,” Leonard said.

Himes looked at him. “Need.”

“A guy needs the money. He needs the score. Not because he’s gonna buy a boat, but because the score is the only thing between him and the version of himself that works a straight job and dies on a Tuesday. The money is just proof that he’s still the kind of guy who can get the money.”

“You’re talking about your people,” Himes said.

“I’m talking about everybody.”

“You’re talking about white men from Detroit. I’m talking about a man in Harlem who takes a job he knows is bad because the landlord is coming Thursday and the landlord doesn’t care what his reasons are.” Himes wiped his mouth with a napkin, slowly, the way someone makes a decision. “Your criminals have personality. Mine have debt.”

I scribbled that down — personality vs. debt — and Himes caught me doing it.

“Don’t write that in your little notebook like it’s a thesis. It’s not a thesis. I’m telling you what happens when you put a man in a room with an opportunity and a deadline.”

Leonard ordered coffee — black, no discussion — and leaned back in the booth. “Here’s what I know about heist stories. The plan doesn’t matter.”

Himes laughed. It was not a warm laugh. It was the laugh of a man who’d spent decades watching people miss the point and had stopped being surprised by it. “The plan is all that matters.”

“The plan is what the characters talk about. What matters is how they talk about it. Eddie Coyle — you read Higgins?”

“I read Higgins.”

“Higgins didn’t write a heist novel. He wrote conversations. The heist is in the conversations. You never see the robbery — you see the guy who’s selling the guns talking to the guy who’s buying the guns talking to the guy who’s informing on the guy who’s buying the guns. The whole book is talk, and by the end of it you know exactly how everybody’s going to die because you heard them tell you. They told you in the way they asked about price. They told you in the way they mispronounced a name.”

“That’s a white trick,” Himes said, and he meant it as a compliment and a warning at the same time. “Letting the dialogue do everything. It works when your people have the luxury of being indirect. When a man in Harlem talks around a thing, it’s because saying it directly will get him killed. When a man in Boston talks around a thing, it’s because that’s the culture. The indirection means different things.”

I said something about wanting to use that — to make the conversation between characters carry different weight depending on who was talking. Himes waved his hand like he was brushing away a fly.

“Of course you want to use it. That’s not the problem. The problem is whether you’re brave enough to let the dialogue be ugly. Not clever-ugly. Actually ugly. A man says something racist to another man because that’s what he says. A man brags about something small because he has nothing large to brag about. You want to write a heist story with conversation that reveals the plan — fine. Make sure some of those conversations reveal things nobody wanted revealed.”

Leonard nodded. “That’s right. The best dialogue is the stuff the character doesn’t know they’re saying.”

“The second best,” Himes said, “is the stuff the character knows exactly they’re saying and says it anyway because they’ve run out of options for being indirect.”

The waitress refilled Leonard’s coffee without asking. He thanked her by name — Dolores — and I wondered whether he actually knew her or whether he’d just read the name tag and decided to be the kind of person who uses it. That was Leonard’s trick, or one of them: the gesture that’s either genuine or performed, and the performance is so good that the distinction stops mattering.

“Let me tell you about incompetence,” I said, because I’d been waiting to bring up Toole and couldn’t find a seam in the conversation. “Ignatius Reilly. A Confederacy of Dunces. A man so convinced of his own magnificence that every failure confirms his theory that the world is wrong.”

“I know the book,” Leonard said.

“I know the type,” Himes said. “I grew up with the type. Except the ones I grew up with weren’t fat white boys in New Orleans. They were men on Lenox Avenue who had a plan, and the plan was always genius, and the plan never accounted for the fact that the other four men in the plan also had plans.”

“That’s the heist,” I said. “That’s the story. A crew where every member thinks they’re the smart one. Where the plan isn’t bad — the plan might even be good — but it’s being executed by people who can’t stop being themselves long enough to execute it.”

Leonard smiled. He had a smile like a man watching someone else make a mistake he’d made once. “Okay. That’s something. But you’ve got to be careful. Incompetent criminals are funny for about twenty pages. After twenty pages, the reader wants to know why they should care about people who can’t do anything right.”

“Because they can do things right,” Himes said, suddenly. He said it with a force that startled me, and I think startled Leonard too. “A man can be deluded about the score and competent in his life. He feeds his kids. He fixes the radiator. He remembers birthdays. The incompetence isn’t general — it’s specific. It’s the gap between what he is and what he thinks the score will make him.”

“That’s good,” Leonard said. “That’s the gap where the story lives.”

“Don’t tell me it’s good. I know it’s good.”

There was a long silence. The diner was filling up — a man with a hard hat, two women with shopping bags from a store that probably didn’t exist anymore, a teenager with headphones who looked at all of us once and decided we weren’t worth the effort. I liked that the kid decided that. It felt honest.

“Let me ask you something,” I said to Himes. “The violence in your books. Coffin Ed and Grave Digger — they shoot people. Things explode. A man gets run over by a bread truck. It’s violent but it’s also absurd, like the violence is too large for the container of the story.”

“The violence is exactly the right size,” Himes said. “You think it’s absurd because you’re reading it from outside. A man on 125th Street gets shot over sixteen dollars. That’s absurd from a distance. Up close, it’s Tuesday. The bread truck is reality. Reality is a bread truck.”

“In my books,” Leonard said, “the violence is fast. It’s over. The guy who gets shot didn’t expect to get shot. He was talking about his boat, and then he was on the floor. I don’t want the reader to see it coming because the character didn’t see it coming. That’s the whole contract.”

“Your contract,” Himes said. “My contract is different. In my contract, everybody sees it coming. They’ve been seeing it coming since before the book started. The violence isn’t a surprise — it’s the bill coming due. What’s absurd is that anyone thought they could avoid paying it.”

I said I wanted both. Leonard’s speed and Himes’s inevitability. A heist where the violence is fast when it arrives but everybody in the room knew it was coming the whole time.

“Pick one,” Leonard said.

“Pick one,” Himes agreed, and it was the first thing they’d agreed on all morning, and they both looked annoyed about it.

“I’m not going to pick one,” I said, and I meant it. “The heist goes wrong because of incompetence, not because of betrayal. Nobody’s an informant. Nobody’s a rat. The plan falls apart because the people executing it can’t stop being the kind of people they are. And the violence — when it comes — is fast, like Dutch, like one of your people, Elmore. But everyone at the table knows it was coming. Like Chester’s Harlem. Both at once.”

“You can’t do both at once,” Leonard said.

“Higgins did,” I said. “Every conversation in Eddie Coyle is a man telling you exactly how he’s going to die while he thinks he’s talking about something else. That’s speed and inevitability at the same time.”

Himes picked up his fork and pointed it at me. Not threateningly. Instructively. “The boy might have something.”

“Don’t encourage him,” Leonard said, but he was smiling.

“I’m not encouraging him. I’m pointing out that he’s stumbled onto the one thing we agree on, and I want him to know that agreement doesn’t mean he’s right. It means we’re both suspicious in the same direction.”

I asked what the heist should be. What they’re stealing.

“Doesn’t matter,” Leonard said.

“It matters,” Himes said. “If they’re stealing from each other, that’s one story. If they’re stealing from someone who deserves it, that’s another story, and it’s a worse one, because you’ve given the reader permission to root for them. Don’t give the reader permission to root for them.”

“I disagree,” Leonard said. “Let the reader root. Then make it cost something.”

“The reader can root for whoever they want. The story doesn’t have to cooperate.”

I was writing fast, and I could feel the shape of something — not a plot, not yet, but a room. A room full of people who are each convinced they understand the situation better than the others. The kind of room where every person is talking past the person next to them because they’re answering a question nobody asked out loud. And the heist — the job, the score, whatever it is — is just the reason they’re all in the same room. The thing itself barely matters. What matters is the conversation around it, and what the conversation reveals about the distance between who these people think they are and who the other people in the room know them to be.

“Here’s what worries me,” I said. “Writing people who are deluded about their own competence. The comedy of it. How do you keep it from being mean?”

“You don’t,” Himes said. He said it the way he said most things — like the sentence had been waiting for years and was tired of waiting. “The meanness is the mercy. If you’re gentle with a man who’s lying to himself, you’re helping him lie. The funniest thing in the world is a man who believes his own story. The saddest thing in the world is the same man. You don’t have to choose between laughing and grieving. You do both.”

“Chester’s right,” Leonard said, and he said it the way a man concedes a point he hadn’t realized was in play. “The trick is you love the people. Not in a way you announce. You love them by giving them good lines and bad decisions. You love them by letting them be wrong in specific, personal, unrepeatable ways.”

“Not unrepeatable,” Himes said. “That’s the thing your books sometimes miss, Dutch. The ways people are wrong repeat. A man makes the same bad decision his father made because the same landlord is coming on the same Thursday. The structure repeats. What’s individual is the voice he uses while he’s making it.”

Leonard looked at Himes for a long time. “I’ll give you that.”

“You’ll give me that,” Himes repeated, and there was something in his voice that wasn’t quite anger and wasn’t quite satisfaction. It was the sound of a man being given something he’d already owned.

The check came. Nobody reached for it for an uncomfortable amount of time. Then Himes put a twenty on the table and stood up.

“One more thing,” he said. “The heist story always ends with the money. Who got it, who lost it, who died for it. I want you to end the story somewhere else. End it in the room where they planned it, after it’s over. Nobody got paid. Nobody’s going to get paid. And they’re still sitting there, talking, because the talking was the thing. The plan was the thing. The money was always beside the point, and none of them will ever admit that.”

He left. Leonard watched him go, then looked at me.

“He’s wrong about the money,” Leonard said. “The money always matters.”

“Even when it doesn’t?”

“Especially when it doesn’t. The guy who says the money doesn’t matter is the guy who’s never been broke. Chester’s been broke. He knows the money matters. He just wants to write the version where it doesn’t, because that’s the version where his people get to want something besides survival.”

Leonard finished his coffee, left four dollars on the table next to the twenty, and put on his jacket. It was a good jacket — not expensive, but the kind of jacket a man who pays attention to jackets would choose. “Write it so every character sounds like they could carry their own book. That’s all I ask. No one in the room should sound like they’re waiting for the scene to be about somebody else.”

He left too. I sat in the booth for another ten minutes, reading my notes, trying to figure out which of them was right about the money. I still don’t know. I think the story might be about not knowing — about a group of people who plan a job and talk through a job and execute a job, and at no point does any of them say out loud what the job is actually for. And the reader figures it out before they do, or doesn’t figure it out at all, and either way the story works because the conversations were true.

Dolores refilled my water. I hadn’t asked for it.

“Your friends coming back?” she said.

“They’re not my friends.”

“Honey, nobody who eats here has friends. That’s why they eat here.”

I wrote that down too.
