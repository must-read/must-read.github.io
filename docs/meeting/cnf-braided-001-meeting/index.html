Creative Nonfiction / Braided Hermit Crab Essay

Boring Into the Stratigraphy of the Personal

A discussion between John McPhee and Roxane Gay

February 21, 2026

McPhee wanted to meet outdoors. He’d suggested a bench near the canal towpath in Princeton, which would have been fine in September but was punishing in February, the wind cutting across the frozen water and into my coat seams. Gay had flown in that morning and was still in her airport layers — puffy jacket, scarf wound three times, boots that looked warm but weren’t. She sat at the far end of the bench and McPhee sat at the near end and I stood because there wasn’t room, which set the dynamic for the next two hours: the two of them grounded, me hovering.

“Hermit crab essay,” McPhee said, not as a greeting but as if resuming a conversation that had been paused. “You borrow a form. A field guide, a recipe, an index. The content lives inside someone else’s structure.”

“I know what a hermit crab essay is, John.”

“I’m saying it aloud because the phrase annoys me and I want to get past the annoyance before we start. The crab doesn’t choose the shell for aesthetic reasons. It chooses it because the old one is too small. There’s biological urgency. Most hermit crab essays I read are clever. Cleverness is not urgency.”

Gay pulled her scarf higher. “What’s the form we’re borrowing?”

I’d been thinking about this. I said I’d been looking at geological survey reports — the language of core samples, stratigraphic columns, the systematic description of what lies beneath a surface. Layers named and dated. Intrusions noted. Unconformities — gaps in the record where time is missing and no one can say why.

McPhee’s eyes did something I can only describe as brightening without moving. He has this quality where interest registers as stillness rather than animation. “An unconformity is one of the great words in science. It describes an absence that is also evidence. You see the layers above and the layers below and between them is a span of time — sometimes millions of years — that left no trace. The rock was deposited, eroded, and whatever happened during the erosion is gone.”

“So the gaps are the subject,” Gay said.

“The gaps are always the subject,” McPhee said, and I could tell he meant it as a statement about all nonfiction, not just geology.

Gay shifted on the bench. “Here’s where I get nervous. Because geological patience — and I mean the McPhee brand of geological patience, where you spend four hundred pages on a road cut in New Jersey and it’s magnificent — that patience can become a way of not saying the painful thing. You can hide in deep time. You can make human suffering into one thin layer in a column of rock and call it perspective.”

“That’s not what I do.”

“I didn’t say it was what you do. I said it’s what the form permits. And if we’re braiding geological time with personal time, with the body — my body, a body — I need to know that the personal strands aren’t going to get compressed into metaphor. A bruise is not a sedimentary deposit.”

The wind picked up. I shoved my hands deeper into my pockets and said something about Claudia Rankine’s Citizen — how it moves between registers without announcing the transitions. A lyric passage, then a prose block, then second person that implicates the reader, then an image. The seams between modes aren’t smoothed. You feel each shift as a small disruption, and the accumulation of disruptions becomes the argument.

McPhee nodded slowly. “Rankine trusts the fragment. I trust the long line. These are not the same instinct.”

“No,” I said. “But the piece we’re writing might need both. Long geological passages — patient, precise, descriptive — interrupted by fragments of personal experience. Not alternating in some tidy pattern. More like the geology itself: long periods of slow deposition, then a sudden intrusion. Something forcing its way up through the layers.”

“Intrusion is a good word for it,” Gay said. “The personal forcing its way into the impersonal. Because that’s what happens. You’re reading about limestone formations and then suddenly you’re reading about someone’s body on an examination table and the shift should be violent. Not graceful. I don’t want graceful transitions.”

McPhee crossed his arms. This was, I would learn, his way of disagreeing before he had his counterargument fully formed. “Graceful is the wrong word to object to. What you want to avoid is decorative. A transition can be graceful and still be honest. In fact, the most honest structural moves in nonfiction are often the most elegant — because the writer has found the real connection, not manufactured one.”

“We’re going to disagree about this,” Gay said.

“We are.”

I let that sit. A jogger went past on the towpath, earbuds in, oblivious. The canal was a sheet of gray ice with a dark crack running down its center like a vein.

I asked about Macfarlane — specifically Underland, the way it uses underground spaces as a lens for everything humans bury, hide, store, lose, or refuse to look at. The catacombs and the nuclear waste repositories and the fungal networks and the caves where Paleolithic artists worked by firelight. What’s beneath.

“I’ve read Macfarlane,” Gay said. “He’s good at surfaces of depth. At making the reader feel they’re descending. But he keeps himself at a certain remove. The awe is real but the vulnerability is managed.”

McPhee smiled — actually smiled, a rare event. “You just described me.”

“I know I did.”

“And you think the piece needs more than managed vulnerability.”

“I think a braided essay about what lies beneath has to actually go beneath. Not beneath the earth — beneath the performance of the writer. Macfarlane descends into caves. Fine. But what are the caves you don’t descend into? What’s the layer you skip? That’s the unconformity in the personal — the thing you eroded away, the time you lost and left no trace of.”

McPhee was quiet for a moment, which from him was a form of assent. Then he said: “In Basin and Range I spent a long time on the idea of deep time — the vertigo of it, how human history is the thinnest veneer on a geological column. I thought I was writing about rocks. My editor told me I was writing about mortality. I told him he was wrong. Twenty years later I think he might have been right, but not in the way he meant. It wasn’t that the geology was a metaphor for mortality. It was that spending enough time with billion-year-old formations changes your sense of what matters. Your urgencies rearrange.”

“That rearrangement is a luxury,” Gay said. It came out flat, not accusatory, but with the weight of something she’d thought about before this conversation. “The ability to let deep time rearrange your urgencies. That requires a certain security. If your body is a site of immediate political contest — if people are legislating what you can do with it, where you can take it, what medical care it deserves — you don’t have the luxury of geological patience. Your urgency is not a veneer. It’s bedrock.”

I felt the temperature of the conversation change. Not hostile, but real. I said I thought that tension — between geological patience and personal urgency — might be the central braid. Not a resolution of the two, but the friction between them. The essay holding both timescales simultaneously and letting the reader feel the dissonance.

“Dissonance is good,” McPhee said. “But dissonance needs a tonal center or it’s just noise. In music, in writing. You need at least one strand that the reader can orient by.”

“The geological strand,” I said. “Because it’s the most stable. The formations don’t change. The dates don’t shift. The Devonian is always the Devonian. It becomes the spine, and the personal material can be as unstable as it needs to be.”

Gay tilted her head. “Or the geological strand is the one that seems stable and turns out not to be. Isn’t that what plate tectonics proved? That the ground moves. That continents drift. That what looks like bedrock is just another surface, slower than the others.”

McPhee said nothing to that, but he uncrossed his arms and put his palms flat on his knees, which I took as the physical equivalent of conceding a point.

I asked Gay about the braiding — not the metaphor of it, but the mechanics. In Citizen, Rankine doesn’t braid in any predictable pattern. You can’t map the structure onto a diagram. The shifts between modes feel governed by emotional logic rather than architectural logic. Was that what she wanted here?

“I want the braiding to have a logic the reader can feel but not predict,” she said. “If you can predict when the personal strand is coming, you brace for it. And if you brace, it doesn’t land. The geological strand should lull you — all that beautiful precise language about formations and epochs — and then the personal should rupture it. Not every time. Sometimes the geological strand should just continue, should go on longer than you expect, and the absence of the personal becomes its own kind of statement.”

“Absence as a structural tool,” McPhee said. “That I understand. In The Pine Barrens I let certain sections run long without a human voice because the landscape needed to be itself before it could hold people. The emptiness was compositional.”

“Except your emptiness was chosen,” Gay said. “Aesthetic emptiness. I’m talking about the emptiness that isn’t chosen. The section of the essay where the personal strand should appear — where the reader expects it, where the structure demands it — and it doesn’t. Because the narrator can’t go there yet. Or ever. That’s an unconformity in the text itself.”

That image — an unconformity in the text, a gap the reader can feel but the writer refuses to fill — stayed with me. I scribbled it in my notebook and underlined it twice.

I said I’d been thinking about the structure as a literal stratigraphic column. The essay borrows the form of a geological survey report — headed sections describing each layer, its composition, its age, its relationship to the layers above and below. But as the column descends, as we drill deeper, the language shifts. The upper layers are precise, scientific, McPhee-patient. The middle layers start to buckle. Personal material intrudes — not as metaphor but as actual intrusion, the way a dike of igneous rock forces through sedimentary layers. And the deepest layers are fragment and lyric, Rankine-mode, because at a certain depth language itself compresses.

McPhee uncrossed his arms. “That’s an architecture I can see. But I want to push back on the assumption that precision belongs only to the upper layers. Precision is not a surface quality. The most precise writing I’ve done has been about things I care about most deeply. You don’t abandon the careful sentence when you get to the painful material. You hold it tighter.”

“That’s your instinct,” Gay said. “And it’s a legitimate one. But it’s not the only one. Sometimes the painful material shatters the careful sentence. Sometimes you reach for precision and your hand shakes and what comes out is a fragment, and the fragment is truer than the complete thought would have been.”

“A fragment can be precise.”

“A fragment can be precise. But it can also just be broken, and the brokenness is the point. I don’t want to clean up every broken sentence into something shapely.”

We were quiet for a while. I was thinking about the Rankine — how Citizen uses the second person as a weapon, a mirror, an embrace. You are walking down the street. You are in the waiting room. It collapses the distance between writer and reader, makes the reader inhabit the experience. I asked whether the braided essay should use second person in any of its strands.

McPhee said, “I’ve never used second person and I don’t intend to start.”

Gay laughed. “John. We’re not asking you to write it.”

“I’m aware. But you’re asking me to think about it, and when I think about second person in nonfiction I think about coercion. You’re telling the reader what they experienced. It’s presumptuous.”

“It’s only presumptuous if it’s wrong,” Gay said. “When Rankine writes ‘you are in the car,’ she’s not guessing. She’s describing something that happens to certain bodies in certain spaces. The presumption is the point — it forces recognition.”

“Or resistance.”

“Or resistance. Both are useful.”

I wrote that down — “both are useful” — because it felt like the kind of thing I’d need later. I said I was thinking about the essay’s subject matter, not just its form. If we’re using the geological survey as our borrowed shell, and we’re braiding deep time with personal time, what’s the actual terrain? Where is this column being drilled?

McPhee said, “Under a specific place. Not a metaphorical place. Geology is always local before it’s universal. You drill into particular rock, in a particular county, and what you find there connects to the Appalachian orogeny and the breakup of Pangaea, but it starts with this hillside, this outcrop, this road cut.”

Gay said, “And under a specific body. In a specific examination room, on a specific afternoon when the fluorescent light was buzzing and the paper on the table crinkled every time you shifted your weight.”

“Both,” I said.

“Obviously both. The question is how they braid.”

I tried something. “What if the column is literal — a real place, a real borehole, real geological data — and the personal material surfaces at unconformities? At the gaps in the geological record, the narrator’s personal gaps appear. Where the earth is missing time, the narrator is missing time too. Not as analogy but as structural echo.”

McPhee leaned forward. “That’s close. But the geological data has to be real. Not evocative science. Not poetic geology. Actual formations, actual ages, actual mineral compositions. I’ve spent my career arguing that the real thing is more interesting than the metaphorical version. Dolomite is a more interesting word than ‘ancient stone.’ The Marcellus Shale is a more interesting subject than ‘the deep past.’”

“Roxane, does that work for you? The precision of the geological strands?”

“It works if the personal strands refuse to match that precision. If the geology is exact — Ordovician, 485 million years ago, gray limestone with fossil brachiopods — then the personal should be inexact. Should resist dating. Should refuse to name its minerals. The contrast is the engine.”

McPhee shook his head. Not in disagreement, exactly. More like a dog shaking off water. “I don’t think the personal should be deliberately inexact. I think it should strive for precision and sometimes fail. That’s different. The failure of precision is more interesting than the performance of vagueness.”

I wanted to ask about the body — Gay’s persistent subject, the body as site of meaning, as political text, as landscape. I said: “In Underland, Macfarlane keeps descending. Each chapter goes deeper. The surface world recedes. What if our essay does the same thing, but the deeper we go into the earth, the deeper we go into the body? Skin as topsoil. Muscle as sediment. Bone as bedrock. The interior of the body mapped as geology.”

Gay’s expression shifted — not to agreement, but to a wariness I’d learn to respect. “That metaphor is available. It’s been available for a long time. And it’s dangerous. Because when you turn the body into landscape, you can aestheticize it. You can make it beautiful. And some bodies — fat bodies, Black bodies, bodies in pain — have been aestheticized enough. I’m not interested in making my body into a terrain for someone else to explore.”

“What if it’s your terrain to explore?”

“Then the exploration has to cost something. It can’t be a nature walk. It has to be an excavation — and excavations damage the site.”

McPhee said quietly, “All geology is destructive. You split the rock to see the crystal inside. You drill the borehole that lets you read the column. The act of knowing the earth is an act of opening it.”

Nobody said anything for a moment. The crack in the canal ice had widened, or I imagined it had.

I asked about the essay’s ending — not to plan a plot, but to understand the instinct. When you drill deep enough, what do you hit?

McPhee said, “Basement rock. The oldest thing. In the mid-Atlantic states, it’s Precambrian gneiss — metamorphic, billion years old, no fossils because there was nothing alive yet to fossilize. You hit a silence.”

Gay said, “You hit the thing you weren’t looking for. The lump the doctor finds when she’s checking for something else. The phone call about a person you’d stopped thinking about. It’s not silence. It’s noise you weren’t prepared to interpret.”

“Those are two different endings,” I said.

“Yes,” Gay said.

“One geological, one personal.”

“Yes.”

“And we’re not going to resolve which one the essay uses.”

McPhee stood up from the bench, slowly, the way someone stands who has been sitting in February cold for too long. He put his hands in his coat pockets and looked at the canal. “The essay will figure it out. That’s what the writing is for. If we knew the ending, there would be no reason to write it.”

Gay stayed seated. “I disagree. I usually know my endings. I write toward them. The question is whether the essay lets me arrive there or whether the geological material redirects me.”

“You’re describing two different relationships to form,” I said.

“I’m describing two different relationships to control.”

McPhee was already walking toward the parking lot. He turned back and said, not loudly, “Write it in layers. Don’t decide the order yet. Write each strand separately — the geological column, the personal material, the Rankine-mode fragments — and then braid them. The braiding will reveal the structure. It always does.”

Gay said to me, not to him, because he was too far away now: “He’s right about that last part. But he’s wrong that the braiding reveals the structure. The braiding is the structure. There’s nothing underneath it.”

I stood in the cold, between a bench and a canal, and wrote that down too.
