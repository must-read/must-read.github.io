The Bird Boys

Combining Edward P. Jones + Denis Johnson | The Known World + Jesus' Son

March 1, 2026

The field was sixty hectares of wheat that year, laid out in plots the size of bedsheets, and our job was to keep the birds from eating it.

There were four of us. Tomás Robles, who was seventeen and had a shoulder that dislocated when he threw rocks too hard, which he always did. Alejandro Fuentes, who was sixteen and who everyone called Gordo though he wasn’t fat — he’d been fat at nine and the name had outlasted the body. Beto Sifuentes, who was fifteen like me, who carried a slingshot made from a guava branch and a strip of inner tube, and who could hit a grackle at forty meters, though grackles were not the problem. The problem was when they came in clouds of four hundred, the house finches that arrived at dawn before we did, and the white-winged doves that drifted in from the thorn forest along the river and sat in the wheat as if they had been invited.

We were hired by the American. We did not know his name for the first two weeks. He spoke Spanish the way a man speaks to a dog — short commands, many gestures, his face doing most of the work. He had a bald head that the sun had ruined and hands that were always moving — even when he stood still those hands were going, tapping his thigh, adjusting the brim of a hat he wasn’t wearing — and he paid us four pesos a day each, which was more than the ejido was paying anyone for anything that season.

The field was on the experiment station south of Ciudad Obregon, on flat land between the road and an irrigation canal that the government had finished the year before and that still smelled of new concrete. The station had a building with a corrugated roof where the American kept his notebooks and a radio that received static and baseball scores from Arizona, sometimes both at once. Around the building were the plots — hundreds of them, each marked with a wooden stake and a number, each containing a different line of wheat that the American was trying to grow in soil that wheat had never wanted.

What we understood, at first, was this: we were to chase the birds. We did this from dawn until about ten, when the heat made the birds disappear into whatever shade they could find, and then again from three until dark. During the midday hours we sat under a mesquite tree near the canal and ate tortillas and talked about women we had not touched and a future we could not have described if pressed. Gordo wanted to be a bus driver. Tomás wanted to be a mechanic. Beto wanted to go to California, where he had heard the streets were made of something smooth and grey that never turned to mud. I did not say what I wanted because I did not want anything — not yet — and because I had learned that the boy without an ambition was the boy the others talked in front of, as if he weren’t fully there.

The American sat on an overturned bucket in the field with a pair of tweezers and did something to the wheat that we watched from the shade the way you watch a man talking to himself on a bus. He wore the same clothes every day — khaki trousers, a shirt the color of dust, boots that had been expensive once and were now held together by the memory of stitching. He ate lunch out of a paper bag: a tortilla folded around beans, sometimes a boiled egg, always an orange that he peeled in one continuous spiral, and Beto once said, with genuine admiration, “That man could peel a snake.”

Harold had a notebook in which he wrote things we could not read, numbers and letters in combinations that meant nothing to us but clearly meant something tremendous to him. He would crouch over a wheat plot with his tweezers and his paper bags, and then he would write in the notebook, and then he would move to the next plot and do it again. The patience of it was not the patience of a man waiting. It was the patience of a man who had decided that this was the only work worth doing and had stopped noticing the hours it took. My grandmother had the same thing when she rolled tortillas — the same motion a thousand times, each one slightly different in a way only she could feel.

“He’s marrying them,” Tomás said one afternoon. He’d been watching longer than the rest of us.

“Marrying what.”

“The wheat. One kind to another. Like you cross a good bull with a cow to get a better calf. He’s crossing the wheat.”

We went over and stood behind the American and watched. He had a wheat head clamped between his thumb and finger, and with the tweezers he was removing something from inside it — tiny pale parts that he dropped into his palm. Then he took a paper bag from a stake next to a different plot, shook something out of it onto the wheat head he’d just stripped, and covered the head with a fresh bag. The whole operation took about ninety seconds, and his hands shook slightly the entire time, and he did not look up.

“What’s in the bag?” Gordo asked.

“Pollen,” Tomás said, though none of us knew the word in Spanish. What he actually said was “the dust from the other one,” and we understood.

The American’s name was Harold, and after three weeks he decided to teach us the crosses. I think this was because there were four thousand wheat heads to cross that season and two hands were not enough. He lined us up at the edge of a plot and showed us what he’d been doing: removing the stamens — the male parts — from one head, then dusting it with pollen from a different variety. He called it emasculation, a word none of us knew. Tomás translated it as “taking the man out of the flower,” and Gordo laughed until Harold looked at him, and then Gordo stopped laughing because Harold’s face had a quality that discouraged amusement. Not anger. Something more focused than anger.

He gave us each a pair of tweezers and a row. The first day I ruined forty wheat heads. The stamens were the size of rice grains, pale yellow, and they hid between the glumes — the little shells that made up each spikelet — like children behind a door. My fingers were too large, too used to slingshots and fence wire and the coarse work of a boy whose hands had never been asked to be careful. I tore glumes off entirely. I crushed the pistils I was supposed to leave intact. I cross-pollinated a head that had already been crossed, which Harold discovered two days later and which produced, I suspect, a plant that confused even the wheat.

But something happened during that first week. It happened in my hands before it happened in my mind. My fingers learned the space between the glumes. They learned the angle at which a stamen releases cleanly, without tearing the tissue beneath it. They learned the resistance of a ripe anther versus one that was a day from opening — a pressure, a temperature, a readiness that my fingertips could read though I had no words for what they were reading.

By the second week, Harold stood behind me and watched me work for twenty minutes without saying anything. I did eleven heads in that time. He’d been averaging eight.

He said something in English to himself. Then he went to watch Tomás, who was working steadily and well, at about Harold’s own pace. Gordo was slower but careful. Beto was erratic — brilliant one hour, sloppy the next, his hands tuned to a frequency that drifted.

After a month, Harold stopped doing crosses himself. He walked the rows and checked our work and wrote numbers in his notebooks. When he found a head I’d done, he would hold it up to the light and look at it from several angles, then move on without comment. Once, near the end of that first season, I saw him hold up a head I’d crossed and show it to a man who had come from Mexico City in a government car — a man in a suit who stood at the edge of the field as if the dirt might damage him. Harold pointed at the head and then at me, and the man from Mexico City looked at me the way you look at a tool that is performing better than expected. I went back to my row.

There was a hierarchy among us that nobody discussed. Tomás was the oldest and therefore the one Harold addressed when he wanted something done. Gordo was the one who could fix things — the broken wheelbarrow, the jammed padlock on the supply shed, the station manager’s radio when it stopped receiving even the baseball scores. Beto was the one who made us laugh, who could imitate Harold’s walk so perfectly that once Harold himself saw it and stood very still before turning away with something on his face that might have been amusement, or might have been the look of a man recognizing himself from the outside. And I was the one whose hands did the thing that nobody talked about.

The mornings were the good hours. We arrived before dawn, when the Yaqui Valley was still cool enough that your breath made a small ghost in front of your face. The wheat was wet with dew that caught the first light and the plots looked wrong somehow, too bright for what they were — a government experiment station, numbered stakes, dirt. The light didn’t know that. The light treated each wheat head like it mattered independently. The birds arrived with the light. We chased them. Then we worked the crosses until the heat drove us to the mesquite tree, and then we went back to the crosses, and then the sun went down and we walked back to the road where the bus sometimes came and sometimes didn’t.

In November of that first year the wheat was harvested and the field was empty and we had nothing to do. Harold went to Mexico City and then to the United States and was gone for three months. He left us a message through the station manager that we should come back in February when the new planting started, and he left us each an envelope with twenty pesos in it, which was more money than I had ever held at one time.

Tomás used his to buy a pair of boots. Gordo gave his to his mother. Beto spent his in two days on things I don’t remember and probably neither does he. I kept mine in a coffee can under my bed. During those three months I worked odd jobs — moving irrigation pipe for a farmer near Cocorít, stacking crates at a warehouse in Obregon — and my hands felt wrong. The pipe work was heavy and simple, and my fingers had nothing delicate to do. I would catch myself rubbing my thumb against my forefinger, testing the air for a resistance that wasn’t there.

My mother noticed something. She didn’t say what she noticed. She put food in front of me and watched me eat and sometimes asked if I was going back to the American’s field. I was going back. The question was how I would fill the months between.

In February we came back and the field had been planted again, new varieties with new stakes and new numbers, and Harold was there on his overturned bucket as if he had never left. He’d gotten thinner. The skin on his head had peeled and re-formed into a permanent redness. He handed us our tweezers. The birds were already in the field.

That second season is when it happened — the thing with the scissors. The description will be wrong because the language for it doesn’t exist.

I was working a row of a line Harold called Kenya Red, which had large heads with tough glumes. The tweezers kept slipping, and the work was slow, and I was losing heads to torn tissue. My mind was somewhere else — the sound of doves in the thorn forest, a girl named Esperanza Villalobos who worked at the panadería on Calle Sufragio and who had smiled at me once in a way that could have meant anything — but my hands were angry. They wanted the glumes open and they could not get them open. At some point I reached into the pocket of my trousers and found the small scissors my mother used for thread, which I had borrowed six months earlier and forgotten to return.

I tried cutting the tip of a glume. Just the top third. And the glume opened like a door.

The stamen was right there. I removed it with the tweezers in one motion, clean, no tearing. The whole operation took maybe four seconds. With the tweezers alone, it took fifteen.

I did the next head the same way. Then the next. By the end of that row I had done thirty-seven heads in the time it normally took me to do twelve. Harold was in the next field checking something and didn’t see me. I didn’t tell him. I’m not sure why.

I used the scissors for three days before Harold noticed. During those three days I crossed more heads than I had in any comparable period, and the quality was better — cleaner cuts, less tissue damage, fewer lost pistils. The numbers would show it later in Harold’s notebooks, though I didn’t know about the numbers then. What I knew was the feeling: a rightness in the motion, a fit between tool and task and hand that I had never experienced with anything else.

On the third day Harold came up behind me. I heard his boots in the dirt, the shuffle he had because of a bad knee, and then the shuffle stopped. I didn’t turn around. I finished the head I was on, and the next one, and the next, working at the pace my hands had settled into — cut, open, extract, bag. When I finally turned around, his face had an expression I hadn’t seen before. Something between recognition and loss.

He held his hand out. I gave him the scissors. He turned them over, opened and closed them once, looked at the wheat head I’d just done, and then he took the scissors to the end of the row and tried the technique himself.

His hands were too big. The fingers that had been adequate for tweezers were wrong for the scissors in a way that had nothing to do with skill and everything to do with proportion. He tore two heads before he got one right, and when he got one right it took him twice as long as it took me. He stood there holding my mother’s scissors in the afternoon sun with an expression on his face that I would see again, years later, on the faces of other educated men confronted with something they could not do.

He gave the scissors back to me and said, “Enséñales.” Teach them.

I taught Tomás that afternoon. He picked it up in two hours, though his dislocating shoulder made the work hurt in a way mine didn’t. I taught Gordo the next day, and he was adequate but would never be fast. Beto took to it like a reflex — within a week his hands were nearly as fast as mine, though he had days where he couldn’t find the rhythm and would revert to the tweezers without explanation.

Harold ordered twelve pairs of scissors from a surgical supply company in Tucson. When they arrived six weeks later, they were better than my mother’s thread scissors — sharper, more precise, with a spring in the hinge that returned them to the open position. He gave me the first pair. I still have them somewhere.

Gordo left at the end of the second season. His brother had gone to Tijuana to work in a factory that made radio cabinets, and there was a second position available, and their mother needed the money more than twenty pesos every three months. These were facts that admitted no argument. A factory job in Tijuana paid eight times what Harold paid, and Gordo’s mother had four other children and a house with a roof that leaked in three places that Gordo had patched with flattened tin cans, and the patches were failing.

He shook our hands on the last day of harvest. He held mine a little longer, or maybe that’s something I’ve added. He said, “Don’t let the birds eat it all,” and then he walked up the road toward the bus stop with a bag over his shoulder that contained everything he considered worth carrying, which was not much. I watched him until the road bent and the mesquite hid him.

I didn’t see him for fourteen years, until we met by accident at a bus station in Hermosillo and he didn’t recognize me and I had to explain who I was. He was heavier again — the name had caught up to the body — and he worked in a tile factory now, not the radio cabinet place, and he had five children and a wife I’d never met. When I told him I was still at the station, still in the wheat, he looked at me and I could see him trying to decide what my life meant, and failing, and covering the failure with warmth.

Tomás left the following spring. Esperanza Villalobos, the girl from the panadería, was pregnant, and the baby would have Tomás’s last name, not mine. He married her in April and took a job driving a delivery truck for a bottling company in Los Mochis. At the wedding he was happy, genuinely and completely happy, and I danced badly and drank too much mezcal and woke up under a table with the scissors in my pocket and a headache that lasted two days, and the next Monday I was back in the field and there were three hundred heads to cross and only two of us.

Beto lasted until October. He never said he was leaving. He just stopped coming. Three mornings in a row his spot in the row was empty, and on the first morning I thought he was sick, and on the second I thought he was in trouble, and on the third I went to his house and his mother told me he’d taken a bus north with a cousin. She said it the way you say the weather has changed — without drama, as something that was going to happen and now had happened. She offered me coffee. I sat at her table and drank it, and she told me Beto had been talking about going north for a year, that the cousin had a connection to a ranch in Arizona that needed hands for the cotton, and that Beto had left his slingshot — the one made from guava wood and inner tube — on the kitchen table, and she didn’t know if that meant he wasn’t coming back or if he’d just forgotten it.

I walked back to the station along the canal. The water was brown and slow and a white egret was standing in the shallows so still it looked painted there, and the morning light on its feathers was blinding, actually painful to look at directly, and I stood on the bank and looked anyway until my eyes watered. The egret caught a fish and flew away and the shallows were just shallows again.

I was seventeen when I became the only one. The field was the same. The wheat was new — different lines, different numbers on the stakes — but the work was the same, and the mornings were the same, and the birds came the same way they always came. The rows Tomás and Gordo and Beto had worked were now my rows. I expected to feel lonely and instead felt something I still cannot name — a widening, as if the field had gotten larger without changing size.

Harold hired new boys that season — two brothers from a village near Navojoa, quiet and competent, and a boy named Francisco who had a gift that reminded me of my own, though his was less certain, more contingent on mood and weather. The brothers learned the work steadily, without flair. Francisco learned the way I had learned — his hands learned before his mind did, and sometimes he would stop in the middle of a row and look at a wheat head he’d just crossed with bewilderment, as if his body had done something without asking permission.

I taught them the scissors. I taught them the angle and the pressure and the four-second rhythm, and I watched their hands learn the way mine had learned. Teaching forced me to slow down, to become conscious again of each small motion that had long since merged into a single gesture. In that slowing-down I discovered things about my own technique — compensations my left thumb made when the glumes were tight, a rotation of the wrist I’d developed without deciding to, a way of holding the scissors at a seven-degree angle that kept the blade from bruising the rachilla. I hadn’t known I did any of it.

Harold began calling me his “right hand,” which was a joke that only worked in English and which he explained to me once, patiently, using both hands to demonstrate, and I pretended to understand it because his face when he told jokes had a vulnerability that I did not want to disturb. Outside the wheat he seemed slightly lost — present but not quite participant. I watched him write letters at his desk some evenings, letters he sealed and addressed to places in the United States, and his handwriting was terrible, cramped and urgent, as if the words were trying to escape the page. Whatever case he was making for more money, more seed, more time, I understood that I was part of the evidence. My hands were part of the argument.

He put my name in his field notebooks. R. VEGA, next to the row numbers, next to the yields. The first time I saw my name written in someone else’s hand it looked foreign, like it belonged to someone I hadn’t met.

There was a morning — I must have been eighteen by then, though I wasn’t sure of my exact birthday, my mother had always been imprecise about it, saying only that I was born during the rains — there was a morning when I was in the field alone. The dew was heavy and the wheat was at the stage Harold called “anthesis,” when the flowers open and the pollen is live and the entire field smells like something becoming what it will be. The sun was low and orange behind the Sierra Madre Occidental and the light came across the field at an angle that lit every dewdrop on every wheat head individually, thousands of them, and the field was vibrating — not actually vibrating, but the light on the dew was doing something to the air between the wheat heads that made the whole sixty hectares look alive in a way that had nothing to do with botany. I was standing in the middle of it with my scissors and my hands were working and I saw myself from very far away, as if through the wrong end of a scope: a boy in a field, crossing wheat. And the boy looked small, and correct, and the field looked enormous and indifferent to whether the boy was there or not, and the light didn’t care, and my hands kept moving.

I finished the head I was on, moved to the next, and kept working.

Years later — many years, after Harold had won a prize I learned about from a newspaper someone left on a bench at the bus station, after the program had expanded to fields I’d never seen, after I had become what Harold called a master technician, which was a title that meant my pay was equal to a college graduate’s though I had never been inside a school — a woman came to the station with a camera and a notebook. She was from Mexico City and she wore shoes that were wrong for the field and she asked me questions in a Spanish that was technically perfect and emotionally foreign.

She asked me how I had learned to do the crosses. I told her Harold had taught me. She asked me about the scissors, and I showed her, and she took a photograph of my hands holding the scissors near a wheat head. She asked if she could photograph my hands closer, just the hands, and I held them out and she adjusted the camera angle and I looked at my own hands in the light and they were the hands of an old man — thickened, scarred across the knuckles, the nails short and clean because the work required it.

“You invented a technique,” she said.

“My hands did something,” I said.

“That’s the same thing.”

It wasn’t the same thing, but the explanation would have required words I didn’t have in either language.

She asked me about the other boys, the original ones, and I told her about Tomás and Gordo and Beto, and she wrote their names down and I could see that in her article they would be a paragraph — the bird boys who had been there at the beginning. It would say that four local boys were hired in 1946, that they learned the crossing technique, that one of them stayed. It would not say that Gordo could fix anything with wire and patience, or that Tomás had a shoulder that came apart like a bad joint in a fence post, or that Beto’s slingshot was a work of craftsmanship that deserved a place in a museum of things nobody thought to preserve.

She asked me why I’d stayed.

I looked at the field. It was February. New planting. The wheat was just coming up, pale green against the dark soil.

“The work is here,” I said.

She waited for more, the way journalists do, leaving a silence they expect you to fill with something quotable. I didn’t fill it. She thanked me and walked back to the station building in her wrong shoes, and I watched her go, and then a cloud of grackles came in from the east, maybe two hundred of them, and they settled in the far plots where the Kenyan lines were, and I didn’t chase them. I stood there. The scissors were in my pocket next to a folded receipt for solder that I’d been carrying for three weeks and had no reason to keep. The grackles ate what they ate. After a while they left on their own.
