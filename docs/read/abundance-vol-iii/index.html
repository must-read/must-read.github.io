Abundance Vol. III

Combining Jonathan Swift + Kurt Vonnegut | A Modest Proposal + Slaughterhouse-Five

February 26, 2026

A Satirical Work Born of Two Ghosts

By Jonathan Swift & Kurt Vonnegut (posthumous collaboration, arranged by séance)

PART ONE: THE WRITING SAMPLES

How Each Author Would Approach This Alone

Jonathan Swift — Three Approaches

Approach 1: “A Modest Proposal for the Profitable Containment of the Idle”

Swift treats the GDP-detention paradox as a straightforward policy paper, written in the voice of a reasonable economist who simply cannot understand why anyone would object.

It has come to my attention, through diligent study of the National Accounts, that there exists among us a great untapped reservoir of economic potential: namely, those persons who contribute nothing to the Gross Domestic Product by the simple fact of their freedom.

A free man, wandering about the streets of any American city, purchasing nothing, producing nothing, and consuming only such scraps as he may find or be given, is a drag upon the Nation’s accounts to the tune of zero dollars. He is, in the parlance of the economist, a rounding error. He does not exist.

But confine this same man — place him behind walls of poured concrete, feed him upon a contractually obligated 2,200 calories per diem, assign him a guard (salaried), a case manager (salaried), a medical officer (salaried but rarely present), and a bed manufactured by the lowest bidder in a competitive procurement process — and he becomes, as if by the purest alchemy, a generator of $143 per day in billable revenue. He is no longer nothing. He is a line item. He is, at last, economically visible.

The implications are clear to any thinking person. If one man confined produces $52,195 per annum in economic activity, and one man free produces nothing, then the path to prosperity is not liberation but its opposite. We have been, all this time, making men free when we ought to have been making them productive — which is to say, making them confined.

I propose, therefore, a Bureau of Productive Containment, tasked with identifying those citizens whose economic contribution falls below a threshold to be determined by a panel of reasonable men — say, $40,000 per annum — and transferring them to facilities where their mere existence might be monetized at market rates. The janitor earning $30,000, for instance, might be more profitably detained than employed, a net gain to the Nation of some $22,000 per year, minus the janitor’s now-unnecessary salary, which need no longer be paid.

The janitor, for his part, need not be troubled by any change in his daily routine. He will still mop floors. The floors will simply be those of his own containment facility, and his labor will now be valued at $0.47 per hour rather than $14.50, a savings which must gladden the heart of any fiscal conservative.

I anticipate the objection that this proposal is monstrous. I assure the reader it is nothing of the sort. It is merely arithmetic.

Approach 2: “A Dialogue Between a Citizen and an Economist, Concerning the Nature of Value”

Swift uses the Socratic dialogue form to slowly, methodically trap the economist in the absurdity of his own logic.

CITIZEN: I am a janitor. I clean the floors of a school. I earn thirty thousand dollars a year. Am I productive?

ECONOMIST: You are. Your labor contributes thirty thousand dollars to the Gross Domestic Product.

CITIZEN: And the man in the detention center on Route 9 — the one who sits in a cell all day, who does nothing, who produces nothing — is he productive?

ECONOMIST: He is more productive than you.

CITIZEN: I don’t understand.

ECONOMIST: His existence in that cell generates one hundred and forty-three dollars per day in revenue. Over a year, that is fifty-two thousand, one hundred and ninety-five dollars. You generate thirty thousand. He generates fifty-two thousand. He is, by the measure that matters, more productive than you by a factor of approximately 1.7.

CITIZEN: But he doesn’t do anything.

ECONOMIST: He doesn’t need to. He exists, and his existence has been monetized. You exist also, but your existence has merely been compensated. There is a difference.

CITIZEN: What is the difference?

ECONOMIST: The difference is twenty-two thousand, one hundred and ninety-five dollars.

CITIZEN: I see. And if I were to stop cleaning floors and instead commit a crime—

ECONOMIST: You would become more valuable to the economy. Yes. Though I would prefer you phrase it differently in public.

CITIZEN: How should I phrase it?

ECONOMIST: You might say you have been “transitioned from the labor economy to the containment economy.” It sounds better in a quarterly report.

CITIZEN: And if everyone did this?

ECONOMIST: Then GDP would increase enormously. We would be the most prosperous nation in the history of the world, though the floors would be very dirty.

CITIZEN: Whose floors?

ECONOMIST: The detainees’. But they wouldn’t notice. They are too busy being productive.

Kurt Vonnegut — Two Approaches

Approach 1: “The Gospel from Galt’s Gulch — A Chapter from an Unfinished Novel”

Vonnegut writes this as a chapter from a Kilgore Trout story, framed in his signature short-sentence deadpan.

There was a planet called Economia-7 where the most valuable citizens were the ones who did absolutely nothing.

This is also true on Earth, but on Economia-7 they were honest about it.

On Economia-7, a man who swept floors was worth 30,000 glorks per year. A man who sat in a concrete room and stared at a wall was worth 52,000 glorks per year. The man in the concrete room didn’t choose to be there. He had been placed there by men who owned the concrete room, and who charged the government 143 glorks per day for the privilege of keeping him in it.

So it goes.

The floor-sweeper went home at night to a small apartment where he ate rice and beans and watched a screen that told him the economy had never been better. The man in the concrete room went nowhere, because he was in a concrete room. His rice and beans cost 47 glorks per day, which was paid to a company called GaltCorp, which was listed on the Economia-7 stock exchange at a valuation of 14 billion glorks.

The CEO of GaltCorp had a private island.

The private island had many young employees.

The young employees were called “kittens.”

This was considered a joke.

 

The economy of Economia-7 was measured by a single number called the Gross Domestic Product. The Gross Domestic Product went up when people built hospitals. It also went up when people got sick. It went up when people built prisons. It went up when people were imprisoned. It went up when there were wars. It went up when there were funerals.

The only thing that made the Gross Domestic Product go down was when people sat in parks and did nothing, or when they gardened, or when they played with their children, or when they made love to someone they actually loved.

These activities were called “economically invisible.”

The floor-sweeper was almost economically invisible. He was barely there. He was a ghost who smelled like Pine-Sol.

The man in the concrete room was a beacon of economic activity.

If you wanted to be a good citizen of Economia-7, the best thing you could do was get yourself locked up. The second best thing was to lock someone else up.

The worst thing you could do was sweep a floor.

 

Kilgore Trout wrote this story in 1987 on the back of a paper placemat at a Denny’s in Cohoes, New York. Nobody read it. The waitress threw it away.

She was, economically speaking, worth less than a prisoner.

So it goes.

Approach 2: “An Index of Abundances”

Vonnegut uses a numbered-list format — a catalogue of absurdities presented as flat facts.

Here is a partial list of things that increase the Gross Domestic Product of the United States of America:

  1. Building a hospital. (+GDP)
  2. Filling it with sick people. (+GDP)
  3. A car crash. (+GDP)
  4. The ambulance that comes to the car crash. (+GDP)
  5. The funeral after the ambulance fails. (+GDP)
  6. A man sweeping a floor. (+$30,000 GDP)
  7. The same man, placed in a for-profit detention cell. (+$52,195 GDP)
  8. The man is now worth more. He is not happier. Nobody asked about happy.
  9. The company that owns the cell is traded on the New York Stock Exchange. Its ticker symbol is GEO. This is short for The GEO Group, Inc. It is not short for geography, though geography — specifically, the geography of being inside a room you cannot leave — is its primary product.
  10. The CEO of this company earns $5.3 million per year.
  11. This is the equivalent of 176 janitors.
  12. Or 101 prisoners.
  13. But only one CEO.
  14. Abundance.
  15. A private island in the Caribbean where the staff are young and called “kittens.” (+GDP)
  16. A penthouse in New York City where the weekends are “abundant.” (+GDP)
  17. A man in a concrete room who has not seen the sky in nine weeks. (+GDP)
  18. A janitor who sees the sky every day on his walk to work, because he cannot afford a car. (-GDP, because he is not buying gasoline)
  19. The janitor is a drag on the economy.
  20. The prisoner is an engine of growth.
  21. The kittens are not listed in any economic index.
  22. Nobody asks about the kittens.
  23. Abundance.

 

If this were a Kilgore Trout story, it would end with the janitor and the prisoner meeting in a hallway. The janitor would be mopping. The prisoner would be generating revenue. They would look at each other. Neither would say anything.

There would be nothing to say.

The floor would be clean, though.

PART TWO: THE COLLABORATION

In Which Two Dead Men Argue and Write Something Together

The Séance

The medium — a woman named Dolores who charged $200 per hour, which contributed positively to GDP — lit two candles and placed two typewriters on the table. One was an 18th-century quill-and-ink set. The other was a Smith Corona portable, the same model Vonnegut used before he switched to a Selectric and then to felt-tip pen on legal pads.

“They’re here,” said Dolores.

The quill began to move.

The Meeting

SWIFT: I have read your work, Mr. Vonnegut. You write as a man who has eaten something that disagreed with him and has decided to describe the taste to the entire world, sentence by sentence, in the plainest possible language. I admire this.

VONNEGUT: Thanks. I’ve read yours too. You write like a man who’s smiling at someone while holding a knife behind his back, and the someone knows about the knife, and you know they know, and somehow this goes on for forty pages. I admire this also.

SWIFT: I am told we are to collaborate.

VONNEGUT: On a piece about GDP and detention centers and rich men on islands with young women they call kittens.

SWIFT: Yes. I wrote a proposal for a Bureau of Productive Containment. I used a Socratic dialogue to let an economist hang himself with his own logic.

VONNEGUT: I wrote a Kilgore Trout story about a planet called Economia-7. And a numbered list. Lists are good. People think they’re reading something organized. They’re actually reading something that’s falling apart.

The Argument

SWIFT: My concern with your approach is that it is too kind.

VONNEGUT: Kind?

SWIFT: You write “so it goes” and the reader feels sadness. Sadness is comfortable. Sadness lets the reader believe they are on the right side — that they feel the injustice and therefore are not complicit in it. I do not want the reader to feel sad. I want the reader to feel implicated.

VONNEGUT: And my concern with your approach is that it’s too clever. You write a perfect logical trap, the reader admires the mechanism, and then they put it down and go on with their day. They’ve appreciated the craft. They haven’t felt the floor under them disappear. Your Modest Proposal — the original one — people have been praising its structure for three hundred years. How many of them have stopped eating?

SWIFT: …

VONNEGUT: I want the guy reading this on his phone on the subway to look up from the screen and think, oh. Not “how clever.” Not “how sad.” Just oh. That little silence where something shifts.

SWIFT: You want the knife to be invisible.

VONNEGUT: I want there to be no knife. I want the reader to realize they’ve been bleeding the whole time.

SWIFT: That is — I must admit — a finer cruelty than mine.

VONNEGUT: But I need your cruelty too. I need the structure. The deadpan gets people in the door. The logic keeps them in the room. Without your logic, my sadness is just sadness. Without my sadness, your logic is just a game.

SWIFT: The frame must be the television program. Your talk-show format. It is the natural habitat of the modern huckster.

VONNEGUT: Agreed. But we intercut it with the other perspectives. The janitor. The detainee. The numbered facts. Stage directions that say the quiet parts.

SWIFT: And the ending?

VONNEGUT: The janitor. The floor. The sky.

SWIFT: Let’s begin.

PART THREE: THE WORK

ABUNDANCE VOL. III

A Variety Program for the End of Accountability

by Jonathan Swift & Kurt Vonnegut

SCENE: A television studio in New York City, which contributes $1.8 trillion to the GDP of the United States, or approximately the combined economic output of detaining every man, woman, and child in the state of Ohio. The set is beautiful. It cost $4.2 million to design and build. A janitor keeps it clean for $31,000 a year, which is less than the set’s monthly flower budget, which is $3,800, because the flowers must be fresh, because the cameras are high-definition, because the audience deserves beauty, because this is America.

The janitor’s name is Edgar. But we won’t learn that for a while.

I. COLD OPEN

HOST: (to camera, smiling, immaculate, his teeth a $47,000 monument to cosmetic dentistry) The US economy is booming. GDP has never been higher. But recent polls show that the average citizen has no respect for this booming economy that’s been handed to them. Gifted to them. By the fine people of Galt’s Gulch.

(Galt’s Gulch is a reference to Ayn Rand’s novel Atlas Shrugged, in which the world’s most productive citizens withdraw to a hidden valley and allow civilization to collapse without them. In the novel, this is presented as heroic. In the real world, the withdrawing is done to private islands, and civilization doesn’t collapse — it merely deteriorates, slowly, for the people who mop the floors, while improving considerably for the people on the islands. But this is not what the host means. The host has not read the novel. The host has read the jacket copy and found it agreeable.)

HOST: Here to educate them on this economic bounty are the authors of the book Abundance — coincidentally, the third book titled Abundance in recent years, which is in itself proof of the economic abundance the average citizen is refusing to acknowledge. Please welcome Prescott Kroll and Tad Renner!

(Applause. The applause sign is controlled by a technician named Wei who is paid $22 per hour, which is less than the per-hour cost of detaining a migrant in a CoreCivic facility in Dilley, Texas, but more than the per-hour wage of the migrant’s labor if the facility subcontracts it out, which it does, at $1 to $4 per day, which is legal, which is another word for abundant.)

(PRESCOTT KROLL and TAD RENNER walk on stage. Prescott is tall, silver-haired, and wears a watch that costs more than Edgar the janitor earns in a year. Tad is shorter, rounder, and laughs before he speaks, to let you know that whatever he is about to say is not to be taken seriously, which is how he gets away with saying it.)

II. THE AUTHORS

PRESCOTT: Thank you for having us. This is such a beautiful studio.

(It is. Edgar mopped the floors at 4 AM.)

HOST: So, Abundance Vol. III. What’s the thesis?

PRESCOTT: The thesis is simple. America is richer than it has ever been. The GDP is at an all-time high. Corporate earnings are extraordinary. Productivity is up. The stock market — I mean, look at the stock market.

TAD: (laughing preemptively) It’s incredible.

PRESCOTT: And yet — and yet — you ask people on the street, “How’s the economy?” and they say, “Terrible.” They say, “I can’t afford groceries.” They say, “I’m working two jobs.” And you have to ask — respectfully — are you looking at the same numbers we’re looking at?

(They are not. Prescott is looking at the Bloomberg Terminal in his office, which costs $24,000 per year to subscribe to. The person on the street is looking at the price of eggs, which has increased 65% in two years. These are different numbers. They live in different economies. But the GDP counts both of them, and the GDP says everything is fine.)

TAD: The problem is vibes. People are going on vibes instead of data.

HOST: And your book corrects this.

TAD: Our book shows, empirically, with data, that abundance is everywhere. You just have to know where to look.

(You have to know where to look. The abundance is in the quarterly earnings reports of private equity firms. It is in the compensation packages of C-suite executives. It is in the real estate portfolios of family offices. It is on private islands. It is not in the grocery store. It is not in the janitor’s apartment. But it is somewhere, and therefore it exists, and therefore the economy is booming. QED.)

III. THE ISLAND

HOST: Now Prescott, I understand you recently visited the Galt’s Gulch retreat?

PRESCOTT: I did. I did. (He leans back. He is about to tell a story. He has told this story before, at dinners, on yachts, in the back seats of cars that cost more than houses.) Well, I was down visiting the Galt’s Gulch private Caribbean island, and I can tell you — there was certainly an abundant supply of youthful kitties.

(The HOST laughs. TAD laughs. The audience laughs. The laughter is genuine. This is a joke among men who understand each other.)

(The island is real. It is in the Lesser Antilles. It was purchased in 2019 for $63 million by a consortium of investors whose primary holdings are in private detention, private healthcare, and private education — three industries that share a common business model, which is: take a public need, restrict access to it, and charge the government to provide it badly.)

(The “kittens” are real. They are between the ages of — )

(We are advised by counsel not to complete this sentence.)

(Instead, here is an economic fact: the island employs 340 staff members. Their average wage is $6,200 per year. The island’s annual operating budget is $41 million. The ratio of the operating budget to total staff compensation is approximately 19:1. The remaining 18 parts go to imported wine, helicopter maintenance, and things that are not listed in any budget because they are paid in cash to people who do not file taxes because they do not, in the legal sense, exist.)

(Edgar the janitor is not on the island. Edgar is in the studio, replacing a trash bag. He has never been to the Caribbean. He has been to Coney Island twice.)

IV. THE APARTMENT

TAD: (laughing, always laughing) Oh, I can top that. Abundance is certainly a word I would pin to my weekend at the Galt’s Gulch famous New York City apartment!

(The Galt’s Gulch New York City apartment occupies the top two floors of a building on East 66th Street. It is technically owned by a holding company registered in Delaware, which is owned by a trust domiciled in South Dakota, which is administered by a law firm in Geneva, which represents a client whose name appears on no document that any journalist has ever obtained. The apartment has fourteen rooms, a private elevator, and a guest book that has been subpoenaed twice and produced never.)

(What happens in the apartment is, legally speaking, a matter of conjecture. What is known: young women enter. Young women leave. Cars arrive at night. Cars depart before dawn. The doorman’s salary was recently doubled, and a confidentiality clause was added to his employment contract. The flowers in the lobby are fresh. A janitor keeps the hallway clean. He is not Edgar. His name is Tomás, and he has learned not to make eye contact with the men who arrive after 10 PM, because eye contact is a kind of witnessing, and witnessing is a kind of liability, and liability is expensive, and the whole point of abundance is that the expensive things are handled by someone else.)

PRESCOTT: (grinning) Yeah. Let them eat youthful kitties!

(The audience ROARS. It is the biggest laugh of the evening. Somewhere in the building, Edgar pauses. He has heard the roar through the walls. He does not know what is funny. He will never know what is funny. He is separated from the joke by a soundproofed wall and an annual income of $31,000 and the fundamental architecture of who is inside the room and who is outside the room, which is, when you think about it, also the fundamental architecture of a detention center.)

(The joke is: powerful men can say what they do, out loud, on television, and it is received as humor. The confession is the entertainment. The crime is the punchline. The audience’s laughter is the acquittal.)

(This has always been true. I wrote about it in 1729. It was true then also.)

(— J. Swift)

(So it goes.)

(— K. Vonnegut)

V. THE NUMBERS

(A brief intermission, during which the following facts are displayed on screen in a clean sans-serif font, because facts look better in sans-serif, which is another thing that costs money:)

1. The average annual cost to detain one person in a private facility in the United States is $52,195.

2. The average annual salary of a janitor in the United States is $31,990.

3. The janitor cleans. The detainee sits. The detainee is worth more.

4. The GEO Group, Inc., which operates private detention facilities, reported $2.4 billion in revenue in 2023. Its stock price increased 187% between 2024 and 2025.

5. The increase was attributed to “favorable policy environments,” which is a way of saying that more people were being put in concrete rooms, which is a way of saying the product was selling well.

6. The CEO of GEO Group earned $5.3 million in total compensation in 2023.

7. This is the equivalent of 165 janitors.

8. Or 101 detainees.

9. But only one CEO.

10. The CEO does not mop floors.

11. The CEO does not sit in a concrete room.

12. The CEO sits in an office, and people bring him things, and the things they bring him are numbers, and the numbers go up, and when the numbers go up the stock goes up, and when the stock goes up the CEO’s compensation goes up, and this is called the economy, and the economy is booming.

13. The janitor’s wage, adjusted for inflation, has decreased 4.2% since 2019.

14. The cost of detaining one person has increased 23% in the same period.

15. This means that the gap between the value of a free man cleaning a floor and a confined man sitting in a cell has widened.

16. Freedom is becoming cheaper. Confinement is becoming more expensive. The market has spoken.

17. Abundance.

18. On the island, the “kittens” serve drinks that cost $43 each. They are paid $3.10 per hour. The ratio of drink cost to hourly wage is 13.9:1. This means that a kitten must work approximately 14 hours to afford one of the drinks she serves. She does not drink them. She is not invited to drink them. She is invited to smile.

19. Nobody asks the kittens about abundance.

20. Nobody asks the janitor about abundance.

21. Nobody asks the detainee about abundance.

22. The people who are asked about abundance are the people who have abundance, which is like asking a man who has eaten a seven-course meal whether hunger exists.

23. He will say no. He will say it with conviction. He will write a book about it.

24. The book will be called Abundance.

25. It will be the third book with that title.

26. Abundance.

VI. LET THEM EAT KITTIES

(We return to the studio. The lighting is warm. Everyone is comfortable. Comfort is a product, like detention. You can sell it to some people by taking it from others.)

HOST: So what do you say to the critics? The people who say this abundance isn’t reaching the average American?

PRESCOTT: I say: open your eyes. Look around you. This studio — this beautiful studio — do you think this exists in a poor country?

(The studio exists because Edgar mopped the floor at 4 AM. But yes.)

TAD: (laughing) I always say, if you can’t see the abundance, you’re not looking hard enough. Or you’re looking in the wrong places.

(The wrong places: grocery stores, hospitals, schools, apartments, bus stops, pay stubs, bank statements, the inside of a detention cell.)

(The right places: Bloomberg Terminals, stock tickers, private islands, the New York City apartment, earnings calls, the inside of a yacht.)

PRESCOTT: The economy doesn’t lie. GDP doesn’t lie. The numbers are right there.

(The numbers are right there. The numbers say that a man in a cage is worth more than a man with a mop. The numbers say that a weekend on an island with “kittens” generates more economic activity than a year of cleaning floors. The numbers say that confinement is productive and freedom is wasteful and youth is a commodity and the floor is always dirty and somebody should really do something about the floor, but not at a wage that would threaten the margins.)

(The numbers don’t lie. But they don’t tell the truth, either. They tell a story, and the story is always told by the people who own the numbers, and the people who own the numbers are on the island, and the people on the island are laughing, and the laughter sounds like )

(—applause—)

HOST: Prescott Kroll and Tad Renner, authors of Abundance Vol. III. The book is available everywhere.

(Everywhere that sells books. Not everywhere that has abundance. Those are different everywheres.)

PRESCOTT: Thank you.

TAD: (laughing) Thank you.

(They stand. They shake hands. They leave the stage. They will go to dinner at a restaurant where the cheapest entrée costs $62 and the waiter is a man named David who has a master’s degree in English literature and who earns $2.13 per hour plus tips, which is less than the per-hour cost of detaining a human being but more than the per-hour wage of a kitten on the island, which positions David, economically speaking, somewhere between a prisoner and a kitten, which is where most people are, which is abundance’s best-kept secret: there is plenty for everyone, and almost no one gets any.)

VII. THE FLOOR

The studio empties.

The cameras turn off. The lights go down. The set, which looked so expensive in high definition, looks like what it is in the dark: plywood and paint and cable and dust.

Edgar comes in with his mop.

He starts in the back corner, the way he always does. He works toward the door. His mop makes a sound on the floor: a slow, rhythmic swish. It is the only sound in the building. It is not recorded. It is not broadcast. It generates $14.90 per hour in economic activity, which is less than the cost of a single drink on the island, less than the per diem of a single detainee, less than the per-minute cost of the host’s airtime.

Edgar does not know about the island. He does not know about the apartment. He does not know about the kittens. He does not know that a man sitting in a cell in Texas is worth more to the economy than he is. He knows the floor is dirty. He knows how to make it clean. He knows that tomorrow it will be dirty again, and he will clean it again, and this will continue until he retires or dies, whichever comes first, and neither event will be noted in any quarterly earnings report.

The floor is clean.

Nobody sees it.

 

Somewhere in Texas, in a facility operated by a company whose stock price has increased 187% in the last year, a man lies on a thin mattress in a concrete room. He has not committed a crime. He is being held pending a hearing that has been delayed nine times. His detention generates $143 per day. He is, in the language of economics, productive. In the language of everything else, he is a man lying on a mattress in a room he cannot leave.

He stares at the ceiling. The ceiling is clean.

A janitor cleaned it.

The janitor was paid less than this man’s daily cost of detention.

The man does not know this.

The janitor does not know this.

The CEO knows this. The CEO knows all of it. The CEO is on the island.

The drinks are $43.

The kittens are smiling.

The economy is booming.

 

Edgar finishes the floor. He puts his mop in the bucket. He wheels the bucket to the closet. He takes off his coveralls and hangs them on the hook. He puts on his jacket. It is a thin jacket. It is February.

He walks outside.

He can see the sky.

This contributes nothing to GDP.

END

The Formula

Author A Jonathan Swift
  • The feigned-reasonable policy voice — a Bureau of Productive Containment proposed with the tone of a quarterly earnings call, the economist's 'I assure the reader it is nothing of the sort. It is merely arithmetic' performing exactly the move of the original Modest Proposal
  • Coiling sentences that turn back on themselves like snakes eating their own tails, euphemisms thinning toward transparency — 'favorable policy environments' meaning more people in concrete rooms, 'youthful kitties' meaning what counsel advises against completing
  • The Socratic dialogue where the economist hangs himself with his own definitions — the citizen asking questions whose answers are self-evidently monstrous, delivered with procedural calm
Author B Kurt Vonnegut
  • Short declarative sentences like doors closing — 'The floor was clean. Nobody saw it.' — and parenthetical stage directions that pull the camera back to show what's actually in the room while the main text continues its performance
  • 'So it goes' deployed as structural refrain marking each point where horror is flattened into economic fact, the phrase simultaneously accepting and refusing to accept
  • The Kilgore Trout planetary parable (Economia-7), the numbered-list format where organized reading dissolves into something falling apart, the flat enumeration of absurdities that refuses to editorialize because the numbers are the editorial
Work X A Modest Proposal
  • The satirical proposal pushed to logical extreme — if a detained man generates more GDP than a free man, the path to prosperity is confinement, not liberation — echoing Swift's original move of making the monstrous proposal sound more reasonable than the humane alternative
  • The reasonable narrator who anticipates and dismisses objections as sentimental, the arithmetic deployed as argument-ender, the entire edifice constructed so the reader cannot find the seam between logic and horror
  • The structural inheritance of pamphlet-as-weapon: a document that performs reasonableness so thoroughly that the reader must choose between accepting the conclusion or rejecting the premise that produced it
Work Y Slaughterhouse-Five
  • 'So it goes' as the piece's recurring punctuation — marking deaths, indignities, and economic absurdities with the same flat refusal to distinguish between them, exactly as the original marks every death in the novel
  • The telescoping between the intimate and the systemic — Edgar's thin jacket in February beside the $2.4 billion revenue figure, the kitten's $3.10/hour beside the $43 drink — mundanity and atrocity sharing the same register without commentary
  • The invisible witness emerging in parenthetical asides, simultaneously present and detached, the author's voice breaking through the performance to say 'I wrote about it in 1729. It was true then also' — the long view of history as repetition
