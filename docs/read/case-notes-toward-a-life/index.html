Case Notes Toward a Life

Combining Derek Raymond + Virginia Woolf | He Died with His Eyes Open by Derek Raymond + Mrs Dalloway by Virginia Woolf

March 1, 2026

STOCKHOLM POLICE AUTHORITY

Criminal Investigation Division — Violent Crimes Unit

Case No. 2025-VCU-04471 Subject: LINDQVIST, Signe E. Investigating Officer: Detective Inspector Axel Rask, Badge 4419 Date of Initial Report: 18 November 2025 Classification: Homicide — Active Investigation

Section 1: Discovery and Initial Assessment

On Monday 18 November 2025, at 09:14, the Violent Crimes Unit received notification from responding officers at Södermalm Station regarding a deceased female at Bondegatan 43, apartment 4B, Södermalm, Stockholm. The deceased was subsequently identified as Signe Eleonora Lindqvist, age 41, resident at the address since 2017, employed as senior archivist at the Stockholms Stadsarkiv (Stockholm City Archives).

The body was discovered by the building superintendent, Tomas Hedlund, who entered the apartment at 08:52 after a downstairs neighbor reported water damage to her ceiling. Hedlund found the kitchen tap running — had been running, he estimated, for at least twelve hours, based on the volume of water that had pooled on the kitchen floor and seeped through to the apartment below. The deceased was found in the living room, seated on the floor with her back against the radiator beneath the window. The radiator was cold. The heating in the building is centrally controlled and had been operating normally; the valve on the radiator in the living room was in the closed position. She had turned it off. The window was open approximately eight centimeters.

I arrived at 10:20. The flat was on the fourth floor, east-facing. The morning light came in through the open window and fell across the floor in a bar that reached almost to where she sat. Her right hand was in her lap. Her left hand was extended slightly, palm up, the fingers loosely curled. The position did not suggest a fall. It suggested sitting down.

She was wearing a grey wool cardigan over a dark blue dress. No shoes. Thick socks, the kind with patterns — these had a pattern of small birds. Wrens, I think, or something similar. I noted this in my field report and then looked at what I’d written and could not determine why I’d written it.

Preliminary cause of death: blunt force trauma to the left temporal region. A single blow. The medical examiner’s preliminary assessment, delivered at scene, was that death occurred between 16:00 and 20:00 on Sunday 17 November. A more precise estimate would follow autopsy.

The flat was orderly. Not clean — orderly. Books on shelves, organized by some system I could not immediately identify. Later examination revealed the organization was chronological by the date she’d finished reading each book, not by author or title. A notation in pencil on the inside back cover of each volume: a date, and sometimes a single word. The word in the last book she’d finished — a collection of Tranströmer’s poetry, annotated — was attending.

On her desk: a notebook. A5, hardbound, dark green cover, approximately sixty pages filled. This notebook was catalogued as Evidence Item 12 and subsequently entered into the case file.

I should not have read it at the scene. Procedure requires evidence collection, logging, and review at the station under controlled conditions. I read it at her desk, standing in the light from the east-facing window, while the forensic team worked in the living room and someone’s radio played low in the corridor. I read twelve pages before I closed it and placed it in the evidence bag. This was a procedural deviation. I note it here for the record.

Section 2: Evidence Item 12 — Excerpts from Notebook (Transcribed)

Note: The following are selected transcriptions from the notebook recovered at the scene. Entries are undated but appear to cover a period of approximately four months. The handwriting is small and regular, with occasional words crossed out and replaced. Transcriptions are verbatim.

The light at 07:00 in November is not light. It is the rumor of light. The sky above Bondegatan is the color of wet cement and the streetlamps are still on and the kitchen window shows me my own reflection more clearly than it shows me the street. I drink my coffee in this doubled state — watching myself watching — and the coffee is too hot and too strong and precisely what is needed. The ceramic warm in both hands. At 07:12 the sky changes. Not brightens. Relents. The grey becomes less certain of itself.

Walked to Nytorget at lunch. The chestnuts are finished — the last ones fell in the gust on Thursday and now the pavement has that look of aftermath. The kiosk on the corner had its awning rolled up though there was no sun. A woman at the bus stop was reading a paperback and turning the pages without looking up when the bus came. She let two buses pass. I wanted to know what she was reading. I wanted to sit next to her and say nothing and read alongside her the way you can sometimes read alongside a stranger on the tunnelbana, catching phrases in the periphery, constructing a parallel text from fragments. But I didn’t. I bought a kanelbulle at the bakery on Skånegatan and ate it walking back and the sugar on my fingers felt like evidence of something.

Processed the Bergström estate today. Fourteen boxes. Letters from 1943-1967 between a woman in Norrköping and her sister in Gävle. Twenty-four years of correspondence and the sister in Gävle never once mentioned her husband by name. He was han — him — or min man — my husband — or simply absent from the sentence entirely, a grammatical hole around which the prose arranged itself. I handled these letters with cotton gloves and the paper was soft from age and I could feel the pressure of the pen through the cotton. She pressed hard. The sister in Norrköping pressed hard when she wrote, as though the words needed to be driven into the paper to survive the transit. And now I am cataloguing them and the pressure of her pen is reduced to a metadata field: Correspondence, personal. Bergström, A. to Karlsson, H. 1943-1967. 312 items. Condition: fair to poor.

This is what archives do. We take a life and produce a finding aid. We take twenty-four years of a woman pressing her words into paper and we give it a call number.

Section 3: Scene Forensics and Witness Canvass

The forensic examination of the apartment was completed on 19 November. Key findings:

  • No signs of forced entry. The front door lock (Assa Abloy d12, double cylinder) was undamaged. The door was unlocked when the superintendent entered.
  • Blood evidence consistent with the injury was found on the living room floor and the base of the radiator. Spatter pattern indicated the victim was already seated or at floor level when the blow was struck.
  • The weapon was recovered at the scene: a pestle, granite, approximately 18 cm in length, belonging to a mortar-and-pestle set found in the kitchen. The mortar was on the kitchen counter with dried herbs — identified as thyme — still in the bowl. The pestle had been washed. Traces of blood were recovered from the junction between the handle and the grinding surface.
  • No fingerprints other than the victim’s were recovered from the pestle. The washing had been thorough.
  • A second coffee cup was on the kitchen counter, unwashed, approximately half full. Coffee residue and a partial lip print, not matching the victim’s. DNA sample collected.

The canvass of residents at Bondegatan 43 produced the following of note:

Hedlund, Tomas (superintendent, ground floor): Described Lindqvist as “quiet, polite, always carried books.” Could not recall any regular visitors. Stated that on the evening of 17 November, he heard footsteps on the stairs at approximately 19:00, which he described as “heavier than hers.” He did not investigate. He was watching television and the sound was “not unusual, people come and go.”

Alexandersson, Britt-Marie (apartment 3B, directly below): Reported hearing music from 4B on Sunday afternoon — “something classical, not loud, she always played music on Sundays.” Stated the music stopped “around five or six” and she heard nothing unusual afterward until the water came through her ceiling Monday morning. When asked if she’d heard voices, she said: “I heard her talking once. On the phone, I thought, but it was a Sunday. It could have been she was reading aloud. She sometimes read aloud. I could hear the rhythm through the floor but not the words.”

She sometimes read aloud. This detail appears in Lindqvist’s notebook — an entry I have not yet transcribed for this report — in which she describes reading a passage from Ekelöf and finding that the poem only became itself when spoken, that the meaning lived in the mouth and not on the page, and that her empty flat was not empty when she was reading aloud because the words occupied the rooms the way furniture does, the way another person would.

The investigation proceeds along established evidentiary channels.

The investigation proceeds.

Section 4: Evidence Item 12 — Additional Excerpts

Sunday. The quality of Sunday is different from other days and I have never been able to determine whether this is subjective — the knowledge that it is Sunday altering perception — or whether the light itself is different, the traffic patterns shifting the sound of the city into a lower register, the absence of delivery trucks and construction creating a silence that is not silence but a different composition of noise. On Sundays I play Sibelius or Grieg. Today it was the Valse Triste. I made coffee and sat at the kitchen table and the music filled the flat the way water fills a basin — from the bottom up, the bass notes pooling on the floor and the higher registers rising to the ceiling where they pressed against the plaster and came back changed.

I notice that I have begun cataloguing my own days the way I catalogue the dead. Date, condition, contents. What arrived in the mail. What was said at the counter of the ICA on Folkungagatan. The man who bags the groceries — not a boy, a man, perhaps fifty, with hands that handle the celery and the milk with the same care, the same attention, as though each item were a document being placed in an acid-free folder. I want to tell him this. I want to say: you handle groceries the way an archivist handles letters. You are preserving something, even if the preservation lasts only until the bag is opened at home and the contents enter the disorder of a kitchen.

But I don’t say it. I smile and take the bag and the weight of it is specific — 3.2 kilos perhaps, a particular heaviness that will never be repeated because I will never buy this exact combination of items again — and I walk home and the bag swings against my thigh and the rhythm of the swinging is the rhythm of my walking and the rhythm of my walking is the rhythm of Bondegatan at 17:40 on a Thursday in November, which is the rhythm of a city that does not notice me.

Section 5: Suspect Identification and Interview Summary

DNA from the second coffee cup was matched on 22 November to Johan Rydell, age 38, previously known to the victim. Employment records and social media analysis established that Rydell had been employed as a photographer for the Stockholms Stadsarkiv from September 2023 to April 2024, working in the same department as Lindqvist on a digitization project. His contract was not renewed. Rydell’s current address: Hammarbyhamnen, no fixed employment since August 2024.

Rydell was brought in for questioning on 23 November at 10:00.

Excerpt from interview transcript (translated from Swedish):

RASK: When did you last see Signe Lindqvist?

RYDELL: Sunday. I went to her flat on Sunday afternoon.

RASK: What time?

RYDELL: I don’t know exactly. Three, maybe. Three-thirty.

RASK: Why did you go?

RYDELL: She’d asked me. She’d — we’d been in contact again. After the archive. She reached out in September. She wanted to talk about the project. The digitization. She had ideas about — I don’t know how to explain this — about the photographs themselves. The originals. She thought digitizing them was a kind of death. That was her word. She said we were killing the photographs by making them searchable. That the point of an archive was that things were difficult to find, that the difficulty was part of the meaning.

RASK: And on Sunday?

RYDELL: We had coffee. We talked. She showed me something she’d written in her notebook — about the Bergström letters, about how the archive reduces things. She was — she was agitated. Not angry. Agitated in the way she got when something mattered to her. Her hands moved. She talked fast. She said she’d been thinking about leaving the archive, about whether preservation was an act of love or an act of violence.

RASK: When did you leave?

RYDELL: Around five. Five-fifteen maybe. She walked me to the door. She was fine.

RASK: Fine.

RYDELL: She was alive. She was standing in the doorway and the light from the kitchen was behind her and she was — she was alive. That’s what I’m telling you. When I left she was alive.

End excerpt.

Rydell was not detained. Forensic evidence places him in the apartment but does not place him in the apartment at the time of death. The coffee cup, half-finished, is consistent with a visit that ended before evening. No defensive wounds on Rydell. No blood on his clothing (surrendered voluntarily for testing on 23 November).

The investigation continues along other lines.

Section 5b: Additional Interview — Colleague

Interview with Elisabet Falk, Senior Conservator, Stockholms Stadsarkiv. Conducted 24 November 2025, 14:30.

Excerpt from transcript:

RASK: How would you describe your relationship with Signe Lindqvist?

FALK: Professional. Friendly. We had lunch together most days. She was — she was difficult to know well. Not cold. The opposite. She felt things very strongly, but she contained it. You could see it in her work. The care she took. Other archivists process materials. Signe processed them the way you’d — I don’t know — dress a wound. Carefully. As though the documents could feel what was happening to them.

RASK: Was she in a relationship?

FALK: She didn’t talk about that. She talked about work, about books, about the city. She loved Stockholm in a way that most Stockholmers don’t — not casually, not with ownership. She paid attention to it. She’d come into the office and say she’d seen something on the walk from the tunnelbana — a particular shadow on a particular wall — and she’d describe it in a way that made you realize you’d walked past the same wall every day and never seen it.

RASK: Did she seem worried about anything? Frightened?

FALK: Worried, no. Restless. She’d been restless for months. She told me she wasn’t sure she could keep doing the work. Not because she didn’t love it — because she loved it in a way that was consuming her. She said she spent all day preserving other people’s lives and had stopped living her own. I thought she meant she needed a holiday.

RASK: Did she mention anyone who might want to harm her?

FALK: No. She mentioned almost no one. That was the thing about Signe. She was surrounded by people all day — colleagues, researchers, visitors — and she was fundamentally alone. Not lonely. Alone. She’d made a space around herself and she lived in it with tremendous intensity and I don’t think she wanted anyone to enter it permanently.

End excerpt.

I note that this last observation — she’d made a space around herself and she lived in it with tremendous intensity — is not, in the strictest sense, evidentiary. I include it because a colleague’s assessment of the victim’s inner life, however inadmissible, contributes to the investigative understanding of who this woman was.

Section 6: Evidence Item 12 — Later Entries

Note: The following entries appear to be from the final weeks before the victim’s death. The handwriting becomes less regular. Some words are written over, as though corrected or re-chosen.

The tunnelbana at 08:15. Standing room only. I am pressed between a man reading Dagens Nyheter — he folds it expertly, a half-page at a time, the kind of fold that takes years to learn — and a girl of perhaps seventeen listening to something through earbuds, her lips moving slightly, not singing, rehearsing. She is rehearsing something. A speech, a poem, a part in a play. I will never know which. The train enters the tunnel between Medborgarplatsen and Gamla Stan and the windows become mirrors and for a moment everyone in the carriage is doubled — the real version and the reflection, the inside and the outside, the person they are being and the person the glass shows them to be — and I think this is what consciousness is, this doubling, this perpetual state of being both the observer and the observed, and the train rocks slightly on the curve and my shoulder touches the newspaper man’s elbow and neither of us acknowledges the contact because in Stockholm you do not acknowledge the contact, the body’s proximity is edited out of the social text, and the girl’s lips keep moving, rehearsing her silent part in something I cannot hear and will never hear, and the train emerges from the tunnel and the reflections vanish and we are ourselves again, singular, untouched.

Today I handled a photograph from 1912. A woman standing in Humlegården in a white dress, facing away from the camera, looking at something beyond the frame. The photograph has survived 113 years. The woman has not. The tree she’s looking at has not. The park bench behind her has been replaced four times. Nothing in the photograph still exists except the photograph. And the photograph is about to be scanned at 600 dpi and assigned a metadata field and filed in a server room in Kista where the temperature is maintained at 18 degrees and the humidity at 40% and no one will touch the original again.

I pressed my thumb to the edge of the print. Through the cotton gloves. I felt the paper resist, slightly, the way skin resists. The photograph does not want to be archived. It wants to be held.

There is a florist on Götgatan that I pass every day and I have never gone in. The flowers in the window change with the season — lilies now, white and oppressive in their sweetness, crowded in galvanized buckets that catch the streetlight. I stand outside and look at the flowers and I do not go in because going in would be an act of acquisition and I am interested in the act of attention. The flowers behind the glass are perfect because they are behind the glass. They belong to the looking, not to me.

But today I thought: what if I went in. What if I bought the lilies and carried them home and put them in the tall blue vase on the kitchen windowsill and let them die there over the course of a week, the petals browning at the edges, the water clouding, the sweetness thickening to rot. Would that be an archive? The vase as acid-free folder. The dying as metadata.

I didn’t go in. I walked home. The street was dark already — 15:47 and dark already — and my footsteps on the pavement had that hollow winter sound.

Section 7: Supplemental Findings and Case Status

On 27 November, a second person of interest was identified through building surveillance footage. Camera at Bondegatan 41 (adjacent property) recorded an individual entering the building at 43 at 18:47 on Sunday 17 November and exiting at 19:22. The individual was male, medium build, wearing a dark jacket and knitted cap. The image quality is insufficient for facial identification. Gait analysis is ongoing. The duration of the visit — thirty-five minutes — falls within the estimated window of death.

The front door of Bondegatan 43 does not have a buzzer system. The main entrance is secured by a code lock. Residents share the code with visitors. The code at the time of the incident was 2847. Hedlund (superintendent) confirmed that the code had not been changed since March 2025 and that “most people on the street know it.”

Additional forensic findings (report dated 26 November): analysis of the victim’s hands revealed traces of dried thyme under the fingernails of her right hand, consistent with the herbs found in the mortar. No defensive wounds. No foreign skin cells under fingernails. The medical examiner’s revised estimate of time of death: between 17:30 and 19:30, Sunday 17 November. The narrowing of the window was based on stomach contents — coffee, a small quantity of bread and cheese, consumed approximately two to three hours before death. She had not eaten dinner. She had been grinding thyme.

I should record that the thyme was lemon thyme. Thymus citriodorus. The forensic report lists it as Thymus vulgaris but I returned to the flat on 28 November and the plant on the kitchen windowsill — growing in a small clay pot, still alive, still growing despite twelve days without water because thyme does this, it persists, it withholds its dying — was lemon thyme. The leaves are smaller. The scent, when you crush them between your fingers, is sharp and citric, nothing like common thyme, and she would have known the difference. She was a woman who knew the difference.

I note it for accuracy.

Section 8: Reconstruction of the Victim’s Final Day

Based on evidence collected, interview testimony, and the contents of Evidence Item 12, the following reconstruction of Sunday 17 November 2025 is proposed:

07:00 — Victim wakes. Makes coffee. The notebook’s last entry begins here: Morning. The light at 07:00 is the rumor of light. She drinks coffee at the kitchen table. She writes in the notebook.

07:00 to approximately 12:00 — No direct evidence. Probable activities based on established patterns: reading, writing, listening to music. Alexandersson (3B) reports hearing classical music beginning “late morning.”

12:00 to 15:00 — No direct evidence. Based on the notebook’s references to Sunday routines and the calendar entry marked A, the victim may have spent this period in her flat. She may have been attending. To what, or to whom, has not been established.

15:00 to 17:15 — Rydell arrives. Coffee. Conversation about the archive, the Bergström letters, the question of preservation. The notebook is shown. Rydell departs at approximately 17:15.

17:15 to 17:30 — The victim is alone. She grinds lemon thyme. She prepares, or begins to prepare, a meal she will not eat. The music has stopped, according to Alexandersson. The window is open. Eight centimeters. The November air comes in and the flat cools and she does not close the window or turn on the radiator.

I keep returning to this interval. Fifteen minutes. The time between Rydell’s departure and the beginning of the revised window for death. Fifteen minutes in which she was alive and alone and grinding thyme and the window was open and the city was outside and she was inside and the space between those two conditions — the inside and the outside, the private and the public, the living and the about-to-end — was eight centimeters of open window and the sound of Bondegatan at dusk.

17:30 to 19:30 — The revised window. Someone enters the flat. The door is not forced. She admits them, or the door is unlocked, or they have the code and the apartment door was left on the latch, which Hedlund says she sometimes did “when she was expecting someone back.” Was she expecting someone back? Was the door on the latch because Rydell had just left and she thought he might return for something forgotten? Or was she expecting someone else?

I have tried to reconstruct what the flat would have been like at this hour. I went there at 17:45 on a Wednesday, the closest I could approximate to the conditions. The light was gone. The streetlamp outside the living room window cast an orange rectangle on the ceiling that moved when the wind moved the branches of the birch on the pavement below. The kitchen was dark except for the small light above the stove — the switch was in the on position when the forensic team arrived, suggesting she was using it for light rather than the overhead fixture. She preferred partial illumination. Her notebook mentions this: the overhead light flattens everything, removes the shadows that give a room its architecture. In the stove light, the kitchen would have been warm-toned, close, the kind of light you would cook by, grind herbs by, not the kind of light you would admit a stranger by.

But she admitted someone.

The blow. A single blow. She was already low — seated, or sitting down, or bending. The pestle from her own kitchen. A weapon of proximity, not premeditation. Whoever struck her was already in the kitchen, already close, already in the radius of her life’s most intimate geography. They picked up a tool she had been using — a tool that still held the heat of her hand, that still smelled of lemon thyme from her grinding — and they used it to end her. The domestic object turned lethal. The intimacy of the weapon is the most brutal fact in the case file and it is recorded as: Weapon: pestle, granite, approx. 18 cm. The record does not say that this pestle was part of a set she had owned since 2019 (purchase receipt found in a desk drawer), that the granite was veined with a dark mineral line she might have run her thumb along while grinding, that the mortar still held the herbs she was preparing. The record says pestle, granite, approx. 18 cm and the record is correct.

19:22 — The individual on the surveillance footage exits the building.

After 19:30 — The water runs. The tap in the kitchen. Whether it was turned on by the victim (preparing to wash herbs, to cook, to clean) or by the killer (washing the pestle, washing hands, an act of ablution that looked like an act of housekeeping) has not been determined. The water runs through the night. It pools on the kitchen floor. It seeps through to apartment 3B. It is the water that brings the superintendent. It is the water that finds her.

Section 9: Case Status — 5 December 2025

Gait analysis of the surveillance footage was inconclusive. The individual recorded entering at 18:47 could not be identified from the available imagery. Canvass of neighboring properties and businesses has not produced additional witnesses. The digital forensic review of Lindqvist’s phone and email is ongoing. Preliminary findings indicate contact with Rydell, routine work correspondence, and a recurring calendar event on Sundays marked only with the letter A. The meaning of this entry has not been determined. It may refer to the archive, to a person, or to the act she described in her notebook — attending.

The DNA evidence and the surveillance footage do not converge on the same individual. The case remains open. I continue to work it.

I have read the notebook four times. This is not procedural. There is no investigative justification for reading a victim’s personal writings four times when the content has been transcribed and entered into the case file. The transcription is complete. The notebook is in evidence storage.

But the transcription is not the notebook. The transcription does not contain the pressure of her pen. It does not contain the place where she crossed out loneliness and wrote solitude and then crossed out solitude and wrote nothing, leaving a gap in the sentence that the reader must step across. The transcription does not contain the way her handwriting changes when she’s writing about the light — looser, faster, as though the hand is trying to keep pace with the seeing.

I applied for access to Evidence Item 12 on 3 December. The request was approved. I sat in the evidence review room and held the notebook and turned the pages and I could feel the indentations her pen had made on the pages beneath the pages she’d written on — the ghost pressure of earlier entries pressing through, the way a life presses through the surfaces that contain it.

She wrote, on what I believe was her last entry, Sunday 17 November, the day she died:

Morning. The light at 07:00 is the rumor of light. I have written this before. I write it again because it is true again, differently true, true in the way that the same street is different every time you walk it because you are different every time you walk it. The coffee. The cup. The weight. The window. The reflection. I am here. Specifically here. Not generally alive but specifically here, in this kitchen, at this hour, holding this cup, attending.

She wrote that on the morning of the day someone came to her flat and struck her in the head with a granite pestle and ended her attending. The investigation has not determined who. The investigation continues. The documents accumulate. I file them in the order they arrive, in the manner prescribed, with the notations required. And in the margins of these documents — in the literal margins — I find myself writing things that do not belong in a case file. Things about light. About the particular sound of footsteps on Bondegatan at 17:40 on a Thursday in November. I have never walked on Bondegatan at 17:40 on a Thursday.

Section 10: Addendum — 12 December 2025

I walked to the florist on Götgatan today. I stood outside the window. Lilies. White, in galvanized buckets. I went in. I bought six stems. I do not know why I bought six. The woman wrapped them in brown paper and the paper crinkled and the sound was specific.

I carried them home. They are on the kitchen counter, next to the case files.
