Synopsis

A content authenticity analyst discovers the AI she trained has flagged her anonymous food blog. Her two selves — corporate professional and passionate Igbo writer — can't both be real according to the algorithm. The system isn't wrong. It's just measuring the wrong thing.

Dick's paranoid self-surveillance and dissolving identity collide with Adichie's luminous immigrant code-switching as a Nigerian-born content moderator discovers her company's authenticity algorithm has flagged her own food blog — forcing her to confront which version of herself the machine considers real.

The Formula

Author A Philip K. Dick
  • Paranoid self-surveillance: Adaeze reviewing her own flagged profile echoes Arctor watching himself on security cameras
  • Dissolving identity boundaries: the growing uncertainty about which version of herself is the performance
  • Consumer culture as existential texture: brand names, product placement, corporate jargon as the fabric of daily unreality
Author B Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie
  • Precise sensory realism grounded in Nigerian daily life: food, language, family phone calls, the texture of diaspora
  • Code-switching rendered through specific cultural detail rather than abstract commentary
  • Warmth and political edge in the same sentence: the blog voice that is simultaneously funny, furious, and intimate
Work X Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? by Philip K. Dick
  • The Voigt-Kampff test reimagined as behavioral authenticity scoring — empathy replaced by consistency
  • The bounty hunter who may be the thing she hunts: the moderator flagged by her own system
  • The electric animal as status object: the curated digital self as artificial substitute for genuine presence
Work Y Americanah by Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie
  • The blog as confessional space where the authentic self lives — digital identity vs. embodied performance
  • Food, accent, and names as sites where identity is performed and policed
  • The impossibility of full return: the question of whether the woman who left Nigeria can ever go back unchanged

★★★★★ Jan 8, 2026

This is the most precise thing I've read about algorithmic colonialism in fiction. The behavioral consistency model's assumption — that a real person has one signature, one self — is not a neutral technical decision. It is the replication of a particular Western, monolingual, monocultural norm embedded as universal truth. Adaeze helped build the system, trained it on her own judgment, and now it cannot recognize her. The Benin Bronze passage crystallizes it: the British sell you a postcard of the thing they stole and call it a souvenir. The AI sells you a model of personhood that excludes you and calls it integrity. I teach speculative fiction, and this is going on my syllabus.

★★★★★ Oct 17, 2025

I want to put this in the hands of every patron who tells me cyberpunk is dead. This story takes the genre's core question — who gets to define what's human — and asks it through the experience of a Nigerian immigrant whose two selves are both authentic and mutually unrecognizable to an algorithm. The food blog passages are extraordinary: "the particular heartbreak of eating jollof rice alone in a country that didn't know what it was." That's not just good SF writing, that's just good writing. And the structural choice to never resolve the flag, to leave Adaeze in the 72-hour limbo, trusts the reader to sit with discomfort rather than offering a neat solution. More of this, please.

★★★★ ☆ Feb 15, 2026

The Raleigh-Durham tech campus setting felt genuinely lived-in, not a prop. The detail about Enugu coffee beans that the protagonist's mother dismisses as a scam — that one line does more cultural work than most SF novels manage in a chapter. The behavioral consistency model documentation (Section 3.2: Assumption of Singular Identity) is where the speculative conceit earns its weight. What I kept returning to was the confidence score climbing from 0.87 to 0.91 — the machine getting more certain as Adaeze gets less certain. That inversion is quietly devastating. The ending refuses resolution, which is the right choice. My only reservation: the middle section with the Filipina woman in Houston felt slightly redundant, making the point a second time when Adaeze's own case had already made it.

★★★ ☆☆ Jan 27, 2026

Accomplished prose and a premise that earns its genre placement. The Verity Integrity Suite is a credible near-future extrapolation, and the story's central irony — the analyst flagged by her own system — is well-executed. The food blog voice is genuinely distinct from the professional voice; you can feel the syntactic shift. But structurally, the story circles its thesis rather than developing it. The Benin Bronze passage, the Filipina woman, the cafeteria dead-internet conversation, the mother's phone call — these are all restatements of the same insight, and by the fourth iteration, the insight has lost its edge. The ending is restrained and effective, but the journey to it could have been compressed by a third without losing anything essential. A good story that wanted to be a great one.

JN
Jin Nakamura
★★★★ ☆ Dec 3, 2025

Interesting counterpoint to the usual cyberpunk aesthetic. No neon, no chrome, no rain-slicked streets — just fluorescent office lighting, a cafeteria serving "seasonal" soup, and a woman eating cold jollof on her kitchen floor. The worldbuilding works through mundane accumulation: the thermos ritual, the Ankara wrapper, the hotel mug from Abuja. The tech is near-future and believable, which makes the identity crisis feel immediate rather than speculative. The jollof rice manifesto line ("this is the tragedy of the diaspora, but we cook anyway") does more than any amount of exposition. Cyberpunk has always been about who controls identity — this just asks the question in a register the genre usually ignores.

The tech setup is solid — I've covered content moderation startups in Nairobi and the BCA pipeline rings true. Batch processing, confidence scores, the way a flag shifts the burden of proof. Where it dragged for me was the pacing. The middle third is Adaeze thinking variations of the same thought: the system can't handle people who code-switch. We get it after the second time. The scene with Lena dismissing the flag as a "known bias" queued for Q3 was sharp — that's exactly how this stuff works in real companies. But then the story keeps going and doesn't really land anywhere new. The ending with the climbing confidence score is strong. I just wish the story had been tighter getting there.
