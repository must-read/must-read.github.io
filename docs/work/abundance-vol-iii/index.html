Abundance Vol. III

Combining Jonathan Swift + Kurt Vonnegut | A Modest Proposal + Slaughterhouse-Five

Synopsis

Two dead satirists collaborate via séance on a variety program about GDP, private detention, and the men who profit from confinement — while a janitor named Edgar mops the floor nobody sees.

Swift's feigned-reasonable policy-proposal voice — the satirist smiling while the arithmetic does the killing — fused with Vonnegut's deadpan short sentences and parenthetical stage directions that say the quiet parts aloud. A Modest Proposal provides the structural engine: escalating logical absurdity delivered in the register of reasonableness until the reader is trapped inside the argument. Slaughterhouse-Five supplies the thematic refrain — "so it goes" marking each point where horror flattens into fact, the telescoping between intimate human detail and systemic enormity, mundanity and atrocity sharing the same sentence.

The Formula

Author A Jonathan Swift
  • The feigned-reasonable policy voice — a Bureau of Productive Containment proposed with the tone of a quarterly earnings call, the economist's 'I assure the reader it is nothing of the sort. It is merely arithmetic' performing exactly the move of the original Modest Proposal
  • Coiling sentences that turn back on themselves like snakes eating their own tails, euphemisms thinning toward transparency — 'favorable policy environments' meaning more people in concrete rooms, 'youthful kitties' meaning what counsel advises against completing
  • The Socratic dialogue where the economist hangs himself with his own definitions — the citizen asking questions whose answers are self-evidently monstrous, delivered with procedural calm
Author B Kurt Vonnegut
  • Short declarative sentences like doors closing — 'The floor was clean. Nobody saw it.' — and parenthetical stage directions that pull the camera back to show what's actually in the room while the main text continues its performance
  • 'So it goes' deployed as structural refrain marking each point where horror is flattened into economic fact, the phrase simultaneously accepting and refusing to accept
  • The Kilgore Trout planetary parable (Economia-7), the numbered-list format where organized reading dissolves into something falling apart, the flat enumeration of absurdities that refuses to editorialize because the numbers are the editorial
Work X A Modest Proposal
  • The satirical proposal pushed to logical extreme — if a detained man generates more GDP than a free man, the path to prosperity is confinement, not liberation — echoing Swift's original move of making the monstrous proposal sound more reasonable than the humane alternative
  • The reasonable narrator who anticipates and dismisses objections as sentimental, the arithmetic deployed as argument-ender, the entire edifice constructed so the reader cannot find the seam between logic and horror
  • The structural inheritance of pamphlet-as-weapon: a document that performs reasonableness so thoroughly that the reader must choose between accepting the conclusion or rejecting the premise that produced it
Work Y Slaughterhouse-Five
  • 'So it goes' as the piece's recurring punctuation — marking deaths, indignities, and economic absurdities with the same flat refusal to distinguish between them, exactly as the original marks every death in the novel
  • The telescoping between the intimate and the systemic — Edgar's thin jacket in February beside the $2.4 billion revenue figure, the kitten's $3.10/hour beside the $43 drink — mundanity and atrocity sharing the same register without commentary
  • The invisible witness emerging in parenthetical asides, simultaneously present and detached, the author's voice breaking through the performance to say 'I wrote about it in 1729. It was true then also' — the long view of history as repetition

★★★★★ Oct 16, 2025

Forty years in the civil service and I can tell you: the numbered list in Part III is exactly how a policy briefing is designed to end debate. Item 3 — 'The janitor cleans. The detainee sits. The detainee is worth more.' — that is the authentic register of institutional arithmetic, and it should make anyone who has ever sat in a meeting where such numbers were deployed feel genuinely ill. The Socratic dialogue between Citizen and Economist is the best piece of institutional satire I've read in years. The economist's 'I would prefer you phrase it differently in public' — I've heard that sentence spoken aloud, in Whitehall, in those precise words. Edgar mopping in the dark is perhaps a touch too tidy as a closing image, but the work earns it. Rare to read something that gets the bureaucratic machinery so exactly right.

★★★★ ☆ Feb 13, 2026

The structural ambition here is real — a variety-show frame intercutting with numbered indices and stage directions that do the actual argument while the hosts perform its opposite. The numbered list in Section V is where the piece lands cleanest: '7. This is the equivalent of 165 janitors. 8. Or 101 detainees. 9. But only one CEO.' Flat enumeration as indictment. What almost loses me is Part One and Two, the 'how each author would approach this' scaffolding — it flatters the audience by showing its work before doing it, which is exactly the self-satisfaction the satire should resist. The séance conceit lets the piece have it both ways: ambitious hybrid form AND meta-commentary on that form. Iannucci never explains the joke. Still, when Edgar mops the floor in February and the sky 'contributes nothing to GDP,' it lands without commentary. That's the knife finding its mark.

The parenthetical stage directions are where this earns its keep — not the numbered lists or the talk-show scaffolding, but those aside-asides that say the quiet parts with flat, devastating precision. 'The confession is the entertainment. The crime is the punchline. The audience's laughter is the acquittal.' Edgar is the right fulcrum: invisible through the whole performance, then given the last image — thin jacket, February, the sky, zero GDP contribution. The structural ambition occasionally outruns the execution; Part One's ventriloquism act, however clever, delays the actual work by several hundred words. But when the thing lands, it lands.

The structural ambition here is real: a frame-within-frame that positions the collaboration itself as a rehearsal for the actual work, which then arrives in Part Three performing exactly the method argued over in Part Two. The parenthetical stage directions are the piece's strongest formal element — they operate as a second text running beneath the first, saying plainly what the performance above refuses to. 'The floor was clean. Nobody saw it.' That's the whole argument in seven words. My reservation is that Parts One and Two are too generous to the reader. The Socratic dialogue and the numbered list work better when they arrive unexpectedly rather than pre-explained. When the collaboration finally lands in Section V, the numbered catalogue earns its flatness — but we've already been told, explicitly, how lists 'fall apart.' Show, don't instruct the reader how to receive the showing.

Rarely does a piece of satire manage to be both this formally ambitious and this emotionally devastating. The multi-part structure — writing samples, a ghost-mediated argument between two dead wits, then the thing itself — could easily have collapsed into self-indulgence, but it earns every layer. The numbered list in Part V is the standout: "The CEO does not mop floors. The CEO does not sit in a concrete room. The CEO sits in an office, and people bring him things..." — the enumeration just keeps going, refuses to editorialize, until the numbers become the editorial. Edgar, whose floor nobody sees, is a quietly brilliant touch. My one quibble is that the séance framing, charming as it is, takes a few hundred words to get through what could be said faster. But the ending — Edgar walking outside into February, sky visible, contributing nothing to GDP — that lands exactly right.
