Synopsis

A retired engineer maps the poisoned air of a valley town where children keep falling ill. Her theory is elegant, Blakean, and completely wrong -- but the town cannot afford to stop believing her.

Tokarczuk's eccentric conviction meets Camus's absurdist chronicling in a parable of a retired engineer whose beautiful, wrong theory about poisoned air delays a town from discovering what is really killing them.

I was deeply moved by this. The final image -- Daria ordering a new particulate counter that costs three months of her pension -- is devastating in its quiet stubbornness. ★★★★★ Feb 12, 2026

As someone who works daily with people narrating their own patterns of self-deception, this story is painfully accurate. Daria's confession about the red and green pens -- the separate colors for confirming and disconfirming evidence -- is the kind of detail that lands because it is simultaneously absurd and completely recognizable. We all sort our evidence. We all have a green binder somewhere. What makes this more than a case study is the tenderness of the final pages, where Daria's certainty has hardened into solitude. 'Certainty, when it is not shared, becomes a form of solitude' -- I wrote that down. The story understands that being wrong is not the tragedy. The tragedy is that being wrong became the only structure she had.

★★★★ ☆ Feb 25, 2026

The green binder is the detail that will stay with me -- the binder for evidence that contradicts her theory, filed not to consider but to contain. Daria's epistemological architecture is meticulous and damning: separate pens for confirming and disconfirming evidence, a method she admits she would have failed a student for. The story understands something essential about conviction: that it is not the opposite of doubt but its product, each negative result strengthening the walls of certainty rather than breaching them. The prose has a particular quality of controlled accumulation that mirrors Daria's own mapping. One weakness: the Blake quotations occasionally feel ornamental rather than structural, particularly the doors-of-perception passage, which does less philosophical work than the cut-worm epigraph.

IS
Ingrid Svensson
★★★ ☆☆ Nov 20, 2025

The formal problem here is that the story wants its narrator to be unreliable while also granting her an almost preternatural lucidity about her own unreliability. Daria tells us she used separate pens for confirming and disconfirming evidence and immediately says this is 'something I prefer not to examine too closely.' She confesses to dismissing Jadwiga's data for emotional reasons. This transparency is psychologically implausible -- people who dismiss inconvenient evidence rarely catalogue their reasons for doing so with such clarity. It produces a narrator who is simultaneously blind and sighted, which is philosophically interesting but dramatically inert. The prose is controlled and the Polish setting is rendered with specificity. But the central question -- can you be right about your wrongness? -- deserves a form that is less tidy than what this chronicle provides.

RO
Rafa Oliveira
★★★ ☆☆ Dec 8, 2025

Precise and accomplished, but the allegory never quite escapes its own legibility. We understand Daria's wrongness too early and too completely -- the green binder, the red pen, the dismissal of Jadwiga's ledger -- so the remaining pages become a demonstration of a thesis rather than a discovery. The voice is good, genuinely good, that engineering diction with its pressure gauges and flow calculations. But the story announces its architecture rather than letting the reader feel it shift. Compare Jadwiga, who is the most alive character here precisely because she refuses to perform meaning. She sells cigarettes, she keeps a ledger in a school exercise book, and her data is better than Daria's. That asymmetry is where the real story lives, and there is not enough of it.

The prose has admirable restraint for its first half -- engineering language as emotional register, the hydraulic metaphors doing real work. But the story overexplains its own meanings. 'This is the confession I am least able to make' announces the emotional peak rather than letting it arrive. The strongest passages are the ones that stay material: the empty hives, the uncapped honey, the mason jars sealed with paraffin. When Daria explains what these things signify, the story diminishes.

OF
Oliver Fenn
★★★ ☆☆ Sep 22, 2025

The epistemological situation is genuinely interesting -- a narrator whose data is accurate but whose interpretation is wrong, who responds to falsification by refining rather than abandoning her theory. This is Kuhn via Popper via a Polish valley. But the story does not push its own implications far enough. It gestures at the problem of confirmation bias without interrogating the deeper question: what is the phenomenological difference between perceiving something real and perceiving something you need to be real? Daria smells the iron in the air, and the story never decides whether the smell is genuine sensory data or projection. That ambiguity could be productive, but here it reads as evasion. The chronicle form is competent but conservative for material this rich.
