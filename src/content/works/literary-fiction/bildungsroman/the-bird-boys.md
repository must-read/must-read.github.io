The field was sixty hectares of wheat that year, laid out in plots the size of bedsheets, and our job was to keep the birds from eating it.

There were four of us. Tomás Robles, who was seventeen and had a shoulder that dislocated when he threw rocks too hard, which he always did. Alejandro Fuentes, who was sixteen and who everyone called Gordo though he wasn't fat — he'd been fat at nine and the name had outlasted the body. Beto Sifuentes, who was fifteen like me, who carried a slingshot made from a guava branch and a strip of inner tube, and who could hit a crackle at forty meters, though crackles were not the problem. The problem was the great-tailed grackles that descended in clouds of four hundred, the house finches that arrived at dawn, and the white-winged doves that came from the thorn forest along the river and sat in the wheat as if they had been invited.

We were hired by the American. We did not know his name for the first two weeks. He spoke Spanish the way a man speaks to a dog — short commands, many gestures, his face doing most of the work. He had a bald head that the sun had ruined and hands that were always moving, and he paid us four pesos a day each, which was more than the ejido was paying anyone for anything that season.

The field was on the experiment station south of Ciudad Obregon, on flat land between the road and an irrigation canal that the government had finished the year before and that still smelled of new concrete. The station had a building with a corrugated roof where the American kept his notebooks and a radio that received nothing but static and baseball scores from Arizona. Around the building were the plots — hundreds of them, each marked with a wooden stake and a number, each containing a different line of wheat that the American was trying to grow in soil that wheat had never wanted.

What we understood, at first, was this: we were to chase the birds. We did this from dawn until about ten, when the heat made the birds disappear into whatever shade they could find, and then again from three until dark. During the midday hours we sat under a mesquite tree near the canal and ate tortillas and talked about women we had not touched and a future we could not have described if pressed. Gordo wanted to be a bus driver. Tomás wanted to be a mechanic. Beto wanted to go to California, where he had heard the streets were made of something smooth and grey that never turned to mud. I did not say what I wanted because I did not want anything — not yet — and in that group the boy without an ambition was the boy who listened, and I listened well.

The American sat on an overturned bucket in the field with a pair of tweezers and did something to the wheat that we watched from the shade with the same detached curiosity you give a man talking to himself on a bus. He wore the same clothes every day — khaki trousers, a shirt the color of dust, boots that had been expensive once and were now held together by the memory of stitching. He ate lunch out of a paper bag: a tortilla folded around beans, sometimes a boiled egg, always an orange that he peeled in one continuous spiral, a skill so practiced and precise that Beto once said, with genuine admiration, "That man could peel a snake."

Harold had a notebook in which he wrote things we could not read, numbers and letters in combinations that meant nothing to us but clearly meant something tremendous to him. He would crouch over a wheat plot with his tweezers and his paper bags, and then he would write in the notebook, and then he would move to the next plot and do it again, and the patience of it was astonishing — not the patience of a man waiting for something, but the patience of a man who had decided that this was the only work worth doing and everything else was noise. I recognized that patience, though I didn't know I recognized it. My grandmother had it when she rolled tortillas — the same motion a thousand times, each one slightly different in a way only she could feel, each one arriving at the same result through a path that was never exactly repeated.

"He's marrying them," Tomás said one afternoon. He'd been watching longer than the rest of us.

"Marrying what."

"The wheat. One kind to another. Like you cross a good bull with a cow to get a better calf. He's crossing the wheat."

We went over and stood behind the American and watched. He had a wheat head clamped between his thumb and finger, and with the tweezers he was removing something from inside it — tiny pale parts that he dropped into his palm. Then he took a paper bag from a stake next to a different plot, shook something out of it onto the wheat head he'd just stripped, and covered the head with a fresh bag. The whole operation took about ninety seconds, and his hands shook slightly the entire time, and he did not look up.

"What's in the bag?" Gordo asked.

"Pollen," Tomás said, though none of us knew the word in Spanish. What he actually said was "the dust from the other one," and we understood.

The American's name was Harold, and after three weeks he decided to teach us the crosses. I think this was because there were four thousand wheat heads to cross that season and two hands were not enough. He lined us up at the edge of a plot and showed us what he'd been doing: removing the stamens — the male parts — from one head, then dusting it with pollen from a different variety. He called it emasculation, a word none of us knew. Tomás translated it as "taking the man out of the flower," and Gordo laughed until Harold looked at him, and then Gordo stopped laughing because Harold's face had a quality that discouraged amusement. Not anger. Something more focused than anger.

He gave us each a pair of tweezers and a row. The first day I ruined forty wheat heads. The stamens were the size of rice grains, pale yellow, and they hid between the glumes — the little shells that made up each spikelet — like children behind a door. My fingers were too large, too clumsy, too used to slingshots and fence wire and the coarse work of a fifteen-year-old boy whose hands had never been asked to be careful. I tore glumes off entirely. I crushed the pistils I was supposed to leave intact. I cross-pollinated a head that had already been crossed, which Harold discovered two days later and which produced, I suspect, a plant that confused even the wheat.

But something happened during that first week. It happened in my hands before it happened in my mind, and I did not notice it the way you do not notice the moment when you stop thinking about how to walk and simply walk. My fingers learned the space between the glumes. They learned the angle at which a stamen releases cleanly, without tearing the tissue beneath it. They learned the resistance of a ripe anther versus one that was a day from opening, and the difference was not something I could have described to you then or now — it was a pressure, a temperature, a readiness that my fingertips read the way a blind man reads a page.

By the second week, Harold stood behind me and watched me work for twenty minutes without saying anything. I did eleven heads in that time. He'd been averaging eight.

He said something in English to himself that I didn't catch. Then he went to watch Tomás, who was working steadily and well, at about Harold's own pace. Gordo was slower but careful. Beto was erratic — brilliant one hour, sloppy the next, as if his hands were tuned to a frequency that drifted.

After a month, Harold stopped doing crosses himself. He walked the rows and checked our work and wrote numbers in his notebooks. When he found a head I'd done, he would hold it up to the light and look at it from several angles, the way a jeweler examines a stone, and then he would move on without comment. I understood from the absence of complaint that the work was acceptable. Once, near the end of that first season, I saw him hold up a head I'd crossed and show it to a man who had come from Mexico City in a government car — a man in a suit who stood at the edge of the field as if the dirt might damage him. Harold pointed at the head and then at me, and the man from Mexico City looked at me the way you look at a tool that is performing better than expected. I went back to my row. I did not need to hear what Harold said about me. The wheat head was the argument, and the argument was sufficient.

There was a hierarchy among us that nobody discussed. Tomás was the oldest and therefore the one Harold addressed when he wanted something done. Gordo was the one who could fix things — the broken wheelbarrow, the jammed padlock on the supply shed, the station manager's radio when it stopped receiving even the baseball scores. Beto was the one who made us laugh, who could imitate Harold's walk so perfectly that once Harold himself saw it and stood very still for a moment before turning away with something on his face that might have been amusement. And I was the one whose hands did the thing that nobody talked about, because talking about it would have meant naming it, and we did not have a name for what it was when a boy who could not read could do something better than the man with the degree.

---

The mornings were the good hours. We arrived before dawn, when the Yaqui Valley was still cool enough that your breath made a small ghost in front of your face and the wheat was wet with dew that caught the first light and turned each plot into something that did not belong on a government experiment station — something that belonged in a church, or in one of those old paintings where the light comes from nowhere and everywhere at once. The birds arrived with the light. We chased them. Then we worked the crosses until the heat drove us to the mesquite tree, and then we went back to the crosses, and then the sun went down and we walked back to the road where the bus sometimes came and sometimes didn't.

In November of that first year the wheat was harvested and the field was empty and we had nothing to do. Harold went to Mexico City and then to the United States and was gone for three months. He left us a message through the station manager that we should come back in February when the new planting started, and he left us each an envelope with twenty pesos in it, which was more money than I had ever held at one time.

Tomás used his to buy a pair of boots. Gordo gave his to his mother. Beto spent his in two days on things I don't remember and probably neither does he. I kept mine in a coffee can under my bed and looked at it sometimes. During those three months I worked odd jobs — moving irrigation pipe for a farmer near Cocorít, stacking crates at a warehouse in Obregon — and my hands felt wrong. Not clumsy, exactly, but unused in some essential way, the way a singer's throat must feel when she hasn't sung. The pipe work was heavy and simple, and my fingers, which had spent six months learning the architecture of a wheat spikelet, had nothing delicate to do, and the absence of that delicacy was a kind of ache that I would not have been able to explain to anyone who asked.

My mother noticed something. She didn't say what she noticed. She put food in front of me and watched me eat and sometimes asked if I was going back to the American's field, and the way she asked suggested that she already knew the answer and was testing whether I knew it too. I was going back. The question was never whether. The question was how I would fill the months between.

In February we came back and the field had been planted again, a new set of varieties with new stakes and new numbers, and Harold was there on his overturned bucket as if he had never left. He'd gotten thinner. The sun damage on his head had peeled and re-formed into a kind of permanent redness, a badge of some private endurance. He handed us our tweezers. The birds were already in the field.

That second season is when it happened — the thing with the scissors. I'm going to try to describe it, though the description will be wrong in the way all descriptions of physical knowledge are wrong, because the language for it doesn't exist.

I was working a row of a line Harold called Kenya Red, which had large heads with tough glumes. The tweezers kept slipping on the glumes, and the work was slow, and I was losing heads to torn tissue. My hands were frustrated in a way that was separate from my mind — my mind was not thinking about anything in particular, was drifting somewhere between the sound of doves in the thorn forest and a girl named Esperanza Villalobos who worked at the panadería on Calle Sufragio and who had smiled at me once in a way that could have meant anything — but my hands were angry. They wanted the glumes open and they could not get them open, and at some point I reached into the pocket of my trousers and found the small scissors my mother used for thread, which I had borrowed six months earlier and forgotten to return.

I tried cutting the tip of a glume. Just the top third. And the glume opened like a door.

The stamen was right there. I removed it with the tweezers in one motion, clean, no tearing. The whole operation took maybe four seconds. With the tweezers alone, it took fifteen.

I did the next head the same way. Then the next. By the end of that row I had done thirty-seven heads in the time it normally took me to do twelve. Harold was in the next field checking something and didn't see me. I didn't tell him. I'm not sure why. It wasn't secrecy exactly — it was more that the discovery felt private, as if the scissors and I had worked something out between us and it was not yet ready for other people's attention.

I used the scissors for three days before Harold noticed. During those three days I crossed more heads than I had in any comparable period, and the quality was better — cleaner cuts, less tissue damage, fewer lost pistils. The numbers would show it later in Harold's notebooks, though I didn't know about the numbers then. What I knew was the feeling: a rightness in the motion, a fit between tool and task and hand that I had never experienced with anything else. My mother's sewing scissors had found their real purpose, and their real purpose had nothing to do with thread.

On the third day Harold came up behind me. I heard his boots in the dirt, the particular shuffle he had because of a knee that bothered him, and then the shuffle stopped and I knew he was watching. I could feel his looking the way you feel sun on the back of your neck. I didn't turn around. I finished the head I was on, and the next one, and the next, working at the pace my hands had settled into, which was fast enough that the individual motions blurred into a single continuous gesture — cut, open, extract, bag. When I finally turned around, his face had an expression I hadn't seen before. Something between recognition and loss, as if he were watching a thing he'd built be improved by someone who didn't know the blueprint.

He held his hand out. I gave him the scissors. He turned them over, opened and closed them once, looked at the wheat head I'd just done, and then he took the scissors to the end of the row and tried the technique himself.

His hands were too big. The fingers that had been adequate for tweezers — competent, steady, educated — were wrong for the scissors in a way that had nothing to do with skill and everything to do with proportion. He tore two heads before he got one right, and when he got one right it took him twice as long as it took me. He stood there holding the scissors in the afternoon sun, looking at them as if they'd spoken to him in a language he could almost but not quite remember.

He gave the scissors back to me and said, "Enséñales." Teach them.

I taught Tomás that afternoon. He picked it up in two hours, though his dislocating shoulder made the work hurt in a way mine didn't. I taught Gordo the next day, and he was adequate but would never be fast. Beto took to it like a reflex — within a week his hands were nearly as fast as mine, though he had days where he couldn't seem to find the rhythm and would revert to the tweezers with a kind of defeated patience.

Harold ordered twelve pairs of scissors from a surgical supply company in Tucson. When they arrived six weeks later, they were better than my mother's thread scissors — sharper, more precise, with a spring in the hinge that returned them to the open position. He gave me the first pair. I still have them somewhere.

---

Gordo left at the end of the second season. His brother had gone to Tijuana to work in a factory that made radio cabinets, and there was a second position available, and their mother needed the money more than twenty pesos every three months. These were facts that admitted no argument. A factory job in Tijuana paid eight times what Harold paid, and Gordo's mother had four other children and a house with a roof that leaked in three places that Gordo had patched with flattened tin cans, and the patches were failing.

He shook our hands on the last day of harvest. He held mine a little longer, or maybe that's something I've added — memory does that, adjusts the duration of a handshake to match what the handshake should have meant. He said, "Don't let the birds eat it all," and then he walked up the road toward the bus stop with a bag over his shoulder that contained everything he considered worth carrying, which was not much. I watched him until the road bent and the mesquite hid him and there was nothing to watch.

I didn't see him for fourteen years, until we met by accident at a bus station in Hermosillo and he didn't recognize me and I had to explain who I was. He was heavier again — the name had caught up to the body — and he worked in a tile factory now, not the radio cabinet place, and he had five children and a wife I'd never met. When I told him I was still at the station, still in the wheat, he looked at me with an expression that was part warmth and part something else — a kind of bewilderment that I had stayed and he had gone and neither of us had turned into the man the other expected.

Tomás left the following spring. Esperanza Villalobos, the girl from the panadería, was pregnant, and the baby would have Tomás's last name, not mine, which was a fact I absorbed the way I absorbed everything that year — through the body, not the mind. He married her in April and took a job driving a delivery truck for a bottling company in Los Mochis. At the wedding he was happy, genuinely and completely happy, and I danced badly and drank too much mezcal and woke up under a table with the scissors in my pocket and a headache that lasted two days, and the next Monday I was back in the field and there were three hundred heads to cross and only two of us.

Beto lasted until October. He never said he was leaving. He just stopped coming. Three mornings in a row his spot in the row was empty, and on the first morning I thought he was sick, and on the second I thought he was in trouble, and on the third I went to his house and his mother told me he'd taken a bus north with a cousin. She said it the way you say the weather has changed — without drama, as something that was going to happen and now had happened. She offered me coffee. I sat at her table and drank it, and she told me Beto had been talking about going north for a year, that the cousin had a connection to a ranch in Arizona that needed hands for the cotton, and that Beto had left his slingshot — the one made from guava wood and inner tube — on the kitchen table, and she didn't know if that meant he wasn't coming back or if he'd just forgotten it.

I walked back to the station along the canal. The water was brown and slow and a white egret was standing in the shallows with the focused patience of something that had been standing there since before the canal was built, before the concrete and the government and the American and his wheat. The egret's stillness had a quality I envied without understanding — a completeness, as though the bird's entire being was gathered into the single act of waiting for a fish, and there was nothing left over, no part of the egret wishing it were somewhere else. I watched until it caught a fish and flew away, and then I went back to work.

---

I was seventeen when I became the only one. The field was the same. The wheat was new — different lines, different numbers on the stakes — but the work was the same, and the mornings were the same, and the birds came the same way they always came, as if they had been keeping a calendar of their own and knew exactly when the grain would ripen. The only difference was the silence where the others had been. Not an empty silence. A filled one. The rows Tomás and Gordo and Beto had worked were now my rows, and I worked them, and the wheat did not know the difference.

Harold hired new boys that season — two brothers from a village near Navojoa, quiet and competent, and a boy named Francisco who had a gift that reminded me of my own, though his was less certain, more contingent on mood and weather. The brothers learned the work the way good students learn — steadily, without flair, reaching competence by accumulation. Francisco learned the way I had learned, which is to say his hands learned before his mind did, and sometimes he would stop in the middle of a row and look at a wheat head he'd just crossed with an expression that I recognized: the bewilderment of realizing your body has done something you didn't tell it to do.

I taught them the scissors. I taught them the angle and the pressure and the four-second rhythm, and I watched their hands learn the way mine had learned. Teaching is a strange thing. You have to slow yourself down, to undo the fluency that makes the work invisible, to become conscious again of each small motion that has long since merged into a single gesture. And in that slowing-down I discovered things about my own technique that I hadn't known — compensations my left thumb made when the glumes were tight, a rotation of the wrist that I'd developed without deciding to, a way of holding the scissors at a seven-degree angle that kept the blade from bruising the rachilla. I found these things the way you find objects in a room you've lived in for years: they were always there, but it took a visitor's eyes to make me see them.

I felt something that wasn't pride exactly — it was closer to recognition, the way you recognize your own handwriting in a letter you don't remember sending.

Harold began calling me his "right hand," which was a joke that only worked in English and which he explained to me once, patiently, using both hands to demonstrate, and I pretended to understand it because his face when he told jokes had a vulnerability to it that I did not want to disturb. He was a man who lived for the wheat and through the wheat, and outside the wheat he seemed slightly lost, the way a priest seems lost at a party — present but not quite participant. I watched him write letters at his desk some evenings, letters he sealed and addressed to places in the United States, and his handwriting was terrible, cramped and urgent, as if the words were trying to escape the page. Whatever he was telling those people, whatever case he was making for more money, more seed, more time, I understood that I was part of the evidence. My hands were part of the argument. And I did not mind being evidence for something I couldn't read, because the thing the evidence pointed to — better wheat, more grain, the vague enormous purpose that Harold carried like a weight on his burned shoulders — was real in the field even if it was invisible everywhere else.

He put my name in his field notebooks. R. VEGA, next to the row numbers, next to the yields, next to the data that would eventually go into a report that would go to a foundation in the United States that would decide whether to keep funding the program, and I understood — vaguely, in the way you understand the current of a river you're standing in — that my hands were part of a system so large I couldn't see its edges.

There was a morning — I must have been eighteen by then, though I wasn't sure of my exact birthday, my mother had always been imprecise about it, saying only that I was born during the rains — there was a morning when I was in the field alone. It was early. The dew was heavy and the wheat was at the stage Harold called "anthesis," when the flowers open and the pollen is live and the entire field has a smell that is difficult to describe because it is the smell of something becoming what it will be. The sun was low and orange behind the Sierra Madre Occidental and the light came across the field at an angle that lit every dewdrop on every wheat head individually, so that the plots glittered as if they'd been wired with thousands of tiny lights, and I was standing in the middle of it with my scissors and my hands were working and I saw myself.

I don't mean mystically. I don't mean I left my body. I mean I became, for a moment, aware of myself the way you become aware of a sound you've been hearing for so long it has become silence, and the awareness was startling not because the thing I saw was remarkable but because it wasn't. I was a boy in a field, crossing wheat. That was what I did. That was what I would do the next morning and the morning after and the morning after that, and the shape of those mornings was the shape of my life, and I had not chosen it in any way I could identify. My hands had chosen it. Or the wheat had chosen it. Or nothing had chosen anything and this was simply where I was, the way the egret had been in the canal — not because it had decided to be there but because that was where the fish were.

The moment passed. I finished the head I was on, moved to the next, and kept working.

Years later — many years, after Harold had won a prize I learned about from a newspaper someone left on a bench at the bus station, after the program had expanded to fields I'd never seen, after I had become what Harold called a master technician, which was a title that meant my pay was equal to a college graduate's, though I had never been inside a school — years later, a woman came to the station with a camera and a notebook to write about the program. She was from Mexico City and she wore shoes that were wrong for the field and she asked me questions in a Spanish that was technically perfect and emotionally foreign, the Spanish of someone who had learned the language from books rather than from mothers.

She asked me how I had learned to do the crosses. I told her Harold had taught me. She asked me about the scissors, and I showed her, and she took a photograph of my hands holding the scissors near a wheat head, and she wrote something down. She asked if she could photograph my hands closer, just the hands, and I held them out and she adjusted the camera angle and I looked at my own hands in the light and they were the hands of an old man — thickened, scarred across the knuckles where the sun had done its work over decades, the nails short and clean because the work required it. They were not the hands of the boy who had chased birds. They were not the hands that had first held his mother's sewing scissors. But they were the same hands, the way a river at its mouth is the same river that started as a spring in the mountains — wider, slower, carrying more, but fed by the same source.

"You invented a technique," she said.

"My hands did something," I said.

"That's the same thing."

It wasn't the same thing, but I didn't know how to explain why, and the explanation would have required words I didn't have in either language. Inventing implies intention. What my hands did was closer to what a vine does when it finds a wall — it doesn't decide to climb, it encounters a surface and responds, and the response looks like a decision from the outside but from the inside it is nothing but the organism's nature meeting the world's shape.

She had already moved on to the next question. She asked me about the other boys, the original ones, and I told her about Tomás and Gordo and Beto, and she wrote their names down and I could see that in her article they would be a paragraph — the bird boys who had been there at the beginning — and that the paragraph would not contain anything true about them. It would contain facts, which is different. It would say that four local boys were hired in 1946, that they learned the crossing technique, that one of them stayed. It would not say that Gordo could fix anything with wire and patience, or that Tomás had a shoulder that came apart like a bad joint in a fence post, or that Beto's slingshot was a work of craftsmanship that deserved a place in a museum of things nobody thought to preserve.

She asked me why I'd stayed.

I looked at the field. It was February. New planting. The wheat was just coming up, pale green against the dark soil, and in a few weeks it would need crossing and my hands would do what they did, and in the early mornings the dew would catch the light, and the birds would come, and the new boys would chase them, and I would stand in the row with my scissors and feel the glumes give way under the blades.

"The work is here," I said.

She waited for more, the way journalists do, leaving a silence they expect you to fill with something quotable. I didn't fill it. The silence was the answer, or the closest thing to an answer that I had. The work was here. I was here. The correspondence between those two facts was the entire architecture of my life, and it was not something I had built. It was something that had grown, the way the wheat grew — not because anyone decided it should, but because the conditions were right and the seed was in the ground and the water came when it came.

She thanked me and walked back to the station building in her wrong shoes, and I watched her go, and then a cloud of grackles came in from the east, maybe two hundred of them, and they settled in the far plots where the Kenyan lines were, and I didn't chase them. I stood there. The birds ate what they ate. The wheat that survived was the wheat that would survive, and the wheat that didn't wasn't, and my hands were at my sides, and the morning was ordinary, and the scissors were in my pocket next to a folded receipt for solder that I'd been carrying for three weeks and had no reason to keep.

A boy from the new crew — I didn't know his name yet — came running from behind the station building, shouting at the grackles, waving his hat. The birds lifted in a single motion, a black cloud that turned once over the field and then broke apart and scattered into the thorn forest. The boy stood in the empty row, breathing hard, looking satisfied.

I went back to work.
