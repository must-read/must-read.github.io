Iden woke before the bell because the river was high.

He could hear it from the house, which should not have been possible. The Aller ran forty yards from his door at its usual level, down a clay bank that in summer cracked into plates and in winter set hard as the floor of an oven. But the rain had been three days steady and the river had come up, and now at the edge of sleep he could hear it moving — not the rush of flood but the steady, patient sound of water occupying ground it had a right to. The sound of a thing returning to a place it knew.

He lay still and listened. Beside him, Margery slept with her mouth slightly open. The children were above in the loft — Hild and the two boys, Thomas and Wat, their breathing a single blurred rhythm through the boards. The house smelled of old smoke and the sheepskins they slept under and the particular smell of a thatched roof that has been wet for three days: a sweet, vegetal rot, not unpleasant, that Iden associated with the onset of real cold.

He rose, pulled his tunic over his head, and stood at the door. The yard was mud. The sky was the color of undyed linen. Across the lane, Godric's cow was already tied at the post, which meant Godric was already milking, which meant the day had shape and could not be taken back.

Iden was the reeve. He had been the reeve for four years and would be the reeve until the lord's steward replaced him or until he died, whichever came first. The position had been offered to him in the way a man is offered a plate of food he cannot refuse — with courtesy and an expectation that bordered on command. The lord's steward had said: *You are respected. You know the customs. The village trusts you.* What the steward had meant was: *You are in debt to us for the oxen and we need someone who can count and who will not steal more than is customary.*

Iden could count. He kept the tallies — the hides, the virgates, the strips in the common fields, the days of labor owed by each tenant to the lord's demesne. He knew that Walter Brewer owed two days' work each week and gave them grudgingly, coming late and leaving with the bell, and that Alys Weaver owed only one because her husband had bought the second with twelve pence and a goose at Michaelmas three years before the husband died, and that the purchase still held, though there were men who said it shouldn't, because the husband was dead and a widow's hold on her husband's bargains was a thing that could be questioned. Iden had not questioned it. This was not kindness. It was the calculation that Alys's single day of work, given willingly and completed well, was worth more than two days extracted from a woman who would spit in the grain.

He walked to the bridge.

The bridge was everything. It had been everything for as long as anyone in the town could remember, and it would go on being everything until the river changed its course or the bridge fell, and one of these was more likely than the other, and everyone in the town knew which one, and nobody said it.

It was a stone bridge — three arches, limestone from the quarry at Breckham, set on piers that went down to the riverbed. The piers had been sunk in Iden's grandfather's time, or perhaps his great-grandfather's; the records were unclear because the records had been kept by the priory and the priory's records reflected the priory's interests, which were not the same as the town's interests, though they overlapped in the way that the interests of a man and his horse overlap — one carried the other and both needed feeding, but only one decided the direction.

The toll was the thing. Every cart that crossed paid a penny. Every drove of cattle paid a halfpenny per head. Every foot traveler going to market paid a farthing. The toll belonged to the lord, and the lord's portion was collected by Iden, and Iden kept the count and sent the money by the steward's man each quarter day, and the difference between what was collected and what was sent was understood by everyone and discussed by no one. This difference was how Iden fed his children. This difference was the compensation for being the man your neighbors hated on the days their labor was due and their backs ached and the rain had ruined the plowing.

The bridge's eastern pier was cracked. Iden had first noticed it in the summer, when the water was low and you could walk out along the dry stones to the base and put your hand on it. A vertical crack, the width of a finger, running from the waterline up through three courses of dressed stone. He had put his hand in it. The stone inside was wet and soft and faintly warm, which meant the river was already inside the pier, doing its work.

He had told no one. He had considered telling the steward, because the steward was responsible for the bridge's maintenance, and the steward would tell the lord, and the lord would commission repairs, and the repairs would cost money, and the money would come from somewhere, and the somewhere would be the town, because the somewhere was always the town. A levy. An extra day of labor. A tithe on the market stalls. The cost would be distributed, and the distribution would be Iden's job, and the distribution would make people angry, and the anger would land on him, because the anger always landed on him, because that was what a reeve was for — to stand between the lord's demands and the village's capacity and absorb the difference in his own body.

So he had told no one. The bridge would crack a little more this winter. He would watch it. In the spring, when the water dropped, he would look again. Perhaps it would stabilize. Limestone did that sometimes — cracked and then settled, found a new shape, held. Perhaps.

Or perhaps in two years, or five, or ten, the eastern pier would give way during a spring flood and the bridge would sag and then fail, and the carts would stop coming, and the toll would stop flowing, and the town would shrink to what it had been before the bridge — a scatter of farms around a ford that was impassable four months of the year. And Iden would no longer be the reeve, because there would be nothing left to reeve over. And his children would scatter to other towns or stay and work the land or starve, because those were the three things that happened to the children of a town without a bridge.

He stood on the western bank and looked at the crack. The water was high enough now that he couldn't see it. The water covered the evidence. The bridge looked fine.

Market day was Wednesday.

By the time the bell rang for Prime, the stalls were going up in the square — trestle boards on saw-horses, canvas stretched between poles, the butchers at the north end where the drainage ran and the cloth sellers at the south end where the wind was softer and wouldn't snap the bolts. The baker had his oven going before dawn, and the smell of bread — wheat bread, not the maslin or the black rye that most of the town ate — drifted across the square like a rumor of a better life.

Iden collected the market tolls. A penny per stall. Tuppence for the man from Breckham who brought cheese in a cart with iron-rimmed wheels that cut the ground. A halfpenny for Old Fritha, who sold eggs and could be relied on to argue about it.

"A halfpenny," she said. "For six eggs and a bad hip."

"The toll is for the stall, Fritha. Not the eggs."

"I haven't got a stall. I've got a basket."

"Then put the basket on the ground and sell from the ground and you owe me nothing. But if you stand in the market square, you pay the market toll."

She paid. She always paid. The argument was ritual, and the ritual was important, because the ritual allowed Fritha to feel that she had resisted and allowed Iden to feel that he had been patient. Both of these feelings were necessary for the market to function. The market was not a place where goods were exchanged for money. The market was a place where a set of relationships were maintained through the exchange of goods for money. The difference was everything.

Iden's daughter Hild worked the stall for Alys Weaver. Hild was fourteen and had her mother's hands — quick, certain, capable of the fine work that distinguished good cloth from middling. She could card and spin and was learning to weave on Alys's loom, a heavy oak frame that took up half the woman's house and produced a narrow, tight-woven wool that merchants from Stourbridge would buy at a good price. Alys paid Hild nothing, because Hild was learning, and the learning was the payment. This arrangement was beneficial to both parties. This was what everyone said, and what everyone said was true, in the way that a thing can be true and also be a way of not saying the other thing, which was that Alys needed the labor and could not afford to pay for it and Hild needed the skill and could not afford to wait for it and the arrangement bound them to each other in a way that looked like generosity and felt like necessity and was, in fact, both.

Iden watched his daughter work. She handled the cloth the way her mother handled the children — firmly, without sentiment, with an economy of motion that came from having more to do than time to do it in. She haggled with a chapman's man over a bolt of kersey and got tuppence more than Alys would have settled for. She was good at this. She was better than Iden had been at her age, which meant she was better than Iden was now, because at fourteen the quickness of the mind has not yet been weighted down by the knowledge of consequence.

"You'll ruin your eyes at that loom," he told her, not because he believed it but because it was the kind of thing a father said, and saying it allowed him to stand at her stall for a moment longer without the standing being strange.

"Alys sees fine," Hild said.

"Alys is forty and holds the thread an arm's length from her face."

"Alys is forty and runs a business and owns her house and doesn't owe anyone a day's work. I'll take her eyes."

Iden said nothing to this. There was nothing to say. His daughter had described a kind of freedom, and the freedom was real, and it had been purchased with a husband's death and a widow's shrewdness and twelve pence and a goose, and it was as fragile as a crack in a limestone pier, and Hild did not know that yet, and Iden would not tell her, because a father who tells his daughter that her ambitions are fragile is a father who has already failed her.

---

In the afternoon, the steward's man came.

His name was Pell. He rode a brown mare and wore a leather jerkin over a wool tunic and had the particular expression of a man who carries authority that is not his own — alert, slightly defensive, ready to be offended on behalf of someone who was not present. He was perhaps twenty-five. He had been the steward's man for two years and in that time had learned to read a tally stick and count to a hundred in French and hold his face very still when people were angry at him.

"The lord requires a survey of the bridge," Pell said.

Iden felt his stomach drop, though his face showed nothing. "A survey."

"The piers. The arches. The road surface. The toll has been down this quarter and the lord wishes to know the condition."

"The toll is down because the weather has been foul and the roads are mud. Fewer carts."

"The lord wishes to know the condition of the bridge."

Iden nodded. The lord did not wish to know the condition of the bridge. The lord wished to establish, through the mechanism of a survey, that the bridge was in good repair, so that if the toll continued to decline, the fault could be laid not on the bridge but on the reeve who collected the toll. This was how the lord operated. Not through direct accusation but through the creation of a record that, if needed later, would tell the story the lord required.

"When?"

"He sends a mason from Breckham on Friday."

"Friday. Two days."

"Friday."

Pell turned his mare and rode back through the market square, and the people who were selling their eggs and their cloth and their pots looked up at him and then looked at Iden, and in that shared looking was the whole architecture of the thing — the man on the horse and the man on the ground and the people who watched them both and drew their own conclusions about who served whom and at what cost.

Iden went home and sat on the bench outside his door and thought about the crack in the eastern pier. Two days. He could do nothing about the crack in two days. He could not repair it, because he was not a mason and did not have the stone or the tools or the knowledge. He could not hide it, because the river was high and the mason would not be able to see it anyway, and if the mason could not see it, the mason's report would say the bridge was sound, and the report would be the truth as far as the lord was concerned, because the lord did not live here and did not look at the bridge and did not put his hand in the crack and feel the warm wet stone inside.

Margery came out and sat beside him. She was thirty-two and looked forty, because that was what five pregnancies and three surviving children and the work of a reeve's wife did to a woman's face. She had been handsome once — sharp-featured, dark-eyed, with a mouth that could express amusement and contempt simultaneously, a skill she had deployed frequently in the years before the children came and the reeveship took what was left of her patience.

"Pell was here."

"The lord wants a survey."

"Of what?"

"The bridge."

Margery said nothing for a moment. Then: "Will the mason see the crack?"

Iden looked at her. He had not told her about the crack. He had told no one about the crack. But of course she knew. She had lived with him for fifteen years and she watched his face when he came back from the bridge and she had seen the particular quality of quiet that settled over him in the evenings after he had been to look at the pier, a quiet that was different from his usual quiet, which was the quiet of tiredness, and different from his angry quiet, which came after he had collected a tithe from someone who could not afford it. This quiet was the quiet of a man carrying a thing he could not put down.

"The water's high," he said. "He won't see it."

"And when the water drops?"

"Then it's spring. And a spring survey is a different survey."

"Iden."

"If the mason reports a crack, the lord orders repairs. If the lord orders repairs, the cost falls on us. On the town. A levy. Extra labor days. Right now, in the middle of plowing season."

"And if the bridge falls?"

"The bridge isn't going to fall."

"You don't know that."

He didn't. He knew only that the bridge had stood for seventy years and that limestone cracked and sometimes held and that the cost of knowing, of truly knowing, was a cost the town could not bear right now, in this season, with the harvest thin and the rains coming and the labor already stretched beyond what the fields required. He knew that the bridge would most likely stand another winter and another spring and that the crack would widen or it wouldn't and that he would check it again when the water dropped and that this cycle of checking and not-telling and hoping was the best he could do, and that the best he could do was not good enough, and that this was true of most things.

"I'll handle it," he said, which was not a lie and not a promise and not anything, really, except a sound that meant the conversation was over.

---

Thursday, Iden collected the labor.

Walter Brewer owed his two days. Iden found him in his house, a timber-and-daub box near the east gate that smelled of malt and was warmer than any other building in the town because the brewing kept the interior heated. Walter was a large man who had once been strong and was now merely heavy. He sat on a stool and did not rise when Iden came in.

"Tomorrow's your day," Iden said.

"I know what day it is."

"The demesne field wants plowing. South strip."

"In this rain."

"In this rain."

Walter looked at his hands. They were thick and scarred and permanently creased with the dark stain of grain. "I'll send Leofric."

Leofric was a cottager — a man who held no land of his own but worked for others on their strips and lived in a two-room house that belonged to Walter by custom and to the lord by law. Leofric did Walter's labor days when Walter's back or Walter's temper or Walter's sense of his own dignity made the work intolerable. Walter paid Leofric nothing for this, because Leofric lived in Walter's house, and the house was the payment.

"The steward wants the labor done by the tenant," Iden said.

"The steward wants the field plowed. He doesn't care whose hands hold the plow."

This was true. Iden knew it was true because the steward had never once asked whether the man who showed up to plow was the man whose name was on the roll. The system required the labor, not the laborer. The name on the roll was a fiction maintained for the purpose of accounting, which was itself a fiction maintained for the purpose of control. What mattered was that the field got plowed and the toll got paid and the rolls showed names and the names showed order.

"Send Leofric, then."

"I'll send him."

Iden left. Outside, the rain had thinned to a cold mist that clung to the skin and soaked through wool in a way that rain proper did not — insidiously, without drama, so that you were wet before you knew you were wet. He walked to the next house, where Agnes Thatcher owed a day of work and would give it herself because Agnes had no one to send and nothing to pay anyone with and would go to the demesne field and plow in the rain and come back at evening with her hands raw and her back seized and her children unfed because there was no one to feed them while she was gone.

Agnes was at her door, already dressed for the field. She wore a man's tunic — her dead husband's — belted at the waist, and her hair was tied back with a strip of linen. She was thin in the way that poor people are thin, not from illness but from the steady subtraction of calories over years, a thinness that looked like a body argument — how little can you take from me and leave me standing?

"I'm ready," she said.

"Your boy could go."

"He's seven."

"He could help."

"He'll stay and mind the baby."

A seven-year-old minding a baby. A mother going to plow in the rain. A reeve standing at the door collecting a day's work from a woman who had nothing to give but a day's work. This was the system. This was what the system produced: not wealth, not order, not even stability, but this — the steady extraction of labor from the bodies of people who had no choice but to give it, administered by a man who had no choice but to collect it, on behalf of a lord who had no involvement in any of it except the receiving.

Iden walked away. He did not help Agnes with her cloak. He did not carry her tools. He did not do these things because doing them would acknowledge that what he was doing to her was a cruelty, and acknowledging the cruelty would make it impossible to continue, and continuing was the only option available, because the system did not have an outside.

---

The mason came on Friday.

His name was Ralf and he was from Breckham and he had built chimneys and repaired walls and cut stone for twenty years and he had the hands to prove it — broad, scarred, with fingers that bent at angles that fingers were not designed to bend at. He came on foot, carrying his tools in a leather roll, and he looked at the bridge the way a physician looks at a patient — professionally, without emotion, already composing the report in his head.

Iden walked with him. They stood on the western bank and looked at the three arches and the roadway above them and the parapets, which were mossy and crumbling in places, the mortar between the stones pocked and receding.

"The parapets need repointing," Ralf said.

"They've needed repointing for five years."

"Then they've needed repointing for five years."

The mason walked onto the bridge and knelt and looked at the road surface, which was packed earth over stone, rutted by cart wheels and softened by rain. He tapped the parapet with the handle of his chisel and listened to the sound the stone made. He leaned over the eastern edge and looked down at the pier.

The water was high. It covered the base of the pier entirely and lapped at the first course of dressed stone above the waterline. The crack — Iden's crack, the crack he had found in the summer and put his hand in and kept to himself — was underwater. Invisible.

"The piers look sound from here," Ralf said.

"They're good stone."

"Breckham limestone."

"Same as the priory."

Ralf nodded. He ran his hand along the parapet and picked at the mortar with his thumbnail. "This is the problem. Not the piers. The mortar. Twenty years of rain and frost and you lose the bond. The stones are still good but they're not held. Another ten years and you'll have parapet stones in the river."

"Can you repoint it?"

"I can. Two weeks' work. Materials — lime, sand, my time. Call it fifteen shillings."

Fifteen shillings. Iden calculated. The quarterly toll brought in roughly forty shillings, of which the lord took thirty-two and Iden kept the rest by custom. Fifteen shillings was almost half the lord's portion for a quarter. The lord would pay it or he wouldn't. If he paid it, the money would come from the town — an extra assessment, another levy, more labor. If he didn't, the parapets would continue to crumble, and the road surface would erode, and in five or ten years the bridge would look shabby and carts would go around, to the ford at Whitmarsh three miles south, and the toll would decline, and the town would decline with it.

"I'll report to the steward," Ralf said. He was writing on a wax tablet — small, neat marks that Iden could not read because Iden could not read, which was another fact about the system: the reeve who ran the town could count but not read, and the accounts were kept in tally sticks that he understood and written records that he didn't, and the written records were the ones that mattered, because the written records were what the lord's court accepted, and the lord's court was where disputes ended.

"The piers are sound?"

"From what I can see. The water's too high to check the base."

"You'll note that in the report?"

"I'll note what I can see and what I can't."

Ralf left. Iden stood on the bridge and looked down at the water. The eastern pier stood in the current, the water parting around it and rejoining downstream in a V-shaped wake that caught leaves and small branches and carried them away. Beneath the waterline, the crack was there. The river was inside the stone, working. And the mason's report would say: piers sound from visible inspection; water level precluded examination of base courses. And the lord would read this — or have it read to him, because Iden was not certain the lord could read either — and the lord would conclude that the bridge was sound except for the parapets, and the parapets would be repointed or they wouldn't, and the eastern pier would go on cracking, and Iden would go on knowing, and the knowledge would sit in him the way the water sat in the stone — slowly, patiently, doing its work.

Saturday, there was a hearing.

The manor court met in the priory's guesthouse, a long stone room with a trestle table at one end and benches for the jurors and a brazier that gave off more smoke than heat. The steward presided. Iden sat beside him, because the reeve sat beside the steward; this was custom, and custom was law, and law was custom, and the distinction between them was a thing that scholars at the universities might argue about but that had no practical meaning in a town of two hundred and forty souls who knew what they owed and to whom.

Three cases. The first: Gilbert Hayward's cow had broken through Edith Cooper's fence and eaten her cabbages. Gilbert owed Edith compensation. The amount was disputed. Iden had looked at the cabbages — what was left of them — and at the fence, which was rotting and had needed repair before the cow ever touched it, and he had formed an opinion, which was that Gilbert owed Edith fourpence for the cabbages and Edith owed herself a new fence, and this opinion would become the court's judgment because the steward deferred to the reeve in matters of local fact, because the steward came from Breckham and did not know whose fence was sound and whose was rotting and whose cow was a habitual wanderer.

"Fourpence," Iden said, and the steward nodded, and it was done.

The second case was harder. Robert Smith — the town's only smith, which gave him a weight that other men of his station did not carry — had refused a labor day. Not sent someone in his place. Not complained and then gone. Simply refused. He had been summoned to the demesne to repair a plow blade and he had said no. He had said: I am behind on the work the town needs. The lord's plow can wait.

The steward looked at Iden. Iden looked at Robert, who sat on the bench with his massive arms crossed over his leather apron and his jaw set in the way that meant he would not speak until he was required to and possibly not then.

"Robert."

"I'm behind. Six horses unshod. Two plow blades. A hinge for the mill door."

"The lord's plow needs mending."

"The town's plows need mending first. If the town doesn't plow, there's no harvest. If there's no harvest, there's no tithe. The lord gets his plow fixed and loses six farms' worth of grain."

This was a good argument. It was the kind of argument that, in a different system, would be decisive. But this was not a different system. This was the system they had, and in this system the lord's claim on Robert's labor was absolute and prior and not subject to the logic of efficiency, because the lord's claim was not based on efficiency. It was based on the fact that Robert held his smithy and his house and his forge on the lord's land and owed the lord a day's work each week in return, and this obligation was not economic. It was legal. It was the price of being Robert, here, in this place, with this forge and this house and these tools.

"You'll go tomorrow," Iden said.

"Tomorrow's Sunday."

"You'll go Monday, then. And you'll make up the day."

Robert unfolded his arms. He looked at Iden — not with anger, exactly, but with the particular expression of a man who has just seen another man choose the system over the person, and who understands the choice, and who knows he would have made the same choice, and who hates the man for it anyway.

"Monday," Robert said.

The third case did not come to judgment. It was a matter of Agnes Thatcher's goat, which had been found on the priory's land, grazing in the monks' orchard. The prior wanted compensation. Agnes said the goat had wandered. Iden knew — everyone knew — that Agnes had put the goat on the priory's land deliberately, because the priory's land had grass and Agnes's strip had none, and the goat needed to eat and Agnes could not afford feed, and so she had made the calculation that the risk of the fine was worth the certainty of the goat's hunger, and the calculation had come out wrong, and now she owed the prior thruppence that she did not have.

Iden deferred the case. He told the steward that the matter needed investigation, that the ownership of the goat was unclear — it wasn't — and that the boundaries of the priory's orchard were in dispute — they weren't — and that the case should be held over until the next session. The steward agreed, because the steward did not care about Agnes's goat and wanted to finish and go back to Breckham before the rain worsened.

After the court, Agnes found Iden outside the guesthouse.

"You put it off."

"I put it off."

"It won't go away."

"No."

"I can't pay thruppence."

"I know."

She stood there, small and wet, her dead husband's tunic hanging from her shoulders, and Iden felt the weight of her looking at him — the weight of a person who understands that the man standing before her is both the source of her trouble and the only person who might alleviate it, and that these two things are not a contradiction but a design feature of the system they both inhabit.

"I'll talk to Brother Eustace," Iden said. "The prior listens to Eustace. Maybe we can settle it as a tithe credit — your labor on the orchard wall offsets the fine."

"More labor."

"It's what there is, Agnes."

She left. Iden watched her go. She walked east, toward the bridge, crossing it to reach the small house on the far side where she lived with her children and her goat in an arrangement that was not poverty, exactly, but was the thing that poverty would become if one more thing went wrong — if the goat died or the baby sickened or the roof gave way or the labor days increased or any of the hundred small disasters that separated survival from ruin in a town where survival was the most ambitious outcome anyone could reasonably pursue.

The bridge held her weight. It held the weight of the cart that followed her, driven by a man bringing firewood from the forest to sell at the priory. It held the weight of two boys running across it, chasing a dog. It held all of this weight as it had held it for seventy years, the three arches spanning the river, the piers standing in the current, the crack in the eastern pier growing imperceptibly, invisibly, beneath the high water.

Sunday was quiet. The town went to Mass and came back and did the things that were permitted on a Sunday, which was very little, and avoided the things that were not permitted, which was everything useful. Margery mended a shirt. Iden sat in the house and looked at the wall and thought about nothing, which was not the same as resting, because resting implied that the mind was at ease, and Iden's mind was not at ease but had merely reached a state of saturation where one more thought would be one too many, and so it had stopped.

In the evening, Hild came home from Alys's house.

"Alys says the loom needs a new heddle bar."

"Tell Alys to talk to Robert."

"Robert's behind. Six horses. Two plows."

"Then Alys waits."

"If she waits, she misses the Stourbridge order. If she misses the order, she loses the merchant. If she loses the merchant, she's back to selling at market for half the price."

"Then she finds another way."

"There isn't another way. The loom needs a heddle bar and the heddle bar needs iron and the iron needs Robert and Robert is behind because you sent him to fix the lord's plow."

Iden looked at his daughter. She was standing in the doorway with her arms at her sides and her face set in an expression he recognized, because it was his own expression — the look of a person who has followed a chain of causes to its origin and found that the origin is the person standing in front of them.

"Robert owes the lord a day's work," Iden said.

"And Alys owes nothing and still pays."

This was true. Alys did not owe the lord a day's work, because her husband had purchased that exemption, and the exemption held. But Alys's business depended on Robert's forge, and Robert's forge depended on Robert's labor, and Robert's labor was owed in part to the lord, and so the lord's claim on Robert's body rippled outward through the town like a stone dropped in the river, touching Alys, touching Hild, touching the merchant at Stourbridge, touching the sheep whose wool became the cloth, touching everyone and everything in a web of obligation that no single person had designed and no single person could undo and that everyone, including Iden, including Hild, including the lord himself, mistook for the natural order of things because the alternative — that it was constructed, arbitrary, maintained only by the collective agreement to maintain it — was too large and too frightening to hold in the mind for any length of time.

"I'll talk to Robert," Iden said.

"When?"

"Monday. After he's done the lord's work."

Hild left. She did not slam the door, because the door was a curtain of heavy cloth and could not be slammed, but she let it fall with a weight that communicated what a slam would have communicated. Margery, from the corner where she was mending, said nothing. The saying of nothing was its own statement, and Iden received it as such, and the evening continued.

---

Monday came. Iden collected the week's toll from the bridge — three shillings fourpence, which was low, which was the weather, which was the roads, which was the time of year, which was everything and nothing. He counted the coins into the leather pouch and tied it and set it on the shelf where the lord's money sat until the steward's man came for it. Beside it, in a smaller pouch, was Iden's portion. Eight pence. The difference. The customary theft that was not theft because custom made it otherwise.

He went to the demesne field and checked that Robert was there, and Robert was there, hammering the plow blade straight on a portable anvil he'd brought from the forge, his boy working the bellows on a small charcoal fire while the rain hissed on the coals. Robert looked up at Iden and looked down again and said nothing, and the nothing was comprehensive.

Leofric was plowing the south strip. Walter's labor, performed by Walter's tenant, on the lord's field, for the lord's benefit, at Walter's direction, using the lord's plow, which Robert was repairing because the lord required it and not because Robert wanted to. The chain of obligation was visible here, out in the open, in the mud and the rain and the smell of turned earth — the lord at the top, the steward below him, the reeve below the steward, the tenants below the reeve, the cottagers below the tenants, and below the cottagers the animals, who at least did not have to pretend that the arrangement was anything other than what it was.

Agnes was in the next strip. She was plowing alone, because her boy was home with the baby and her husband was dead and she had no cottager to send. She leaned into the plow with her whole body — not the technique a man would use, leveraging upper-body strength against the handles, but a full-body lean, using her weight as force, dragging the blade through the clay by the simple fact of her existence pressing forward. It was not efficient. It was not elegant. It was the only way she could do it, and so it was the way she did it, and by midday she would have plowed a quarter of what Leofric had plowed, and the record would show a day's labor given, and the quality of the labor would not be noted, because the system did not measure quality, only compliance.

Iden watched her for a moment and then walked away. He walked to the bridge and stood on the eastern side and looked down at the water. Still high. Still covering the crack. The rain was lighter today — more of a mist, really — but the river was slow to drop, and it would be a week or more before the base of the pier was visible again.

A woman crossed the bridge toward him. Alys Weaver, carrying a bolt of cloth wrapped in oilskin. She was going to the priory to sell it — the monks bought cloth for their habits and for altar coverings and for the small luxuries that monks were not supposed to want but did, because monks were men and men wanted soft things next to their skin even when their vows said otherwise.

"Iden."

"Alys."

"Hild tells me Robert can't do the heddle bar until next week."

"Robert's on the lord's work today."

"Tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow Robert has six horses to shoe."

"And after the horses?"

"After the horses, Robert will do what Robert does, in the order Robert decides, and I don't tell Robert how to run his forge."

Alys looked at him. She had a face that showed exactly what she was thinking at all times, which was a disadvantage in business but an advantage in conversation, because you never had to guess. Right now she was thinking that Iden could help her and was choosing not to, and she was right, and they both knew it, and the knowing made the moment uncomfortable in the way that truth always makes moments uncomfortable.

"I'll manage," she said, and went on to the priory, and the bridge held her, and the water flowed, and the crack grew or didn't grow, and the day went on.

---

That night Iden dreamed of the bridge, but the dream was not dramatic. In the dream, the bridge was exactly as it was — three arches, limestone, a crack in the eastern pier. In the dream, Iden stood on the bank and watched the water rise, and the water rose slowly, and the bridge did not fall. The bridge simply stood there, cracking, holding, carrying its weight, doing the thing it was built to do while slowly, imperceptibly, ceasing to be capable of doing it. And Iden stood and watched, because that was the thing he was built to do.

He woke to the sound of the river. Still high. Still patient.

---

Tuesday was Iden's day. Not the lord's day or the steward's day or the day Iden owed to the demesne, but his own day — the day he worked his own strip, his own land, his own ten acres in the common field that fed his family and paid his tithe and justified his existence as a man who held land and owed labor and collected tolls and kept tallies and managed the affairs of two hundred and forty people who had not chosen him and could not remove him and needed him in the way that a body needs a joint — not for itself but for what it connects.

He plowed his strip. The soil was heavy clay, waterlogged, difficult. The plow blade caught on roots and stones and the ox — his one ox, shared with Godric across the lane — strained and stopped and strained again, and the furrow that Iden cut was uneven and shallow and would need to be plowed again before planting, and the planting was late, and the planting being late meant the harvest would be late, and the harvest being late meant the tithe would be late, and the tithe being late meant the lord would note it, and the lord's noting would travel down through the steward to the reeve, who was Iden, so the note would arrive at the same man who had caused it, completing a circuit of blame that had no beginning and no end.

He plowed until the bell for Vespers. His back ached. His hands were blistered where the plow handles had worn through the calluses he'd built up the previous season. He walked home through the town and passed the bridge and did not look at it, or looked at it in the way that a man looks at a thing he has decided not to think about — with a deliberate absence of attention that is its own kind of attention.

At home, Margery had made pottage. Beans, an onion, a heel of the black bread that was their daily fare. Hild was still at Alys's house. Thomas and Wat were asleep in the loft, curled around each other like puppies.

"The water's dropping," Margery said.

"I know."

"When it drops enough —"

"I know."

She set the bowl in front of him and he ate. The pottage was thin and tasted of nothing much. In the houses around them, two hundred and forty people were eating their own thin pottage and going to their own straw beds and sleeping the heavy sleep of people who had worked from dawn to dark at labor they had not chosen for the benefit of people they would never meet, and they would wake tomorrow and do it again, and the day after that, and the day after that, in a sequence so long and so unbroken that it had the appearance of permanence, of nature, of the way things simply were.

The bridge stood over the river. The crack in the eastern pier was a finger's width, or two fingers, or three — Iden could not remember exactly, and the not-remembering was itself a choice, because a man who knows the exact width of a crack is a man who has measured it, and a man who has measured it is a man who must report it, and a man who must report it is a man who must bear the cost of the knowledge, and the cost was more than Iden could carry, not because he was weak but because the cost was designed to be more than any one man could carry, so that no one man would ever bear it, so that it would be distributed, like the labor, like the tithe, like the toll, across the whole body of the town, which would absorb it and continue, as it had always absorbed and always continued, the system maintaining itself through the ordinary daily action of ordinary people doing what they had always done because they could not imagine doing otherwise.

Iden finished his pottage. He set the bowl on the table. Outside, the river moved in the dark, carrying silt and branches and leaves and the slow patient weight of water over stone, over the crack, over everything. The bridge held. It held today. Tomorrow it would hold again.

This was not a story about a bridge falling. This was a story about a bridge standing, and standing, and standing, while the thing that would bring it down grew so slowly that no one who lived beside it could see the change from one day to the next, only the accumulation, over seasons, over years, of small failures that no single person caused and no single person could prevent and that everyone, in their own small way, by their own small choices, allowed to continue.

The bell rang for Compline. Iden went to bed. The river ran. The bridge stood over it, cracked and holding, cracked and holding, doing the work.
