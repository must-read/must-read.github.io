# Story Plan: hf-epic-001

## Assignment

- **Genre**: Historical Fiction
- **Subgenre**: Historical Epic / Saga
- **Combination ID**: hf-epic-001
- **Target Word Count**: 7,800 words (range: 7,500-8,100)
- **Risk Card**: None

### Formula

| Element | Source | Contribution |
|---------|--------|-------------|
| Author A | Colson Whitehead | Speculative-inflected historical fiction; Black American history rendered through alternating registers from lyrical to brutal; sentences that shift from documentary to mythic mid-paragraph |
| Author B | Ken Follett | Sweeping multi-plot architecture; accessible, cinematic prose; the engineering of suspense across decades; material detail |
| Work X | *The Sympathizer* (Viet Thanh Nguyen) | Confession/memoir form; first-person account as political argument; narrator as double agent; a single voice carrying multiple loyalties |
| Work Y | *Beloved* (Toni Morrison) | The past that will not stay buried; the cost of survival; the body remembers what the mind forgets; a people haunted by their own history |

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Colson Whitehead (Author A)
**Source**: *The Underground Railroad*, opening section (Ajarry)
**Why representative**: Shows Whitehead's signature register-shifting -- documentary slave-trade fact rendered in compressed, mythic language that treats economic transactions as existential events.

> The first time Caesar approached Cora about running north, she said no. This was her grandmother talking. Cora's grandmother had never seen the ocean before that bright afternoon in the port of Ouidah and the water dazzled after her time in the dungeon. The dungeon stored them until the ships arrived. Dahomeyan raiders kidnapped the men first and returned to the village for the women and children, marching them in chains to the sea. Ajarry was sold a few times on the way to the fort, passed between slavers for cowrie shells and glass beads... She learned to quickly528 528 528 528 read the new masters. Distinguishing the merely cruel from the savage. She survived her time on the Nance farm, on the Bard farm, and the Fields farm. The Randall plantation was the last stop. Two yellow-haired sailors rowed Ajarry out to the ship, humming. White skin like bone.

**Additional Whitehead sample** from *The Nickel Boys*:
> Even in death the boys were trouble. The secret graveyard lay on the north side of the Nickel campus, in a patchy acre of wild grass between the old work barn and the school dump.

This shows Whitehead's gift for the opening that combines irony with moral gravity, and his tendency to let a physical landscape carry historical weight.

### Sample 2: Ken Follett (Author B)
**Source**: *The Pillars of the Earth*, Tom Builder section
**Why representative**: Demonstrates Follett's material-detail approach, where physical objects and their construction carry emotional and dramatic weight, and his technique of embedding exposition within compelling narrative momentum.

> It would have been a lifetime job, barring accidents. But Tom had turned it down, for he wanted to build another cathedral. His wife, Agnes, had never understood that decision. They might have had a good stone house, and servants, and their own stables, and meat on the table every dinnertime; and she had never forgiven Tom for turning down the opportunity. She could not comprehend the irresistible attraction of building a cathedral: the absorbing complexity of organization, the intellectual challenge of the calculations, the sheer size of the walls, and the breathtaking beauty and grandeur of the finished building.

Follett's technique: the material reality of architecture (stone, calculations, walls) becomes the vehicle for exploring ambition, marriage, and the cost of vision. He makes readers care about mortar by connecting it to human desire. His prose is deceptively simple, each sentence load-bearing, optimized for readability while engineering suspense across long timespans.

### Sample 3: Viet Thanh Nguyen / *The Sympathizer* (Work X)
**Source**: Opening pages
**Why representative**: Shows the confession form in action -- the narrator addressing a captor/authority figure, the ironic self-awareness, the voice that carries political argument within intimate revelation.

> I am a spy, a sleeper, a spook, a man of two faces. Perhaps not surprisingly, I am also a man of two minds... I am simply able to see any issue from both sides. Sometimes I flatter myself that this is a talent, and although it is admittedly one of a minor nature, it is perhaps also the sole talent I possess.

And later, regarding the physical conditions of confession:

> Privacy, not cleanliness, is my only prerequisite for writing this confession.

The confession form in *The Sympathizer* functions as: (a) a literal document produced under constraint, (b) a vehicle for ironic self-examination, (c) a political argument disguised as personal revelation. The narrator's voice is characterized by compound-complex sentences, philosophical asides, and a sardonic intelligence that makes complicity readable. The confession is addressed to a specific authority (the Commandant), which gives it direction and a power dynamic. The entire novel is staged as self-criticism -- the narrator constructing a narrative that serves a structure of power while simultaneously subverting it.

### Sample 4: Toni Morrison / *Beloved* (Work Y)
**Source**: Opening of *Beloved*
**Why representative**: Shows Morrison's technique of making the supernatural feel material, her use of numbered specificity, and her method of delivering historical trauma through domestic detail.

> 124 WAS SPITEFUL. Full of a baby's venom. The women in the house knew it and so did the children. For years each put up with the spite in his own way, but by 1873 Sethe and her daughter Denver were its only victims. The grandmother, Baby Suggs, was dead, and the sons, Howard and Buglar, had run away by the time they were thirteen years old -- as soon as merely looking in a mirror shattered it (that was the signal for Buglar); as soon as two tiny hand prints appeared in the cake (that was it for Howard).

Morrison's key techniques for the writer to internalize:
- **The house as character**: 124 is "spiteful" -- given agency, personality, will. The physical dwelling accumulates the trauma of its inhabitants.
- **Embodied knowledge**: The body remembers what the mind cannot articulate. Sethe's back carries the scar-tree. Denver's deafness was psychosomatic. The past intrudes physiologically.
- **Prose that breaks under pressure**: In the most intense passages, Morrison's prose fragments into poetry -- grammar dissolves because the experience exceeds language's capacity to hold it.
- **The haunting as unresolvable**: Beloved is exorcised at the novel's end, but the final page insists "This is not a story to pass on" -- the telling itself is both necessary and insufficient. The past cannot be laid to rest; it can only be witnessed.

## Wikipedia Concepts

### Concept Mining Results

From research into obscure/specific historical and natural phenomena, two concepts selected for fusion:

**Selected Concept 1: South Carolina Phosphate Mining (1870s-1890s)**
After the Civil War, massive phosphate deposits were discovered in the South Carolina Lowcountry. By 1880, over 1,600 Black men mined the land, extracting ancient fossilized bone deposits from the earth for fertilizer production. Workers earned about $1.75/day for grueling labor -- digging, by hand, into soil packed with the compressed remains of creatures millions of years old. The industry peaked at 5,242 workers by 1892 before collapsing in the late 1890s. Convict leasing supplied additional forced labor. The metaphorical resonance is extraordinary: Black men literally mining bones from the ground in the decades after emancipation, bones that are millions of years old, while their own ancestors' remains lie in unmarked graves in the same Lowcountry soil.

**Integration**: The family's second generation works the phosphate mines near Charleston. The grandfather digs fossilized bones from the earth -- ancient marine animal remains compressed into phosphate rock. This becomes the story's central material image: bones extracted from soil, the past literally unearthed and processed into something else (fertilizer -- growth from ancient death). The narrator will describe the phosphate dust that coated her grandfather's hands, the way it smelled like old rain and something older, the way the mine left sinkholes that swallowed houses decades later.

**Selected Concept 2: Foxfire (Bioluminescent Fungi in Decaying Wood)**
Foxfire is the bioluminescence produced by certain fungi colonizing rotting wood. The bluish-green glow is created by luciferin oxidation -- the same chemical process as in fireflies. Aristotle described it as "cold fire." The phenomenon is common in the American Southeast, particularly in old-growth hardwood forests. Miners in the 19th century used glowing pieces of foxfire as safety markers in tunnels where open flames would be dangerous.

**Integration**: The family house -- built by the second generation from salvaged timber -- develops foxfire in its foundation beams and porch posts as the wood slowly decays over decades. The narrator's grandmother noticed it first in the 1920s: a faint bluish-green glow visible only in total darkness. She called it the house breathing. The foxfire is not supernatural, but it FEELS supernatural -- the house emitting light from its own decomposition. This becomes the physical manifestation of the "haunting" -- the past glowing through the structure that contains it, visible only when you stop trying to see by other light. The narrator, in her own generation, discovers the glow is brightest in the room where the first death occurred.

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

Oralee Gaines, 61, sits down to write a document she has been avoiding for thirty years: a letter to her daughter Josephine, who has asked why her mother cannot sleep in rooms with wooden floors. The question is simple. The answer requires a century. What Oralee produces is not a letter but a confession -- four generations of a Lowcountry Black family whose relationship to the land beneath them has been defined by what that land holds and what it refuses to release. The engine: each generation believed they were building something permanent on ground that was, in every sense, undermined.

### 2. Protagonist: Oralee Gaines (the narrator/confessor)

- **Age**: 61 at the time of writing (circa 1972)
- **Situation**: A retired schoolteacher living alone in Philadelphia, having left South Carolina in 1938 at age 27. She has not returned. Her daughter Josephine, now 33, a social worker in Atlanta, has been asking questions Oralee has deflected for years -- about the family land, about why her mother left, about the house.
- **What she WANTS**: To answer Josephine's question cleanly. To give her daughter a narrative that makes sense. To close the ledger.
- **What she NEEDS**: To admit that she cannot close the ledger because the debt is not hers alone -- it runs through the soil itself, and her flight north did not escape it but only delayed its claim. She needs to confess that she is not the narrator of this story but one of its symptoms.
- **Her flaw**: Oralee believes that understanding equals resolution. She has spent her life as an educator, teaching children that knowledge is power, that naming a thing gives you authority over it. She applies this same logic to her family's history -- if she can narrate it coherently, she can master it. But the history resists coherence, and her insistence on imposing it is itself a form of evasion. She keeps narrating OTHER generations' lives to avoid the thing she did.

### 3. Structure (Confession Form, Four Generations, Three Registers)

**Generation 1 -- MYTH REGISTER (~1,200 words)**
**Era**: 1860s-1870s (Reconstruction)
**Figure**: Abedna, Oralee's great-grandmother

Oralee opens by acknowledging she is inventing. She knows almost nothing about Abedna -- a name on a Freedmen's Bureau record, a birthdate that may be wrong, a notation that she claimed forty acres near the Stono River. The register is oral/legendary: the cadence of a story passed down and worn smooth by retelling. Oralee tells Josephine what she was told as a child, but she marks the seams where invention replaces knowledge. Abedna walked off a rice plantation with her two children and nothing else. She found the land. The land had something wrong with it -- a smell, a softness in certain places, a tendency to swallow fence posts. She built on it anyway because it was hers and ownership was still a word that tasted new.

The land's "wrongness" is the phosphate deposits beneath the soil -- ancient bone compressed into rock, millions of years of marine death layered under Lowcountry mud. Abedna didn't know what was under her feet. She only knew the ground sometimes sighed when you dug into it, and that the well water had a mineral taste that coated the teeth.

**Generation 2 -- DOCUMENTARY REGISTER (~2,400 words)**
**Era**: 1880s-1910s
**Figure**: Ruel Gaines, Oralee's grandfather

This is the section that swells -- the generation rendered in the most detail, the anchoring center of the confession. Ruel is the one Oralee's mother talked about constantly, the one whose life was documented enough to feel real. Ruel worked the phosphate mines from age sixteen. He married a woman named Dovie who wove sweetgrass baskets in the Gullah tradition. They saved for fifteen years and expanded Abedna's original claim, building a proper house from salvaged cypress timber. Ruel's hands were permanently stained gray-white from phosphate dust. He could identify fossilized shark teeth and whale vertebrae in the mine face. He knew he was digging up an ocean floor that had been dead for millions of years, and he had a complicated relationship with that knowledge -- extracting death from the earth to be processed into fertilizer, into growth, into profit that was mostly not his.

Follett's architectural/material detail dominates here: the construction of the house (cypress beams, tin roof, porch facing east), the mechanics of open-pit phosphate mining (the pick work, the washing, the drying beds), the specific economy of the Lowcountry (what phosphate paid, what land cost, what a Black family needed to survive the century's turn). This section has the tangibility of a Ken Follett set piece -- the reader can see, smell, and touch this world.

The crisis of this generation: the phosphate industry collapses in the late 1890s. Ruel has no income but owns the land. He discovers that the mining company has legal claims on mineral rights beneath his property -- rights sold by the previous "owner" before emancipation. The land is his on the surface. What's under it belongs to someone else. A man can own his ground and still have the bones beneath it claimed by another.

**Generation 3 -- TRANSITIONAL REGISTER (half-documented, half-legendary) (~1,800 words)**
**Era**: 1910s-1930s
**Figure**: Nola Gaines, Oralee's mother

Nola is the generation Oralee knew personally but understood least. Born in 1899, Nola grew up in the house Ruel built. She was the one who first noticed the foxfire -- the bluish-green glow in the foundation timbers, visible only on moonless nights when every lamp was out. She told young Oralee the house was breathing, that the old cypress was remembering being alive. Nola refused to leave the Lowcountry during the first wave of the Great Migration. Neighbors, cousins, entire families packed for Philadelphia, New York, Chicago. Nola stayed. Her staying was not passive -- it was fierce, daily, and increasingly expensive.

This section is where Whitehead's register-shifting is most evident. Oralee's narration of Nola moves between documentary confidence (dates, events, the historical context of Jim Crow South Carolina) and mythic uncertainty (what Nola believed about the house, the rituals she performed, her relationship with the land that was simultaneously a prison and the only freedom the family had ever possessed).

The crisis: Nola's husband (Oralee's father, Clement) is arrested on a vagrancy charge in 1928 and leased to a turpentine camp. He returns seven months later with a broken jaw that never set right and a refusal to speak about what happened. He cannot sleep in the house. He sleeps in the yard, then in the woods, then not at all. He dies in 1931 of what the county doctor called pneumonia but what Nola called going away. The land that was supposed to protect them could not protect him. The body remembered what the law did to it.

**Generation 4 -- CONFESSION REGISTER (evasive, fragmented, present-tense intrusions) (~2,400 words)**
**Era**: 1930s-1972
**Figure**: Oralee herself

This is where the narrator's own story should emerge in the most detail, but instead -- per Whitehead's suggestion from the meeting -- the prose becomes most evasive. Oralee narrates everything around her own central act. She describes the house in the 1930s: the foxfire brighter now, the subsidence from old phosphate mines creating depressions in the yard, the property tax increases that threatened to swallow the land. She describes her mother's determination. She describes her own education -- Nola insisted -- at a one-room school where she learned the word "geology" and suddenly understood what was beneath the house.

But when she reaches 1938, the narration fractures. Sentences become shorter. The confession form, which has been fluid and controlled for thousands of words, begins to stutter. What Oralee did: she sold the mineral rights to the family land -- the same rights Ruel had fought to retain -- to a phosphate prospector who was re-surveying depleted mines. She did this without telling Nola. She used the money to buy a train ticket to Philadelphia. She told Nola she was going to visit a cousin. She never came back.

The prospector's operation created new sinkholes. The house foundation cracked. Nola lived in the house until 1954, when a section of the east porch collapsed into a subsidence void. She moved in with a neighbor. She died in 1959. Oralee did not attend the funeral. The house is still standing, though it has sunk fourteen inches on one side. The foxfire, Oralee imagines, still glows in its leaning timbers.

### 4. Key Scenes

1. **Ruel in the phosphate mine** (~800 words): A cinematic, Follett-detailed scene of a day in the open-pit mine. Ruel identifies a fossilized vertebra the size of a dinner plate. His foreman tells him to break it and keep digging. Ruel pockets a fragment. The material specificity of this scene -- the weight of the pick, the color of the exposed rock, the sound of the washing sluice -- must make the reader feel the labor. This scene also introduces the central metaphor: bones in the ground, extracted by hands that have their own bone-history.

2. **Nola discovers the foxfire** (~500 words): Oralee, age eight, is woken by her mother in the dead of night. Nola carries her to the east porch and tells her to look. The cypress posts glow faintly blue-green. "The house is breathing," Nola says. This is the story's uncanny fulcrum -- the moment where the natural (bioluminescent fungi) and the haunted (a house that remembers) become indistinguishable. The child Oralee is terrified. The adult narrator Oralee, writing the confession, realizes the terror was not about the glow but about the realization that even wood can carry something it didn't choose.

3. **Clement's return from the turpentine camp** (~600 words): He arrives at the house at dawn. His jaw is wrong. He says nothing. Nola makes him food he cannot eat because his teeth don't meet. He sits in the kitchen and looks at his hands. This scene must operate entirely through physical detail (Follett's technique) and withheld information (Morrison's technique). The reader never learns what happened in the camp. The body tells them enough.

4. **Oralee signs the mineral rights document** (~400 words): The scene Oralee has been avoiding for the entire confession. Rendered in short, flat sentences -- almost bureaucratic. A man with a leather case. A table in a lawyer's office in Beaufort. Her signature. The amount: $340. What she bought with it. What she left behind. The prose here should be the most controlled and the least emotional, because the emotion is so large that the narrator cannot touch it directly. The contrast with the mythic register of the Abedna section should be stark.

5. **Oralee in Philadelphia, present tense, 1972** (~300 words): The confession's final scene. Oralee puts down her pen. She is sitting in a rented room with linoleum floors -- no wood. She has arranged her life to avoid the material that carries the past. She realizes she has not answered Josephine's question. She has given her daughter a century of context for a symptom she still cannot name. The letter ends mid-thought, not because the narrator chooses to stop but because the confession has reached the limit of what it can say.

### 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Opening**: The reader feels the narrator's self-possession -- an educated, articulate woman sitting down to answer a simple question. There's confidence, even authority. The reader trusts this voice.

**Abedna section**: Awe and unease. The mythic register creates something dreamlike -- a woman claiming land that seems alive, soil that sighs. The reader feels the grandeur of Reconstruction hope and the wrongness underneath it.

**Ruel section**: Grounded absorption. The Follett-detailed mine scenes pull the reader into physical reality. They care about this man, his hands, his house. They feel the weight of labor that is simultaneously dignified and exploitative. When the mineral rights crisis arrives, the reader feels outrage on Ruel's behalf -- a clean, comprehensible injustice.

**Nola section**: Growing dread. The foxfire, Clement's return, the tightening economic noose. The reader senses the narrator avoiding something. The gaps in the storytelling become visible. The reader begins to distrust not the narrator's honesty but her completeness.

**Oralee section**: The emotional floor drops out. When the mineral rights sale is revealed, the reader realizes the entire confession has been a delay tactic -- the narrator telling three generations of suffering to contextualize (and defer) her own betrayal. The feeling is not judgment but recognition: this is what guilt does to narrative. It makes you tell every story except the one that matters.

**Final moment**: Not catharsis. Not resolution. The reader feels the weight of a confession that fails at its stated purpose but succeeds at something the narrator didn't intend -- making the shape of the damage visible. The letter cannot answer Josephine's question because the question is not about wooden floors. It's about whether inheritance includes the things you did to survive it.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Colson Whitehead (Author A)** -- Most visible in: the **register-shifting across generations** and in the **Oralee section's evasive fragmentation**. Whitehead's technique of moving from documentary to mythic mid-paragraph should be the narrator's dominant mode in the Abedna section, where history and invention bleed together. His influence also governs the story's moral architecture: no generation gets to be purely heroic or purely victimized. Each carries complicity alongside endurance. The tone should carry Whitehead's signature quality of ironic compassion -- never cynical, but refusing to be sentimental.

**Ken Follett (Author B)** -- Most visible in: the **Ruel/phosphate mine sequence** and the **construction of the house**. This section must have the tangibility and cinematic specificity of Follett's best set pieces. The reader should understand how a phosphate mine operates, how a cypress house is built, what the economy of the 1880s Lowcountry looked like. Follett's influence also shapes the story's overall architecture -- the sweeping, compressed saga across decades requires his engineering of time (transitions, temporal landmarks, the material details that orient the reader in a specific year without stating the date). His principle: "equality is the enemy of drama" -- the four generations are unevenly distributed, with Ruel's section swelling to anchor the whole.

**The Sympathizer / Viet Thanh Nguyen (Work X)** -- Most visible in: the **confession form itself** and in Oralee's **voice as a narrator who is constructing a case**. Like the Sympathizer's narrator addressing the Commandant, Oralee addresses Josephine -- but where Nguyen's narrator knows he is performing for an authority, Oralee is performing for her own child, which makes the power dynamic inverted and more painful. The confession is ostensibly a gift (here is your history, daughter) but is actually a defense (here is why I did what I did). The double agent structure: Oralee is simultaneously the historian of the family and its betrayer. She carries "multiple loyalties" -- to truth, to self-justification, to the dead, to the living daughter she fears burdening.

**Beloved / Toni Morrison (Work Y)** -- Most visible in: the **foxfire/haunting motif**, **Clement's broken body**, and the **insistence that the past does not resolve**. Morrison's influence governs everything the story believes about history: that it lives in the body, that houses absorb the people who lived in them, that survival has a cost the survivor cannot name. The foxfire IS the story's Beloved -- not a ghost but a physical manifestation of the past's refusal to decompose cleanly. The house glows because it is dying, and the dying is visible, and you can only see it when you turn off every other light. Morrison's final page ("This is not a story to pass on") should echo in the story's ending: Oralee's confession, once written, does not save her. It just makes the damage precise.

### 7. Title

**"Bones Below the Deed"**

Rationale: Does not start with "The." References the phosphate mining (bones in the ground), the property deed (the legal document of ownership), and the deeper meaning of "deed" as an act -- the things done, the betrayal committed, the bones of the past lying beneath every action the family has taken. The title works on three levels and should intrigue without revealing the story's direction.

Alternative titles considered and rejected:
- "Foxfire Confession" -- too on-the-nose
- "What the Ground Holds" -- too close to "What the Creek Said" in existing catalog
- "Phosphate and Penance" -- too alliterative, too clever
- "Mineral Rights" -- too cold, though the double meaning is good
- "Sinking Ground" -- too literal

## Writer Instructions

### Voice
The narrator is a 61-year-old Black woman who spent her adult life as a schoolteacher in Philadelphia. Her voice is educated but not academic -- she has the precision of someone who has explained things for a living, but the rhythms of Lowcountry speech surface when she narrates the earlier generations. She is addressing her adult daughter. The tone is intimate but controlled, confessional but strategic. She is building a case.

### What NOT to do
- Do NOT resolve the haunting. The foxfire persists. The house still stands. The question remains unanswered.
- Do NOT make Oralee's betrayal sympathetic in a simple way. She did it for survival. She also did it for escape. These are not the same thing, and the story should not collapse them.
- Do NOT explain the foxfire scientifically within the text. The narrator, an educated woman, could explain it. She chooses not to. That choice tells the reader everything about what the foxfire means to her.
- Do NOT use the word "legacy" or "heritage" or "roots." These are words that make history sound manageable. This history is not manageable.
- Do NOT have any generation's crisis mirror another's too neatly. Whitehead's warning from the meeting: "If every generation faces the same choice in a different costume, the reader will feel manipulated."
- Do NOT end with the narrator finding peace, understanding, or closure. End with her putting down the pen, aware that she has written a hundred-year delay to avoid saying: I sold what my great-grandmother claimed, and the ground opened up, and I was not there.

### Structural Notes
- The confession is a written document (a letter that grew beyond letters), addressed to Josephine.
- Occasional direct addresses to "you" (Josephine) should be sparse -- maybe 5-6 times across the whole piece. More frequent at the beginning (confident) and end (desperate), scarce in the middle (where the narrator loses herself in the history).
- Time transitions should use Follett's technique: logic-of-confession rather than chapter breaks. "To understand what your great-grandmother claimed, you need to understand what was underneath it. To understand what was underneath it, you need to understand the man who dug it up."
- The present tense should intrude only in the final section, when Oralee's control breaks and she slips from past (narrating) to present (confessing).
