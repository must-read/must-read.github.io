**Ticket #4471**
*Category: Recreation & Amenities — Audio — Hold System*
*Priority: Low*
*Settler: Gage, R. (Hab-12, Unit 3)*

*Complaint: Hold music is Vivaldi. Has been on hold 40+ min. Wants to know who chose Vivaldi. Wants to know if anyone on this planet has heard of literally any other composer.*

---

You open the ticket.

You note the category. You note the priority level. You check the settler's account and confirm residency status: active, current on contribution credits, no outstanding flags. Per the Ares Proximity Customer Relations Handbook (rev. 4.1), you compose a preliminary acknowledgment using Standard Response Template 2-C ("Amenity Feedback — Non-Urgent").

*Dear Settler Gage, Thank you for your feedback regarding the hold audio experience. Your input is valued and has been logged for review during our next Settler Satisfaction Cycle. We are committed to ensuring that every interaction with Ares Proximity reflects our dedication to community well-being.*

You do not send it yet. You read it once, the way the handbook instructs: aloud, sotto voce, checking for tone. The handbook says the tone should convey warmth, competence, and forward momentum. You mouth the words at your desk. The desk across from yours is empty. It has been empty for eleven days. Priya's succulent is still there, in its small tin pot, and you have been watering it, though the handbook does not address the care of former colleagues' plants and you are not sure if the water rations cover it.

You send the response.

You open the next ticket.

**Ticket #4472**
*Category: Provisions — Inquiry — Reallocation*
*Priority: Medium*
*Settler: Okonjo, T. (Hab-6, Unit 19)*

*Complaint: Received the Phase-Aligned Dietary Restructuring memo. Does not understand what "Phase-Aligned Dietary Restructuring" means. Asks: Is this a reduction? How much? For how long?*

---

This morning, before your shift, you received the memo yourself. You stood in the corridor outside the service office and read it on your personal display and the words arranged themselves into the shape of something you already knew. "Restructuring" is a word that contains the word "structure," which contains the word "sure," which is the last thing anyone around here is. The memo was three paragraphs. The first paragraph thanked the community. The second paragraph introduced "a refined caloric framework designed to optimize nutritional throughput across all settlement demographics." The third paragraph encouraged settlers to "embrace this transition as an opportunity to align personal consumption with collective resilience."

Nobody wrote this memo on Mars. The phrasing has the specific quality of language that has traveled a long distance — composed in an office on Earth, by a person who has never missed a meal involuntarily, transmitted across however many million kilometers of void, and received here where the word "caloric" does not mean what it means in a boardroom. Here it means a woman in Hab-6 asking you, through the mediation of a complaint ticket, whether her children will eat this week.

You compose a response using Standard Response Template 7-A ("Provisions — Policy Clarification — Supportive").

*Dear Settler Okonjo, Thank you for reaching out regarding the recent dietary guidelines. Phase-Aligned Dietary Restructuring reflects our ongoing commitment to equitable resource distribution across the settlement. Specific allocation details will be communicated through your Hab coordinator within the next provisioning cycle. We appreciate your patience and your partnership in building a sustainable community.*

You send it. The word "sustainable" sits in the response like a stone in a shoe.

You open the next ticket.

**Ticket #4473**
*Category: Water Quality — Cosmetic — Minor*
*Priority: Low*
*Settler: Wren, P. (Hab-9, Unit 2)*

*Complaint: Yellow residue on bathroom fixtures. Faucet, shower head, toilet rim. Residue appeared two weeks ago and will not come off. Smells faintly of sulfur. Settler asks whether the water is safe.*

---

The yellow is sodium maleonitriledithiolate. You know this because you filed twenty-three identical tickets last month and, on your own time, looked it up. It is a filtration byproduct — the chemical equivalent of a bruise, the mark left when the system strains against something it was not built to handle. The original filtration infrastructure was rated for a settlement population of 4,200 and a resupply cycle of ninety days. The current population is 1,307. The last resupply was eleven months ago. The filters are improvising.

You file the ticket under "Water Quality — Cosmetic — Minor" because that is the closest category and because the alternative — filing it under "Infrastructure — Systemic — Critical" — would escalate it to a department that no longer exists. Infrastructure lost its last three technicians to the atmospheric maintenance reassignment in September. Or October. The months have become approximate.

You compose a response.

*Dear Settler Wren, Thank you for reporting this issue. The discoloration you've observed is a normal byproduct of our advanced filtration process and poses no health risk. Our maintenance team has been notified and will address the cosmetic concern at the earliest opportunity. In the meantime, we recommend gentle cleaning with the standard-issue surface solution available at your Hab supply station.*

The water is safe. Probably. The word "advanced" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, carrying more weight than any five-letter word should have to bear. You send the response.

You stand up. You walk to the window.

Mars.

It is the hour when the light changes — when the salmon-colored haze of afternoon thins and for eleven minutes the sky deepens to something that is not blue but is the closest this planet comes. The first colonists called it the Bradbury hour, which is not in any official documentation but which everyone knows. Outside the window, the dust plain extends to the base of the nearest ridge, and the ridge is the color of dried blood, and beyond it the sky holds that almost-blue like a breath held too long. Olympus Mons is not visible from here. It is six hundred kilometers to the northwest. But you know it is there the way you know the shape of a room in the dark, and on certain evenings you understand why people came here, why they left everything green and wet and breathable and crossed the void to stand on iron oxide and call it home. On certain evenings the beauty of this planet is so absolute that you forget, for a moment, that the filtration system is leaving yellow stains on the faucets and nobody has answered the resupply request and Priya's desk has been empty for eleven days.

You sit down. You open the next ticket.

**Ticket #4474**
*Category: Housing — Lighting — Interior — Malfunction*
*Priority: Low*
*Settler: Farrow, E. (Hab-2, Unit 11)*

*Complaint: The light in my unit is wrong. I know that isn't a category. I looked at the categories and none of them are what I mean. The light is wrong. It's not broken — it comes on, it goes off, it dims when I ask it to. But the quality of it is wrong. It's not how light is supposed to look. I've been here nine years and the light has always been wrong but I used to not notice and now I notice every day. The light here is not the light I remember. Can someone fix this? Can someone make the light right?*

---

There is no template for this. There is "Lighting — Interior — Malfunction" and "Lighting — Interior — Request" and "Lighting — Exterior — Schedule" and you have used all of them for complaints that were not really about lighting, the way a doctor's office sees patients who come in for a sore knee and leave having talked about their marriage. Settler Farrow is not complaining about a lightbulb. Settler Farrow is complaining about Mars. About the way sunlight here is thinner, pinker, filtered through an atmosphere that is one percent of what a human eye evolved to expect. The light is wrong because the planet is wrong, or the planet is right and the human eye is wrong, and there is no ticket category for the gap between what a species needs and what a planet provides.

You file it under "Lighting — Interior — Malfunction" and compose a response, and the response is warm and competent, and it will not fix the light, because the light is not broken. The light is Martian. The light has always been Martian. You send it and you open the next ticket.

**Ticket #4475**
*Category: Facilities — Transit — Schedule Inquiry*
*Priority: Low*
*Settler: Babic, N. (Hab-3, Unit 7)*

*Complaint: Settler requests updated shuttle schedule for Mentor Station. Current posted schedule shows no departures or arrivals past 14 October. Asks if this is an error.*

---

Mentor Station is the transit hub at the colony's center. It was built to receive supply shuttles from the orbital transfer point — four bays, each wide enough for a mid-class cargo lander, with an arrivals board that used to update every six hours. The station was the colony's heartbeat. You could stand in the terminal and watch the board cycle through arrivals and departures, cargo manifests and crew rotations, and feel that the colony was connected to something larger, that the void between here and Earth was crossable and was being crossed.

The board has been blank since October.

You pass through Mentor Station every morning on your way to the service office. The terminal is still clean. The custodial bots still sweep the floors and polish the benches and keep the arrival board lit, its screen glowing a steady, empty white. The station smells of ozone and floor polish, which is the smell of a place maintaining itself for visitors who are not coming. You have begun to think of it as a church — a space defined by the absence of the thing it was built to hold.

You compose a response.

*Dear Settler Babic, Thank you for your inquiry regarding Mentor Station scheduling. The current schedule is undergoing a system-wide update to reflect revised transit protocols. Updated departure and arrival information will be posted as soon as the new scheduling framework has been finalized. We apologize for any inconvenience.*

You send it. There is no new scheduling framework. There is no revised transit protocol. There is an empty board and an empty terminal and four landing bays where the dust collects in the shapes of ships that are not there. But the response template does not have a field for that. The closest field is "Scheduling — Update Pending," so that is what you select, and the system accepts it, and the ticket moves from your queue to the resolved column, and the number at the top of your screen decreases by one.

**Ticket #4476**
*Category: Personnel — Missing Person — Report*
*Priority: High*
*Settler: Asante, D. (Hab-14, Unit 1)*

*Complaint: Neighbor (Lum, V., Hab-14, Unit 4) has not been seen in six days. Settler has knocked on door repeatedly. No response. Requests welfare check.*

---

You flag it. You route it to Security — Community Welfare. You do not compose a custom response because the template for missing persons ("Personnel — Welfare — Active Inquiry") is comprehensive and requires only the insertion of the reporting settler's name and the missing person's last known location. The template generates automatically. You watch it populate the fields and you notice that the Security — Community Welfare department has a response time listed as "within 24 hours," and you notice that the last time Security — Community Welfare responded to anything was nineteen days ago, and you notice that you are noticing this, which means you are thinking about it, which means the space between the form and the reality has widened to the point where you can see daylight through it.

You process the ticket. You open the next one.

**Ticket #4477**
*Category: Recreation & Amenities — General — Feedback*
*Priority: Low*
*Settler: Gage, R. (Hab-12, Unit 3)*

*Complaint: Follow-up to Ticket #4471. Settler has now been on hold for 3 hours and 17 minutes. Vivaldi has looped eleven times. Settler no longer cares about the music. Settler asks: Is anyone there?*

---

You recognize the name. Gage, R. The Vivaldi complaint. Three hours ago the problem was the music. Now the problem is the silence behind the music. You understand this shift because you have lived inside it — the moment when the irritation about the small thing burns off and reveals the terror about the large thing crouching underneath.

*Is anyone there?*

You are here. You are at your desk, in the service office, on the fourth floor of the administrative module. The window shows Mars. The office holds six desks and one of them is yours and the other five are empty. You are here.

You compose a response using no template. This is a deviation. The handbook is clear: all settler-facing communications must originate from an approved template. Deviations are logged, reviewed, and may result in a Performance Alignment Consultation. But the approved templates do not contain a response for "Is anyone there?" The closest template is 2-C ("Amenity Feedback — Non-Urgent"), which you already sent, and which did not answer the question because the question was not about amenities.

*Dear Settler Gage, Someone is here. Your call — *

You delete it. You start again.

*Dear Settler Gage, I — *

You delete it. The "I" is a deviation. The handbook specifies the collective first person ("we") or the impersonal ("your Ares Proximity team"). The "I" slipped out like a word in a language you are not supposed to speak at work.

You compose a response using Template 2-C again. You send it. The response is warm, competent, and conveys forward momentum. The response does not answer the question.

**Ticket #4478**
*Category: Environmental — Atmospheric — Alert*
*Priority: Critical*
*Settler: Automated System Alert (Hab-7 through Hab-12)*

*Alert: Atmospheric recycler output in Sectors 7-12 has dropped below 94% efficiency threshold. Affected settlers may notice reduced air quality, headache, or fatigue. Maintenance has been automatically dispatched.*

---

Maintenance has not been automatically dispatched. Maintenance is five people where there used to be forty. The automated system generates the alert and generates the dispatch notice and the dispatch notice goes to an inbox that nobody monitors because the person who monitored it was reassigned to atmospheric maintenance in September and then was reassigned to wherever people go when atmospheric maintenance cannot maintain the atmosphere.

You cannot respond to an automated alert. There is no template. The system does not expect a human in this loop. You are in this loop because every other loop has broken and the tickets have nowhere else to go, and so they come to you, the customer service representative, the person whose job is to close the distance between complaint and resolution, except resolution has left the building and complaint is all that remains.

You flag the alert. You tag it "Escalated — Awaiting Response." You know that "Awaiting Response" is the system's polite term for a ticket that will never be answered, the way "departed" is a polite term for a ship that is not at Mentor Station and is not coming to Mentor Station and was possibly never coming to Mentor Station, and politeness, you are beginning to understand, is the last infrastructure to fail.

**Ticket #4479**
*Category: General — Inquiry — Youth Submission*
*Priority: Low*
*Settler: Lazenby, K. (Hab-11, Unit 6)*

*Complaint: When is my mom coming home? She went to fix the air and she said she'd be back before dinner and it's been four days. Is she coming home for dinner? I did my homework.*

---

You read the ticket. You read it again. The category is wrong — "General — Inquiry — Youth Submission" is for children requesting information about recreational programming or educational resources. The system auto-categorized because the submitter's age flag triggered the youth filter and the youth filter does not have a category for what this is.

You know the name. Lazenby. Rena Lazenby. Atmospheric maintenance. She volunteered for the deep-shaft repair on Recycler 9 four days ago because she had the certification and the certification mattered more than the risk, or the risk mattered less than the air, or both. You processed her transfer paperwork yourself. You filed it under "Personnel — Reassignment — Voluntary" and the form asked for her emergency contact and she wrote her daughter's name, which is Kaia, which is the name on this ticket, which is a child asking whether her mother is coming home for dinner.

The handbook does not cover this.

The handbook covers complaints and inquiries and feedback and reports and escalations and schedule requests and amenity suggestions and every possible category of human need as expressed through the medium of a complaint ticket, but it does not cover a child who has done her homework and is waiting for her mother and does not yet know what the colony knows, which is that Recycler 9 is in a shaft three hundred meters below the surface, and the shaft's communication relay failed two days ago, and nobody has come up and nobody has gone down and the word for this, the real word, is not in any template.

You sit at your desk. The office is empty. Through the window, Mars has gone dark — the almost-blue is gone and the sky is the color of a bruise, violet and brown, and the first stars are not stars but satellites, or were satellites, the dead ones that still orbit because orbit does not require permission or funding or a resupply schedule. They cross the sky like something's memory of purpose.

You type:

*Dear Kaia,*

And you stop. Because "Dear Kaia" is not a template. "Dear Kaia" is a name, spoken aloud to a person, and the handbook does not speak to persons. The handbook speaks to settlers. The handbook addresses account numbers and residency statuses and contribution credits, and a child who has done her homework is none of these things, or is all of these things, or is the reason all of these things were invented in the first place — the forms, the templates, the hold music, the colony, the dream of Mars, the whole enormous structure of human effort that crossed the void and planted itself on iron oxide and said: here. We will live here. And it will be worth it because our children will do their homework and wait for us to come home for dinner.

You type:

*Kaia, your mom is the kind of person who fixes things. She went to fix something important, because that's what she does. The people who fix things sometimes take longer than they expect, because the things that need fixing are bigger than anyone thought. But she said she'd come home, and the kind of person who volunteers to fix the air for everyone is the kind of person who keeps their word.*

*I don't know when she's coming home. I wish I did. But I know she'd want you to eat dinner and keep doing your homework and know that the air you're breathing right now is better because of her.*

You read it. It is not warm, competent, or forward-leaning. It does not convey the values of Ares Proximity. It would fail a Performance Alignment Consultation on every metric. It is not a template. It is a person talking to a person, which is the thing the entire system was designed to prevent, because persons are unpredictable and unpredictability is a liability and liability is something the company cannot afford, though the company can no longer afford anything, including the company.

You send it.

The ticket moves to the resolved column. The number at the top of your screen decreases by one.

---

You stand up. You water Priya's succulent. You put on your jacket — the thin one, with the Ares Proximity logo on the breast pocket, the logo that is a stylized mountain that is supposed to be Olympus Mons but looks more like a corporate chevron, which is perhaps the same thing.

You walk through Mentor Station on the way to your hab. The terminal is lit. The benches are polished. The arrival board glows white and says nothing. Your footsteps echo in the way that footsteps echo in places built for crowds. The custodial bot is buffing the floor near Bay 3 and it hums as it works, a low mechanical tone that is not Vivaldi but is not nothing, and you stop for a moment and listen to it, and the sound fills the terminal the way light fills the almost-blue hour — not completely, not enough, but enough to know that something is still running, something is still making sound, and you are still here to hear it.

You walk home. Mars is cold. Mars is always cold, but tonight there is a quality to the cold that you have not felt before — a thinness, as if the air itself is stretching, as if there is slightly less of it than there was yesterday. You breathe in and the breath tastes of iron and distance and the faintest trace of sulfur, which is the sodium maleonitriledithiolate in the filtration system, which is the yellow on the faucets, which is the twenty-three tickets you filed last month, which is the colony's body showing its bruises.

Tomorrow you will open the queue. There will be new tickets. You will compose responses from approved templates and the responses will be warm and competent and will convey forward momentum, and the settlers will read them and know they are being heard, or at least know that someone is still at the desk, which is not the same thing but is not nothing.

And in your hab, alone, you will stand at the small window that faces northwest, toward Olympus Mons, toward the mountain you have never seen but believe in the way the colony once believed in resupply, and the sky will be dark and full of dead satellites, and you will think about Kaia Lazenby doing her homework and waiting for dinner, and you will think about Rena Lazenby three hundred meters below the surface fixing the air, and you will not cry because crying is not in the handbook, but you will stand at the window for a long time, longer than a person who is fine would stand at a window, and the mountain will be out there in the dark, enormous and invisible and older than anything you can name, and it will not answer and it will not arrive and it will not resolve your ticket, but it will be there, and you will be here, and the distance between those two facts is the whole story, the only story, the hold music playing in the empty terminal of a planet that did not ask to be colonized and does not care that you came and will not notice when you leave.

But you are here.

You are still here.
