## REPORT ON THE PHENOMENON AT SCARDEN VALE

### Submitted to the Proceedings of the Northern Naturalist Society by Edmund Hayle, F.L.S., Member of the Society since 1831

### Filed: December 1847. Classification: Pending.

---

I set down these observations in the full expectation that they will be rejected. The Northern Naturalist Society demands evidence that can be weighed, measured, and pinned to a board, and what I have brought back from Scarden Vale resists all three operations. I have specimens. I have sketches. I have measurements taken with a steel caliper under conditions that, I maintain, were controlled. But the thing I measured does not belong to any phylum the Society recognizes, and I am no longer certain it belongs to science at all.

I record this anyway. What a naturalist sees, a naturalist reports. That compact with observation is the only professional ethic I have left.

---

I arrived in the vale on 14 September of this year, dispatched by Heaton of the Society's Yorkshire Committee, who had received three reports from shepherds concerning a figure observed on the moors north of Scarden farmhouse. The reports were consistent in their particulars: a woman, standing in the heather at dusk or in early fog, motionless, dressed in clothing of an old fashion. No shepherd had approached closer than forty yards. One, a man named Gill, claimed he had heard it speak — a low voice, he said, talking as though someone were in the room with her, though there was no room and no companion.

Heaton suspected an escaped lunatic. He dispatched me because my work on the peat formations of the North York Moors had given me some familiarity with the terrain, and because I had once written a paper on the acoustic properties of fog over open moorland that might, he thought, explain the voice.

I took lodgings at the Scarden Arms, a public house in the village at the southern end of the vale. The landlord, a Mrs. Outhwaite, gave me a room overlooking the street and did not ask my business. When I mentioned the reports, she went very still and said only that Scarden farmhouse was occupied by an old man named Josiah Blacket, who kept to himself, and that the moors above were fit for sheep and not much else.

I asked if she had heard the reports of a woman on the moor.

"I have heard them," she said. "I have also heard that the river runs backward in May and that Gill's grandfather was a selkie. I don't hold with reports."

She charged me three shillings a week and brought me breakfast without further conversation.

---

Scarden farmhouse sits at the head of the vale, backed against a fold in the moor where the heather gives way to exposed rock and thin grass that seems to grow at an angle, as though reaching for something. The house is stone, two stories, with a roof of slate and a single chimney that, on the day I first approached, emitted a thread of smoke so thin it might have been the house breathing.

Josiah Blacket answered the door on the third knock. He was seventy or near it, stooped but not frail, with hands so large they seemed to belong to a younger body. His nails were black — not dirty black but stained, as though he had been working with something that left its color in the skin. Peat, I assumed. The cutters in this district stain their hands to the wrist.

I introduced myself. I told him I was surveying the peat formations above his property for the Naturalist Society. He looked at me with an expression I have since spent many hours attempting to classify and cannot. It was not suspicion. It was the look of a man measuring the distance between what he knows and what you are equipped to hear.

"Survey away," he said, and shut the door.

I surveyed. I spent four days on the moors above Scarden, taking soil samples, sketching the terrain, noting the species of heather (Calluna vulgaris, predominantly, with patches of Erica tetralix in the wetter ground). The work was genuine — I did, in fact, intend to contribute a paper on the peat stratigraphy — but it was also cover. I walked the moor at dusk. I walked it in morning fog. I kept to the areas where the shepherds had reported the figure, and I waited.

On the fifth evening, I saw her.

---

The fog had come in from the north at about half past four, a low fog that erased the ground and left the sky oddly clear, so that the upper edge of the moor was sharp against the evening and the lower half was a grey nothing. I was at approximately the position Gill had described — a shallow depression in the heather, fifty yards from the stone wall that marked the boundary of Blacket's land.

She was standing at the wall. Not leaning on it, not climbing over it — standing beside it, one hand resting on the top stone, as though she had just arrived and was deciding whether to proceed. She was dressed in a dark gown that the fog made colorless. Her hair was loose. She was young — thirty, perhaps, or younger. She was not transparent, not luminous, not spectral in any way I associate with the apparitions described in the literature of popular superstition. She was solid. The fog moved around her the way fog moves around a tree or a gate post. She displaced air.

I am a naturalist. I have held still in blinds for nine hours waiting for a bittern to show itself. I can be very quiet. I sat in the heather and I watched, and what I saw in the next quarter-hour undid everything I had come to Scarden to prove.

She walked. Not gliding, not drifting — walked, with the particular gait of someone who knows uneven ground. Her feet found the tussocks and avoided the wet hollows. She walked along the wall toward the farmhouse and she stopped at the kitchen window and she placed her hand flat against the glass.

The gesture was not theatrical. There was no beating, no clawing. She placed her palm on the pane the way you might touch someone's face when they are sleeping and you do not wish to wake them. And she stood there, hand on glass, looking in at whatever was inside — the fire, the supper, the chair, the old man — and her lips moved, though at that distance and through fog I heard nothing.

I remained in the heather until full dark. She did not move from the window. When I could no longer see, I retreated to the Scarden Arms and wrote these notes by the light of a tallow candle that smoked abominably.

---

Over the next fortnight I returned to the moor each evening. I saw her nine times. She appeared always at dusk or in fog, always walked the same path to the farmhouse window, always placed her hand on the glass. I drew closer by degrees — forty yards, then thirty, then twenty. At twenty yards I could see the texture of her clothing: coarse wool, hand-dyed, of a style I associate with the district's women of a generation past. At fifteen yards I could see her hair, which was dark and carried in it fragments of heather — not stuck to it, as though she had been lying in the field, but woven through it, threaded into the strands as though the heather had grown there.

At ten yards I took a sample.

I record this with some shame. She was standing at the window — her posture was the same, her hand on the glass, her lips shaping words I still could not hear — and I approached from behind and, with a pair of forceps I use for lichen collection, removed a fragment of material from the hem of her gown. She did not react. She did not flinch or turn. I am not certain she was aware of me. I retreated with the sample and examined it under my field lens in the lamplight of my room at the Scarden Arms.

It was peat.

Not cloth woven from plant fiber. Not a fabric that had been buried in peat and taken on its character, as bog-preserved textiles do. Peat itself, compressed and layered and formed into a material that mimicked the drape and weight of wool. The structure under magnification was identical to the peat I had taken from the moor that morning: Sphagnum moss, partially decomposed, dark brown, with filaments of heather root running through it like thread through cloth.

I did not sleep that night. In the morning I walked to Scarden farmhouse and knocked until Josiah Blacket opened the door.

---

He knew what I had come to ask. I saw it in the way he held the door — not closing it, not opening it farther, holding it at the precise angle that said: I have been expecting you, and I wish you had not come.

"The woman at your window," I said.

He looked past me, over my shoulder, at the moor. "You'd better come in."

The farmhouse interior was dark and very clean. One chair by the fire. One plate on the table. One cup. On the mantelpiece, a daguerreotype of a young woman — dark-haired, solemn, with a high collar and an expression that combined patience and ferocity in proportions I found unsettling. Beneath the daguerreotype, a name scratched into the stone of the mantel in rough letters: ANNABEL.

"My wife," Blacket said. "Dead thirty years this November."

"The woman at the window—"

"Is her. Looks like her. Sounds like her. Has her way of standing. Has her way of holding her head when she's thinking of something she wants to say but won't."

"Mr. Blacket. The figure I observed is not a ghost. It is solid. It displaces the fog. The material of its clothing is composed of compressed peat and heather fiber. I took a sample."

"You took a sample." He said it without inflection. He sat in his chair — the only chair — and I stood by the door, and between us the fire made the shadows move.

"Has she spoken to you?"

"She speaks every night. She says the things Annabel never said. The things that were between us when she died. We had quarreled — a stupid quarrel, about the dog, about whether the dog should be let in the house at night. The quarrel was about the dog and it was not about the dog. She died three days later. Fever. Quick. And the quarrel stayed unfinished."

"What does she say?"

He looked at the fire. His hands were on his knees and I saw again the black staining — deep in the creases, under the nails, along the ridges of the knuckles. Peat. Or something that stained like peat.

"She says she was wrong about the dog. She says she knew she was wrong when she said it but she couldn't stop saying it because the saying had its own force, and stopping would have been a kind of giving up, and she was not able to give up anything, not even a stupid argument. She says she is sorry. She says sorry is not the right word but there isn't a right word, only a sound that sits in the place where the right word should go, and sorry is the closest sound she has."

He wiped his eyes with the back of his hand. The peat stain smeared across his cheekbone.

"And she says other things. Private things. Things I will not repeat to a man from the Naturalist Society or any other body of men who think they are owed the insides of a marriage."

I did not press him. I asked instead about the shed.

---

There was a shed behind the farmhouse, set into the slope of the moor so that its back wall was half-buried in earth. The door was unlocked. Inside: a workbench, tools for peat-cutting, a spade, a barrow. And, on the bench, several sheets of paper covered in drawings.

The drawings were anatomical. A woman's hand, rendered in detail — the bones of the wrist, the tendons, the way the fingers curl at rest. A foot with the sole exposed, showing the arch and the distribution of weight. A rib cage, seen from the front, with annotations in a cramped hand I could not fully decipher. One word, repeated in several margins: *Annabel*.

I showed the drawings to Blacket. His face did something I have no word for — not surprise, not guilt, not recognition. Something underneath all three, like a current running beneath the surface of a river.

"I don't remember making those," he said.

"They are in your hand."

"They are in somebody's hand. I sleepwalk. Mrs. Outhwaite can tell you — when I took lodgings in the village during the repairs to the roof two winters ago, she found me in the yard at three in the morning with my arms full of heather. I was braiding it. She said I was braiding it like rope. She said I spoke to her when she woke me and what I said made no sense but I said it in Annabel's voice."

His hands were shaking. The peat stain made the tremor look worse than it was, or perhaps it made it look exactly as bad as it was.

"I do not know if I made her, Mr. Hayle. I do not know if the moor made her. I do not know if she made herself, out of everything I've put into this ground over thirty years — the words I said to the walls, the name I called into the wind when I thought no one was listening. I have dug peat from this moor every season of my life. My sweat is in that ground. My breath. Every prayer I have offered and every curse. If the land can absorb thirty years of a man's grief and give it back in the shape of his wife, then the land is not what the Naturalist Society believes it to be, and neither is grief, and neither am I."

---

Josiah Blacket died on 3 October. I found him in the morning — he had not come to the door when I knocked, and Mrs. Outhwaite, who had accompanied me with a basket of bread and butter, produced a key she claimed not to have known she possessed. He was in his chair by the fire, which had gone cold. His hands were folded in his lap. The peat staining on them was darker than I had seen it, or perhaps it only seemed so against the pallor of his skin.

He was buried in the churchyard beside Annabel. The headstones are five inches apart. I measured, because measuring is what I do and because I did not know what else to do with myself in a churchyard in October with the wind coming off the moor and the heather dying back to its winter brown.

That night I went to the farmhouse.

---

She was at the window. I saw her from the path — the shape of her against the darkened glass, her hand flat on the pane, her face close to the surface as though trying to see into a room she already knew was empty. The fog was low and thick and it wrapped around her waist and gave her the appearance of a woman standing in deep water.

I approached to within five yards. Close enough to see the composition of her skin, which was the color and texture of bog oak — dark, polished, dense. Close enough to see the heather roots that ran from her scalp down through her hair like veins. Close enough to see that her hand, pressed against the glass, left no print. Not because it was insubstantial but because the surface of her palm was peat, and peat does not leave the mark that skin leaves.

She was speaking. I could hear her now.

"The kettle needs water. I meant to tell you about the kettle. The spout has a crack and if you fill it past the rivet the water leaks onto the hob and the hob rusts and you won't notice the rust until the winter and then you'll curse the kettle and you'll mean me, because I was the one who cracked the spout, dropping it in January, do you remember January, I dropped the kettle and you laughed and the laughter was real, Josiah, that was real laughter, you forget but I don't, the laughter was before the arguing and the arguing was before the silence and the silence was before the dying and I have been trying to say this for thirty years: the laughter was first. The laughter was before everything. I need you to know that the laughter was first."

She said this to a dark window, to a cold house, to a chair with no one in it. Her voice was low and measured and carried the accent of the district in a form I associate with women who died before the railway came. It was the most articulate thing I have ever heard. It was more articulate than anything I could compose in my report, more precise than my calipers, more truthful than my field notes. The rebuilt thing spoke with an eloquence that made my own language — the language of classification, of specimen, of phylum and genus and report — seem not merely inadequate but obscene.

I stood in the heather and I listened and she spoke for nearly an hour. She spoke about the curtains she had wanted to replace and never did. She spoke about the morning she found a wren's nest in the thatch and held the eggs in her palm and felt the warmth still in them. She spoke about the argument — the dog, the money, the fear underneath both — and she said she was sorry and then she said sorry was a word for small debts and what she owed was not small and could not be paid in a word. She spoke about his hands. She said his hands were the first thing she had noticed and the last thing she had wanted and that the wanting had not stopped when she died. She said the wanting was in the ground now. She said the ground wanted him too.

At some point — I cannot determine when, because my faculty for marking time had abandoned me — she stopped. She withdrew her hand from the glass. She turned and looked at me.

Her eyes were dark. Not brown, not black — dark the way peat water is dark, with amber at the edges where the light catches. She looked at me and I understood that she knew what I was, what I carried, what my notebooks and specimens and calipers meant for her. A naturalist had found her. A naturalist would name her. And a name, in science, is the first act of possession, and possession is the prelude to the pin, the board, the glass case, the cabinet.

"Are you going to write about me?" she asked.

Her voice, when she addressed me directly, had a different quality. The monologue at the window had been intimate, unguarded, the voice of a woman speaking to a man she had known for forty years. This voice was careful. This voice was the voice of a creature assessing the instruments of its potential destruction.

"I am a naturalist," I said. "I report what I observe."

"And what do you observe?"

I looked at my notebook. I looked at the specimen in its waxed-paper envelope in my coat pocket — the scrap of peat-cloth I had taken from her hem. I looked at the drawings in the shed, which proved a human hand, or implicated one. I looked at the moor, which proved a geological process, or enacted one.

"I don't know," I said.

She nodded. She turned back to the window and placed her hand on the glass again, and I understood that the conversation was over and that the gesture — hand on glass, face close, the permanent posture of exclusion — was not a haunting. It was a vigil. She was keeping watch over an empty house because keeping watch was all she had and all she was and the watching would continue until the moor reclaimed her or a man with a report and a specimen jar decided what she was and what should be done with her.

---

I returned to my lodgings. I sat at the table in my room. The candle smoked. My notes were spread before me — thirty-two pages of observation, measurement, sketches, soil analysis, testimony. Enough for a paper that would make my reputation and unmake hers.

I picked up my pen. I put it down. I picked it up again.

What follows is the report I filed with the Northern Naturalist Society. It concerns the peat stratigraphy of the North York Moors above Scarden Vale, with particular attention to the interaction between Sphagnum deposits and Calluna root systems at elevations above eight hundred feet. It contains no mention of a woman at a window. It contains no mention of Josiah Blacket except as the occupant of a farmhouse at which I was afforded a night's shelter during inclement weather. It contains no mention of anatomical drawings, sleepwalking, or the eloquence of things that should not be able to speak.

The specimens I collected — the peat-cloth, the soil samples from her footprints, the heather threaded through with something that was not quite root and not quite hair — these I have kept. They are in a box in my study, beneath papers that have nothing to do with anything. I do not know what to do with them. I do not know if keeping them makes me her custodian or her captor.

The drawings from the shed I burned. I am aware this constitutes destruction of evidence. I am also aware that evidence, in the hands of the Society, becomes a warrant, and a warrant becomes an expedition, and an expedition becomes the thing Mary Shelley warned us about in her novel: the pursuit to the ends of the earth of something that wanted only to be left alone.

I do not know if the moors made her. I do not know if Josiah made her. I do not know if grief is a geological force or a human one, or if there is a difference, or if the difference matters. I know that she stands at the window of an empty farmhouse on a moor in Yorkshire, speaking the unsaid words of a marriage that ended thirty years ago, and that she will continue to stand there until the peat takes her back, and that my report — this report, the one I am writing now, which I have no intention of submitting to any Society — is the only record that she existed.

I have given her no name. I have assigned her no classification. I have pinned her to no board.

This is the only thing I have done in my professional life that I am certain was correct.

---

*Postscript, appended 4 March 1848: I returned to Scarden Vale in February. The farmhouse is empty. The window at which she stood is broken — not from outside but from inside, as though someone had finally pushed through the glass. The moor above is unchanged. The heather grows. The peat accumulates. I found no trace of her, but the wind across the vale that evening carried a sound I could not classify — low, sustained, shaped like speech but containing no words I could transcribe. I stood in the heather and I listened until the sound faded, and when it faded the silence was not empty but full, the way a room is full after someone has just left it. I walked back to the village in the dark, and I did not take notes, and I did not take samples, and I am not going back.*
