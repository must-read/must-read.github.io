# Story Plan: hum-parody-001

**Genre**: Humor / Satire
**Subgenre**: Parody / Pastiche
**Combination ID**: hum-parody-001
**Target Word Count**: 4,200 words (acceptable range: 3,900-4,500)
**No Risk Card**

## Formula Sources

- **Author A — P.G. Wodehouse**: Intricate comic plotting; Edwardian farce; baroque similes that perform triple axels; the comedy of incompetent aristocracy
- **Author B — Jonathan Swift**: Savage indignation masked by deadpan reasonableness; "A Modest Proposal" school of escalation; irony as moral weapon
- **Work X — *Pale Fire* by Vladimir Nabokov**: Text-and-commentary form where the commentary hijacks the original; the annotator as unreliable narrator; scholarly apparatus as comic vehicle
- **Work Y — *Don Quixote* by Miguel de Cervantes**: A character so devoted to a literary genre they mistake reality for its conventions; the comedy and pathos of living inside a story

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: P.G. Wodehouse — *My Man Jeeves* (opening)

**Source**: Author A (Wodehouse)
**Origin**: *My Man Jeeves*, Chapter 1: "Leave It to Jeeves" (1919)
**Why representative**: Demonstrates Bertie's self-deprecating narration, Wodehouse's elaborate comic comparisons, and the essential dynamic of the charming incompetent vs. the competent servant.

> Jeeves — my man, you know — is really a most extraordinary chap. So capable. Brave and resourceful — there's no knowing what he'll be up to next. ... He's like those chappies who sit peering sadly over the marble battlements at the Pennsylvania Station in the place marked "Inquiries." You know the kind. You go up to them and say: "When's the next train to Dovetail?" And they reply, without stopping to think, "Two forty-nine, Track Eleven, change at Nashville." And they're always right.
>
> I'm a bit short on brain myself; the old bean would appear to have been constructed more for ornament than for use, don't you know; but five minutes to talk the thing over with Jeeves, and I'm ready to tackle anything.

The key Wodehouse techniques for the writer to internalize:
- **Self-deprecation as comedy engine**: The narrator cheerfully admits inadequacy. "The old bean would appear to have been constructed more for ornament than for use" is the voice our annotator needs in the early footnotes — someone who laughs at themselves, whose self-awareness makes them endearing.
- **Baroque similes**: The comparison of Jeeves to Pennsylvania Station information clerks — a comparison that is at once too elaborate and perfectly exact. Our annotator should produce similes of this quality.
- **Casual authority**: Bertie speaks with absolute confidence about matters he doesn't understand. He narrates as though his perspective is the only possible one, which is precisely the technique the annotator will use — the difference being that Bertie's confidence is harmless, and the annotator's will curdle.

Additional Wodehouse voice sample — from the author meeting, this captures the target simile register:
> "Footnote 47: 'Professor Hartwell's assertion that the meter in line 14 is iambic rather than trochaic reflects, I fear, the same intellectual carelessness that led him to wear brown shoes with a blue suit at the Hilary Term reception.'"

This is the pitch. Early footnotes should read exactly like this: scholarly pettiness delivered with devastating social observation.

### Sample 2: Jonathan Swift — *A Modest Proposal* (1729)

**Source**: Author B (Swift)
**Origin**: *A Modest Proposal for Preventing the Children of Poor People from Being a Burthen to Their Parents or Country*, full text (public domain)
**Why representative**: The definitive example of Swiftian escalation — calm, reasonable tone delivering monstrous content. The tone never breaks.

> It is a melancholy object to those, who walk through this great town, or travel in the country, when they see the streets, the roads, and cabbin-doors crowded with beggars of the female sex, followed by three, four, or six children, all in rags, and importuning every passenger for an alms. ...
>
> I shall now therefore humbly propose my own thoughts, which I hope will not be liable to the least objection.
>
> I have been assured by a very knowing American of my acquaintance in London, that a young healthy child well nursed, is, at a year old, a most delicious nourishing and wholesome food, whether stewed, roasted, baked, or boiled; and I make no doubt that it will equally serve in a fricassee, or a ragout.

Key Swift techniques for the writer:
- **Deadpan economic register**: The horror is delivered in the language of a market report. "A most delicious nourishing and wholesome food" treats children as livestock with no tonal shift. Our annotator's late footnotes must achieve this: proposing monstrous things in the register of textual commentary.
- **"Humbly propose"**: The false modesty. The annotator should perform humility while committing intellectual violence.
- **The list of preparations**: "Stewed, roasted, baked, or boiled" — the specificity makes it worse. The annotator's late proposals should have this same awful specificity.
- **Structural trick**: The essay reads as reasonable for its first third. The reader is nodding along before the proposal arrives. Our piece must do the same — the early footnotes must genuinely charm before the late ones horrify.

### Sample 3: Vladimir Nabokov — *Pale Fire* (1962)

**Source**: Work X (Nabokov)
**Origin**: Foreword and Commentary sections
**Why representative**: Kinbote's voice — grandiose, self-serving, and utterly convinced of his own centrality — is the direct model for our annotator's relationship with the text.

> "Let me state that without my notes Shade's text simply has no human reality at all since the human reality of such a poem as his (being too skittish and reticent for an autobiographical work), with the omission of many pithy lines carelessly rejected by him, has to depend entirely on the reality of its author and his surroundings, attachments and so forth, a reality that only my notes can provide."
>
> "I have no desire to twist and batter an unambiguous apparatus criticus into the monstrous semblance of a novel."

Key Pale Fire techniques for the writer:
- **The parasitic commentary**: Kinbote's notes devote 232 pages to a 36-page poem. The commentary is six times the size of the text. Our piece should have a similar disproportion — a tiny, slight poem drowning in annotations.
- **Self-importance as comedy**: "Without my notes Shade's text simply has no human reality at all." Our annotator must believe this absolutely. The comedy comes from the gap between the text's actual slightness and the annotator's conviction of its profundity.
- **Denial as confession**: "I have no desire to twist and batter an unambiguous apparatus criticus into the monstrous semblance of a novel" — Kinbote denies doing exactly what he's doing. Our annotator should similarly deny, in footnotes, the very thing the footnotes are accomplishing.
- **The foreword as territory claim**: Kinbote uses the foreword to establish his authority and sideline everyone else (especially Shade's wife). Our annotator needs a prefatory note that similarly stakes a claim.

### Sample 4: Miguel de Cervantes — *Don Quixote* (1605)

**Source**: Work Y (Cervantes)
**Origin**: Chapter VIII — the windmill episode
**Why representative**: The defining scene of a character so devoted to a genre that reality bends to match its conventions.

> "Fortune is arranging matters for us better than we could have shaped our desires ourselves, for look there, friend Sancho Panza, where thirty or more monstrous giants present themselves, all of whom I mean to engage in battle and slay, and with whose spoils we shall begin to make our fortunes; for this is righteous warfare."
>
> "Look, your worship, what we see there are not giants but windmills, and what seem to be their arms are the sails that turned by the wind make the millstone go."
>
> [Don Quixote], commending himself with all his heart to his lady Dulcinea, imploring her to support him in such a peril, with lance in rest and covered by his buckler, he charged at Rocinante's fullest gallop and fell upon the first mill that stood in front of him; but as he drove his lance-point into the sail the wind whirled it round with such force that it shivered the lance to pieces, sweeping with it horse and rider, who went rolling over on the plain, in a sorry condition.
>
> "Did not I foretell unto you that you should look well what you did, for they were none other than windmills?"

Key Don Quixote techniques for the writer:
- **Sincerity as comedy**: Don Quixote doesn't pretend — he genuinely sees giants. Our annotator genuinely sees profundity in the mediocre poem. The comedy is not in deception but in sincere delusion.
- **The Sancho Panza role**: Someone nearby says "that is a windmill." In our piece, this role falls to the academic nemesis — the colleague who sees the poem clearly and is despised for it. But crucially, our annotator has NO Sancho who can reach them. The nemesis is only invoked to be dismissed.
- **Righteous warfare**: Don Quixote believes his tilting is moral. Our annotator believes the annotation project is moral — a rescue of great literature from the indifference of a philistine world. This belief is what carries them from charming eccentricity to monstrous proposals.
- **The attribution to enchantment**: When the windmill throws him, Don Quixote blames a sorcerer. Our annotator should similarly explain away any contradiction between their reading and reality by invoking conspiracy — colleagues who fail to see, institutions that suppress, a world that refuses to recognize what the poem truly contains.

## Wikipedia Concepts

### Concept Mining Results

From research into unusual Wikipedia topics, the following concepts were evaluated:

1. **Phantom islands** — Purported islands that appeared on maps but were later found not to exist, originating from navigational errors, mistaken observations, or deliberate fabrication. Cartographers copied them from map to map for centuries because "no cartographer wants to be the one to erase an island without confirmation of its nonexistence." Examples: Frisland (ubiquitous on 16th-17th century maps near Iceland), Sandy Island (appeared on maps of the Coral Sea, "undiscovered" in the 1970s), Isle Phelipeaux (used as a border landmark for the US/Canada boundary despite not existing).

2. **Ghost words** — Words published in dictionaries or authoritative reference works that had no previous meaning or use, originating from typographical errors being interpreted as unfamiliar vocabulary. The term was coined by Professor Walter William Skeat in 1886. Famous example: "dord," which appeared in the 1934 Webster's New International Dictionary after someone misread "D or d" (an abbreviation for density) as a word.

3. Museum fatigue — exhaustion experienced during extended gallery visits.
4. Nuclear pasta — theoretical degenerate matter in neutron stars.
5. The Cat Gap — period in the fossil record with mysterious absence of feline evidence.
6. Cunningham's Law — incorrect statements prompt faster corrections than direct questions.
7. The Mpemba effect — hot water sometimes freezes faster than cold water.
8. The Dancing Plague of 1518 — people danced for days uncontrollably in Strasbourg.
9. The Voynich Manuscript — undeciphered illustrated codex from the 15th century.
10. Spite houses — buildings constructed specifically to annoy neighbors.
11. The Gavle goat — structure famous for being destroyed 37 times by arson.
12. Paris Syndrome — disappointment when Paris doesn't match expectations.
13. Saudade — Portuguese emotional state of profound nostalgic longing.
14. Boston Marriage — historical cohabitation arrangement between two wealthy women.
15. Microsoft v. MikeRoweSoft — legal dispute over domain name.
16. Capitol Hill Mystery Soda Machine — unexplained vending machine.
17. Bermeja Island — longest-lived phantom island, in the Gulf of Mexico.
18. Isle Phelipeaux — phantom island used as US/Canada border landmark.
19. Frisland — phantom island ubiquitous on 16th-17th century maps.
20. Dunning-Kruger effect — cognitive bias where people with low ability overestimate competence.

### Selected Concepts: Ghost Words + Phantom Islands

**Concept 1 — Ghost Words**: The annotated poem in our piece will contain a "ghost word" — a word that doesn't exist but which the annotator treats as a rare archaism and devotes an elaborate footnote to tracing its etymology. This is the mechanism by which the commentary creates reality: the annotator's footnote gives the non-word an etymology, a history, scholarly precedent. By the end of the piece, other (invented) scholars are citing the annotator's definition. The ghost word becomes real through the weight of apparatus around it. This mirrors the annotator's broader project: if you annotate something thoroughly enough, it becomes important regardless of whether it deserved the attention.

**Integration**: The ghost word "dorse" appears in line 7 of the poem. It is, in fact, a typo — the poet meant "dorsal" or perhaps "doors." The annotator devotes Footnote 12 to a magnificent three-paragraph etymology tracing "dorse" through Old English, Middle French, and a fabricated 14th-century Flemish agricultural manual. By Footnote 31, the annotator casually references "the now-standard definition of 'dorse' (see n.12)" as though the word's existence were settled fact.

**Concept 2 — Phantom Islands**: The annotator is specifically an expert on phantom islands — islands that appeared on maps but never existed. This is their academic specialty, and it provides both their scholarly credential and their psychological portrait. A person who has devoted their career to studying things that were believed to be real but weren't is exactly the person who would find infinite depth in a mediocre poem. The phantom island specialty also provides the vehicle for the Swift-style escalation: the annotator's late footnotes begin proposing that phantom islands should be "restored" to maps — that cartographic accuracy is itself a form of violence against the human imagination — and the proposals escalate from there.

**Integration**: The annotator is Dr. Elspeth Froom, reader in Speculative Cartography at a minor university (the discipline itself being somewhat phantom). Her published monograph is titled *Frisland and Its Discontents: Toward a Restitutive Cartography*. Her academic nemesis, Dr. Pettifer, is a "positivist cartographer" who insists on mapping only things that exist — a position the annotator finds morally bankrupt. The phantom island motif threads through the footnotes: the annotator keeps finding references to non-existent places in the poem, each one validated by increasingly unhinged scholarly apparatus.

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

A scholarly critical edition of a 14-line poem by an obscure 1970s nature poet — a poem so slight it barely registers as verse — annotated by Dr. Elspeth Froom, whose 78 footnotes gradually reveal that she has constructed an entire alternative reality inside the margins. The early footnotes are charming, eccentric, and genuinely funny. The late footnotes are calm, reasonable, and quietly horrifying. The tone never changes. The reader laughs at the same things throughout, but the laughter changes meaning underneath them.

### 2. Protagonist: Dr. Elspeth Froom

- **Name/situation**: Dr. Elspeth Froom, 61, reader in Speculative Cartography at the University of South Axminster (a university so minor that even its faculty sometimes forget which building they're in). She has published one monograph (*Frisland and Its Discontents*), which was reviewed in a single journal by a single reviewer (Dr. Pettifer), unfavorably. She has devoted the last eleven years to this critical edition.
- **What she WANTS**: To produce the definitive scholarly edition of Humphrey Tench's poem "Midsummer, Looking North" — to rescue it from obscurity and prove that it is one of the great unrecognized works of English verse.
- **What she NEEDS**: Connection. Human contact. She has been alone with this poem for so long that the poem has become her only relationship. The footnotes are not scholarship — they are letters to a world that stopped answering.
- **Her flaw**: She cannot distinguish between loving something and consuming it. She loves the poem the way Don Quixote loves chivalric romance — with a devotion so total that it has destroyed her ability to see anything else clearly. Her love has become indistinguishable from control. She "rescues" the poem the way a kidnapper "rescues" a child from neglectful parents.

### 3. Structure

The piece is presented as an excerpt from *A Critical Edition of "Midsummer, Looking North" by Humphrey Tench, with Introduction, Commentary, and Apparatus Criticus, Edited by Dr. Elspeth Froom*. The reader receives:

1. **A brief Editor's Note** (200 words) — Froom stakes her territorial claim. She explains that the poem has been "grievously neglected" and that her edition restores it to its "proper station." She mentions that a rival edition by Dr. Pettifer was "mercifully abandoned." The tone is Kinbote: grandiose, assured, slightly wounded. The note also mentions, in passing, that the edition was completed "during a period of enforced solitude which, if anything, sharpened the critical faculties."

2. **The poem itself** (14 lines) — "Midsummer, Looking North" by Humphrey Tench (1923-1981). A mediocre nature poem about light on water. It is competent but unremarkable — the kind of poem that appears in a regional anthology and is never reprinted. Superscript numbers litter every line, sometimes two or three per line. The poem is nearly unreadable under the weight of its annotations, like a patient buried under monitoring equipment.

3. **The footnotes** (the bulk of the piece, ~3,400 words) — Numbered 1 through approximately 40-45 (not all are shown; the piece presents "selected annotations" from a larger work, with editorial ellipses indicating omissions). The footnotes are the story. They escalate through four phases:

   **Phase 1 — The Charming Eccentric (Footnotes 1-12)**
   The opening footnotes are pure Wodehouse comedy. Froom is delightful: she traces the word "shimmer" through seven centuries of usage with infectious enthusiasm. She mentions her cat (Ptolemy) who sits on the manuscript. She drops asides about Dr. Pettifer that are exquisitely petty — his pronunciation of "littoral," his failure to return a borrowed book in 1997, his regrettable mustache. She invents the ghost word "dorse" and gives it a magnificent false etymology. The reader is charmed. This is a harmless, lovable eccentric.

   **Phase 2 — The Wobble (Footnotes 13-22)**
   The annotations begin to strain. Froom starts finding references to phantom islands in the poem — a mention of "the north" becomes a discussion of Frisland, the phantom island that appeared on maps for two centuries. She cross-references Tench's poetry with her own monograph extensively. She begins to argue that the poem is "encrypted" and that its true subject is the relationship between cartographic representation and ontological reality. The comedy is still present but the reader starts to notice that Froom is seeing things in the poem that simply aren't there. She insists that "gull" in line 9 refers not to the bird but to the verb "to gull" (to deceive), and therefore the entire poem is about the deception of maps. A footnote about Dr. Pettifer reveals that he once called her work "a comprehensive misunderstanding of both cartography and poetry," and Froom's response — calm, precise, four sentences — contains something that isn't quite humor anymore.

   **Phase 3 — The Modest Proposals (Footnotes 23-35)**
   The annotations cross a line the reader can't quite identify. Froom begins proposing things. She argues, in a footnote about the word "tide," that cartographic positivism — the insistence on mapping only what exists — is a form of "intellectual violence against the territories of the mind." She proposes, calmly, that phantom islands should be restored to official maps as a corrective. She proposes that universities should fund chairs in Speculative Cartography (she would, of course, be willing to serve). She proposes that Dr. Pettifer's "positivist cartography" be "defunded and its practitioners redirected toward more productive fields." The tone is *A Modest Proposal* exactly: reasonable, measured, logically structured, and getting worse. A footnote about the word "stone" becomes a proposal for reforming the entire discipline of literary criticism along lines that would, if implemented, require the firing of approximately 80% of English department faculty. She includes cost estimates.

   **Phase 4 — The Consumed Text (Footnotes 36-45)**
   The final footnotes have lost track of the poem entirely. Froom is annotating her own earlier footnotes. A footnote references Footnote 12 (the ghost word) as established scholarly fact. A footnote about Humphrey Tench's biography reveals that Froom has been writing letters to the dead poet and is confused about why he hasn't responded — this detail is mentioned in passing, in a subordinate clause, as though it were the most natural thing in the world. The last visible footnote proposes, with serene specificity, that a small island in the North Sea — currently uninhabited — be renamed "Tenchland" and designated as a "living monument to the poem." She has drawn up a petition. She includes the proposed coordinates. She mentions that Ptolemy the cat has been "consulted on the matter and raises no objection." The piece ends mid-annotation — a footnote that begins a sentence about what should be done with scholars who refuse to recognize the poem's greatness, and then the text simply stops with an editorial note: "[The remaining annotations, comprising Footnotes 46-78, will appear in Volume II, forthcoming.]"

   **The ending does NOT resolve.** We do not learn what happens to Froom. We do not learn whether the edition is published. We are simply left in the middle of a footnote that is going somewhere we'd rather not follow, with the promise of 33 more annotations to come.

### 4. Key Scenes/Annotations

1. **Footnote 3 (the mustache footnote)**: Pure Wodehouse. A footnote about Tench's use of "light" pivots into a three-sentence digression about Dr. Pettifer's mustache ("a facial accoutrement which, I have been given to understand, was cultivated during a sabbatical in Innsbruck and which, if anything, has proven more durable than his scholarship"). This establishes the comic register and the Pettifer rivalry. The reader laughs and trusts the narrator.

2. **Footnote 12 (the ghost word "dorse")**: The annotator encounters the word "dorse" in line 7 and produces a magnificent false etymology spanning Old English, Middle French, and a fabricated Flemish agricultural manual from 1347. The footnote is confident, detailed, and entirely invented. It is both a joke about scholarly overreach and the first sign that Froom is building reality from footnotes. This is the hinge between Wodehouse and Nabokov — charming fantasy with a structural function.

3. **Footnote 19 (Frisland in the poem)**: The annotator argues that Tench's phrase "looking north" is a direct reference to the phantom island of Frisland, which "occupied the cartographic imagination for two centuries despite the trifling inconvenience of not existing." She writes: "If an island can persist on maps for two hundred years through the sheer unwillingness of cartographers to admit error, then a poem can surely persist in the canon through the devotion of a single attentive reader." This is the Quixote moment — the explicit articulation of her worldview. She will love this poem into importance the way mapmakers loved Frisland into existence.

4. **Footnote 28 (the defunding proposal)**: A footnote about the word "current" becomes a calmly reasoned argument that Dr. Pettifer's entire field of study should be defunded. Froom calculates the savings (she has spreadsheets). She proposes redirecting the funds to "a permanent endowment for the study of literature that matters." The tone is identical to Footnote 3. The reader realizes with discomfort that the voice they found charming is the same voice now proposing institutional destruction.

5. **Footnote 42 (the letters to Tench)**: In a subordinate clause of a footnote about line 12's use of "dusk," Froom mentions that she has been "corresponding with Tench on the matter of the semicolon in line 8, though his responses have been, as of this writing, delayed." Tench died in 1981. This detail is mentioned without emphasis, without drama, as one would mention a colleague being slow to reply to email. It is the saddest and funniest moment in the piece — the point at which eccentricity has become something else entirely, and the reader realizes that the footnotes are not scholarship but a kind of desperate, one-sided conversation with someone who cannot hear.

### 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning**: Delight. The reader is laughing. Froom is wonderful — a comic creation in the Wodehouse tradition, all charm and elaborate pettiness. The reader wants more footnotes.

**Middle**: Unease. The laughter continues but something has shifted. The reader is still laughing at the same kinds of things — elaborate cross-references, scholarly grudges, flights of etymological fancy — but the ground beneath the humor is tilting. Each footnote that should be funny is also, somehow, not quite funny.

**Late middle**: The feeling of reading *A Modest Proposal* for the first time — the slow recognition that the reasonable voice you've been listening to has been leading you somewhere you didn't agree to go. The proposals are logical within Froom's framework. That's what makes them disturbing.

**End**: The reader feels the specific vertigo of a text that stops instead of ending. The promise of Volume II — 33 more annotations — is not a joke. It is a threat. The reader puts the piece down and feels, for a moment, the way you feel when you realize a person you thought was eccentric is actually alone, and the eccentricity was the loneliness all along, and you were laughing at it.

The reader should NOT feel "quietly devastated." The reader should feel the way you feel when you laugh at something and then realize the thing you laughed at is looking back at you.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Wodehouse (Author A)** — Most visible in: Footnotes 1-12, especially the Pettifer asides. The elaborate similes, the social comedy, the narrator who is charming precisely because they are oblivious. The register of the early footnotes should be indistinguishable from a Wodehouse narrator — someone for whom the world is essentially comic and whose prose performs acrobatic feats of analogy. Also: the annotator's relationship with their cat, Ptolemy, is pure Wodehouse — the minor domestic detail that humanizes and delights.

**Swift (Author B)** — Most visible in: Footnotes 23-35, the "modest proposals" section. The defunding proposal, the cartographic restoration program, the cost estimates. The key is that the tone does not change. The voice that described Pettifer's mustache is the same voice proposing his termination. This is the Swiftian trick: the reader is the one who has to notice the shift, because the text refuses to signal it. Also: the calm inclusion of specifics (coordinates, cost estimates, petition language) echoes Swift's "whether stewed, roasted, baked, or boiled."

**Pale Fire / Nabokov (Work X)** — The entire formal structure. The piece IS a Pale Fire: a text + commentary where the commentary devours the text. Specific echoes: the Editor's Note mirrors Kinbote's Foreword (territorial claim, dismissal of rivals). The disproportion between poem and annotation mirrors the 36-page poem vs. 232-page commentary. The annotator's conviction that "without my notes the poem has no human reality" is a direct Kinbote move. The ghost word "dorse" being treated as established fact in later footnotes mirrors how Kinbote's Zembla becomes "real" through the weight of its own footnotes.

**Don Quixote / Cervantes (Work Y)** — The annotator IS a Don Quixote figure. Her devotion to the poem is Don Quixote's devotion to chivalric romance — total, sincere, and completely delusional. The phantom islands provide the specific Quixote mechanism: she sees Frisland in every line, the way Don Quixote sees giants in every windmill. Footnote 19's explicit comparison between phantom islands and literary canonization is the philosophical core of the Quixote element. And crucially: there is no Sancho Panza who can reach her. Pettifer is Sancho — "that is a windmill" — but he is only ever quoted in order to be dismissed. She has expelled the corrective voice from her reality.

### 7. Title

**"Froom's Frisland"**

Rationale: Does not start with "The." Uses a possessive structure (name + place) that is distinctive among the existing titles. "Froom" is the annotator's name; "Frisland" is the phantom island. The title works on multiple levels: it names the annotator's relationship to the phantom island she studies, but also names the phantom reality she has constructed inside the footnotes — a place that exists only because she insists it does, mapped in annotations instead of cartography. A reader encountering the title cold would be curious. A reader finishing the piece would find it devastating.

Alternative titles considered: "Selected Annotations from a Critical Edition," "Dorse and Its Discontents," "Phantom Cartography," "Love Letters to a Dead Poet."

## Technical Notes for the Writer

### Formatting
The piece requires careful visual formatting:
- The Editor's Note should be in standard prose, italicized or set off
- The poem should appear as centered verse with superscript footnote numbers
- The footnotes should be numbered and set as standard academic footnotes
- Editorial ellipses (e.g., "[Footnotes 13-17 omitted]") should appear between shown footnotes to indicate the "selected" nature of the presentation
- The final footnote should end mid-sentence, followed by the editorial note about Volume II

### The Poem
"Midsummer, Looking North" should be:
- 14 lines (a loose sonnet, though Froom insists it's actually a "modified Sapphic stanza," which it isn't)
- About light on water, a gull, some stones, the word "north"
- Competent but unremarkable — the kind of poem that earns a C+ in an MFA workshop
- It MUST contain: the word "dorse" (the ghost word, actually a typo), the word "gull," a reference to "looking north," a semicolon in line 8
- The poem should be good enough that a generous reader could see why someone might love it, but not so good that Froom's devotion seems justified

### Tone Management
The single most important technical challenge: the annotator's voice must remain CONSTANT from first footnote to last. The escalation happens in content, never in tone. If the early footnotes sound like a charming eccentric and the late footnotes sound like a dangerous obsessive, the piece has failed. They must sound the same. The reader must be the one who changes, not the text.
