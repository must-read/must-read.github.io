It is a truth universally acknowledged that a village in possession of a vacant living must be in want of a curate, and the village of Hoxham-in-the-Weald had been in want of one for so long that the absence had become, in the way of these things, a kind of presence. Mrs. Flood, who operated the informal intelligence service that passed for village life, had extracted more conversational value from the empty rectory than most women could have managed from a full one. She had opinions about what sort of curate should fill it, opinions about what sort should not, and opinions about the opinions of others on the subject, which formed a tertiary layer of commentary that could, on a slow Tuesday, sustain a full afternoon's visiting.

When the Reverend Mr. Calloway arrived in September, therefore, the village faced a crisis of surplus. Here was an actual curate, occupying actual space, and Mrs. Flood was forced to pivot from speculation to observation, which is rather like asking a general to stop planning campaigns and start doing the washing up.

He was tall. He was dark. He had ten thousand a year — a figure that traveled through Hoxham faster than influenza and with more lasting effects on the constitution. He read his sermons in a voice that made the Nicene Creed sound like something you might actually wish to believe. He called upon the sick with broth. He remembered children's names, including the difficult ones, including the ones that had been changed twice. He did everything, in fact, that a curate should do, with such exactness that the performance had a quality of completeness normally reserved for things that have been rehearsed.

"Nobody's that good at being a curate," said Prudence Bottlesworth, who ran the post office and also, by general if unspoken consensus, the village. "Not even curates."

Prudence was the eldest of the three women whom Hoxham called, with a fondness that was nine-tenths wariness, the Odd Lot. She was sixty-three, starched as a communion cloth, and had a moral sense so acute it had become, in practice, indistinguishable from malice. She could detect a lie the way other women detected a draft — by the sensation of something cold passing through a room where it had no business being.

Agnes Mudd, who occupied the middle position in this unelected tribunal, was a widow of independent means and dependent appetites. She grew cabbages the size of ottomans and had buried two husbands with an efficiency that suggested she viewed matrimony as a horticultural problem: plant, tend, harvest, compost. She could set a bone, deliver a calf, and remove a wart by means she declined to specify. Her cottage smelled of sage and something under the sage that made visitors sneeze in a way they found difficult to describe afterward.

Dorcas Pennywhistle, the youngest at twenty-four, was in love with love in the manner of a naturalist who has read extensively about tigers but never actually met one. She wrote poetry. It did not scan. She believed fiercely in the spiritual improvement of mankind and kept a diary in which the word "sensibility" appeared, on average, four times per page. She was, in the taxonomy of the Odd Lot, the romantic, which meant she was the one who believed things could change, a conviction the other two regarded with the tender pity normally reserved for the terminally confused.

It was Prudence who first said it, three Sundays into the curate's tenure, while the three of them sat in Agnes's kitchen drinking tea that was technically tea in the same way that a river is technically water — the basic substance was present, but Agnes had added things to it that made the eventual product something for which the East India Company could not reasonably be held responsible.

"He's a demon," she said.

Agnes, who was shelling peas, did not look up. "What sort?"

"Does it matter what sort?"

"It matters enormously what sort. There's demons of avarice, demons of sloth, demons of lust — though that one's rarer in curates than you'd think — demons of petty bureaucratic malice, which is actually more common in curates than you'd—"

"He's *wrong*, Agnes. I can feel it in my knees."

"Your knees predicted rain last Thursday and we got nothing but sun."

"My knees," said Prudence, with the dignity of a woman whose knees had once correctly identified the Henshaw boy's fraudulent limp six weeks before the doctor, "are not the point. I watched him shake hands with every parishioner after Sunday service. Every single one. And he *enjoyed* it."

Agnes conceded that this was suspicious.

Dorcas, who had been writing in her diary, looked up with an expression of delighted alarm, which is the natural expression of a young person who has just learned that the world contains a genuine metaphysical crisis and it is happening within walking distance. "A demon," she breathed. "An actual—"

"Don't romanticize it," said Prudence.

"I wasn't—"

"You were composing a sonnet. I can tell. Your lips move."

---

The difficulty, as the Odd Lot quickly discovered, was not in identifying the problem but in persuading anyone else to admit it existed.

Mrs. Flood, whose opinion shaped Hoxham's social weather the way the Gulf Stream shapes Britain's actual weather — invisibly, persistently, and with consequences nobody could quite trace to their source — was *enchanted* by the curate, a word Prudence found uncomfortably precise.

"Such manners," Mrs. Flood reported to anyone stationary enough to receive intelligence. "Such *address*. He complimented my curtains. He said they had a quality of repose."

"Curtains don't have qualities," said Prudence.

"My curtains do."

There was no arguing with this. Mrs. Flood's curtains were, like Mrs. Flood herself, a settled fact of Hoxham life that predated rational inquiry and would certainly outlast it.

Mr. Beamish, the churchwarden, was similarly immovable, though he expressed his immovability in the fiscal rather than the aesthetic register. The curate had increased Sunday attendance by forty percent. The curate had organized a whist drive that raised eleven pounds for the roof fund. The curate had suggested — and this was the fact that concerned the Odd Lot most — amateur theatricals.

"Amateur theatricals," said Agnes, in the tone of a woman who has just heard the first low rumble before the avalanche.

"For Christmas. He proposes to stage a play in the parish hall. Something seasonal. Something *uplifting*."

"I'll bet he does," said Prudence.

But amateur theatricals, in a village like Hoxham, were not merely entertainment. They were the mechanism by which the village talked to itself about itself — the one occasion on which a farmer's wife could shout at the squire's daughter and call it *art*. They were the single licensed arena in which the village's actual feelings, normally compressed beneath seventeen layers of manners and thirty generations of knowing better, could escape under the protective fiction that someone else had written them down first.

And now the curate proposed to direct.

---

Prudence undertook her investigation with the systematic rigor of a woman who has spent forty years sorting post and knows that the truth about a person can be found in what they order, how they address their envelopes, and whether they tip at Christmas.

The curate ordered no personal correspondence. None. Not a letter, not a parcel, not a periodical. In Prudence's experience, the only people who received no post were people who had not existed prior to arriving.

She shared this finding with Agnes over a cabbage that Agnes was attacking with a knife in a way that suggested the cabbage had wronged her in a previous life.

"No post proves nothing," said Agnes. "It's also consistent with a private man who collects his own from the town. Which is consistent with a reserved nature. Which is consistent with being a curate."

"You're arguing his case."

"I'm arguing with your evidence. Those are different things, and if you can't tell them apart, it explains a good deal about your post office accounts."

Prudence let this pass, because Agnes's insults, like Agnes's scones, had a density that rewarded patience more than confrontation. "I also checked the diocesan register. There is no record of a Reverend Calloway. Not in this diocese. Not in any adjacent diocese. Not *anywhere*."

Agnes paused in her cabbage work. This, Prudence noted, was the equivalent, in Agnes, of a lesser woman fainting.

"Well," Agnes said at last. "That's a different cabbage."

"Yes. It is."

"But Prudence — even if he *is* what you say he is, what do you propose we do about it? Go to Mrs. Flood and say, 'Your curate is a creature of darkness, please adjust the seating chart'? She'll have us committed by Thursday."

"We have to try."

"We tried with the Henshaw boy and his fraudulent limp. They didn't believe us about that either, and he was only pretending to be injured, not pretending to be *human*."

---

They tried. They tried in the way that people try things in villages, which is to say obliquely, through implication, over scones, at the margins of conversations about other things entirely, because in Hoxham the direct statement of a direct opinion was considered roughly as socially acceptable as removing one's clothes in church — technically possible, but ruinous to one's standing and unlikely to improve the ventilation.

Prudence mentioned to Mrs. Flood that the curate had no references from his previous parish. Mrs. Flood said this merely proved his humility. Prudence pointed out that his previous parish appeared not to exist. Mrs. Flood said there were many small parishes and one could not be expected to keep track of all of them, particularly as the Church of England was, in Mrs. Flood's opinion, rather too large and would benefit from some pruning, a remark that landed so far from the original subject that Prudence found herself arguing about ecclesiastical governance and wondering how she had got there, which was, she reflected later, probably the point.

Agnes took a more direct approach. She invited the curate to tea and served him a scone made with certain additions. A scone of this particular recipe, in Agnes's experience, caused truthfulness in the way that a bucket of cold water caused wakefulness: suddenly, comprehensively, and with some sputtering. She had once given one to a horse and it had confessed, through a series of unmistakable gestures, to three counts of deliberate biting.

The curate ate three. He complimented the texture. He asked for the recipe. He told her, with apparent sincerity, that he had never tasted a finer scone, and then he told her, with equal apparent sincerity, that he was grateful for her welcome and hoped to be of service to the parish for many years to come.

"Either he's telling the truth," Agnes reported afterward, "or the scones don't work on him. And the scones work on *everything*."

Dorcas, inevitably, tried poetry. She left a sonnet on the curate's doorstep — fourteen lines of iambic pentameter in which a creature that could only be seen truly by moonlight served as the conceit, and into the final couplet she had worked a small... well, it wasn't a *spell* exactly, more of an *intention*... the sort of thing that might, under the right atmospheric conditions, with a following wind, reveal a person's true nature, assuming they had one, which was rather the question at hand.

The curate returned the poem the following Sunday with a note complimenting her enjambment and gently correcting her scansion. The corrections were, Dorcas admitted with something close to despair, accurate.

"He fixed my weak ending," she said.

"That's not evidence of demonhood," said Agnes.

"It might be," said Prudence. "What sort of curate scans poetry for hidden intentions and returns it with notes?"

"A literate one," said Agnes.

"Or one who recognized what was being attempted and wanted us to know he recognized it."

There was a pause in which each of them considered this possibility and found it, for different reasons, very cold.

---

The theatricals arrived in December like a siege — slowly at first, then all at once, until every conversation in Hoxham had been requisitioned for the war effort.

The curate had written the play himself. It was called *A Winter's Reckoning*, and it concerned a village that discovers a stranger in its midst and must decide whether to welcome him or cast him out. The village, naturally, chose welcome. The stranger, naturally, rewarded their faith. The whole thing lasted forty minutes, required eleven actors and one sheep, and was the most perfectly constructed argument for the curate's own acceptance that could possibly have been devised, and it had been devised by the curate, which was either audacious or innocent, and Prudence could not determine which because the two options produced identical behavior.

A guilty man performing innocence and an innocent man being innocent looked, sounded, and smelled exactly the same. This, she suspected, was not a flaw in her reasoning but a feature of the universe, and she resented it accordingly.

Rehearsals consumed the village for three weeks. They met in the parish hall on Tuesday and Thursday evenings, and the curate ran them with a gentleness that was somehow more commanding than authority. He never raised his voice. He never insisted. He merely suggested, in a tone that made the suggestion sound like something you had been about to think of yourself, and people found themselves doing things they had not planned to do — Mrs. Flood emoting, Mr. Beamish attempting comedy, the Henshaw boy memorizing lines — as though the curate had not directed them at all but merely arranged the room so that these things became inevitable.

Prudence attended every rehearsal. She told herself she was watching for evidence. What she was actually doing was watching the curate watch other people, which was the most unsettling thing she had ever witnessed, because he watched them the way a very good clockmaker watches clocks — with admiration for the mechanism, and no confusion about the difference between himself and the thing he had wound.

Or so she thought. Or so she wanted to think. She was no longer certain which.

The entire village was involved in the performance. Mrs. Flood played the village matriarch with a conviction that drew on deep reserves of personal experience. Mr. Beamish, who had never acted in his life, discovered in himself a gift for comic timing that startled everyone, himself most of all. Even the Henshaw boy — whose limp had mysteriously recurred for the occasion — played a shepherd.

The curate directed from the wings. He was extraordinary. He knew exactly when to let a silence breathe. He knew exactly when to cut a line that wasn't working. He coaxed a performance out of Mrs. Flood that made her, for seventeen minutes on a Wednesday evening in a parish hall that smelled of damp wool and floor polish, something other than Mrs. Flood — something larger and more frightened and briefly, flickeringly, honest.

Prudence watched from the back row. She watched Mrs. Flood become someone else. She watched Mr. Beamish find something about himself he had not known was there. She watched the village — her village, the one she ran, the one she sorted and stamped and delivered six days a week — perform itself, and in performing itself become something it had not been before, and she watched the curate watch all of this with an expression she could not read because it contained either satisfaction or hunger and the two looked, from the back row of a parish hall, exactly alike.

The play was a success. Everyone said so. Nobody mentioned the smell — faintly sulfurous, just at the edge of perception — that lingered after the footlights were doused. Agnes attributed it to Mr. Beamish's pipe. Dorcas attributed it to *atmosphere*, which was her word for things she could feel but not prove. Prudence attributed it to nothing, because to attribute it to anything would be to admit evidence that the village had already decided not to receive.

---

After the theatricals, the curate became part of Hoxham the way a river becomes part of a landscape: not by fitting into the existing contours but by wearing new ones, slowly, until the new shape felt like it had always been there and nobody could remember what the valley had looked like before.

Prudence found herself consulting him about the post office accounts. Agnes found herself sending him cabbages. Dorcas found herself editing her poetry based on his suggestions, and the poetry was *improving*, which seemed like exactly the sort of bargain that ought to concern a person, though Dorcas seemed more concerned about her scansion than her soul, which might, Prudence reflected, be the same thing.

"We're being managed," Prudence said, one gray February afternoon in Agnes's kitchen, where the tea was particularly assertive and the cabbages cast long shadows in the low winter light.

"He's making himself necessary."

"He *is* necessary," said Agnes. "The roof fund is solvent for the first time since the dissolution of the monasteries. Mrs. Flood has stopped telling people about her surgery. The Henshaw boy is reading actual books."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange for what? He's a curate. Curates do things for parishes. That is the function of a curate."

"He's not a curate."

"He is performing the function of a curate. In a village, Prudence, what is the difference?"

This was, Prudence felt, precisely the sort of argument that someone who had been got at would make, except that Agnes had not been got at, not really, not in any way that could be identified and confronted. Agnes had simply done what Agnes always did, which was to assess the practical situation and conclude that a useful arrangement was preferable to a righteous void. Agnes composted husbands. Agnes was not sentimental about categories.

"You think I'm wrong," said Prudence.

"I think you're right," said Agnes. "I just don't think it matters."

Dorcas, who had been quiet for some time — an unusual state for Dorcas, who treated silence the way most people treated holding their breath, as something to be endured briefly and then abandoned with relief — spoke up from behind her diary.

"What if he's doing good?" she said.

"Good?"

"Actual good. Whatever he is. What if the good is real? Mrs. Dawkins's arthritis has improved. The Henshaw boy can read. The roof doesn't leak anymore. Does it matter *why* those things happened?"

It was, Prudence thought, a genuinely terrible question, because it had a genuinely terrible answer, which was: probably not. Probably the universe did not care about the provenance of kindness any more than a hungry person cared about the provenance of bread. You ate what was offered. You were grateful. You did not ask the baker what he was.

"Yes," she said. "It matters."

But she said it the way people say things they need to be true, which is not quite the same as the way they say things they know are true, and all three of them heard the difference.

---

Spring came. The curate organized an Easter fete. He judged the flower competition with such diplomacy that not a single entrant felt slighted, which was the first time in living memory that the competition had concluded without at least one tearful accusation of bias. He visited the old. He baptized the new. He sat in rooms where people were dying and held their hands and said things that made the dying less frightening, and Prudence had to admit that whatever he was, he was better at comfort than most people who had legitimate claim to the skill.

She still felt it, though. Not every day. Some days the wrongness receded and she thought perhaps she was a suspicious woman growing old in a post office, constructing theories from loneliness and pattern recognition. Other days it came back sharp as a splinter, and she would catch the curate smiling at someone and see in that smile the precision of a thing that had studied smiling the way a painter studies light — not from the inside but from a great and clinical distance.

One evening in May she went to the rectory. She had not planned to go. She went because her feet took her there, or because the evening was warm, or because forty years of sorting other people's lives had left her with an acute and unmet need to sort her own.

The curate opened the door and smiled, and his smile was — what? Perfect. Complete. A smile with no seams, no rough edges, no place where the human mechanics of it showed through.

Or a smile. Just a smile. From a curate who was pleased to see a parishioner.

"Mrs. Bottlesworth. Do come in. I've just made seed cake."

She went in. She ate the cake. It was exceptional. She ate it in the curate's sitting room, which was furnished with exactly the degree of modesty appropriate to a man of the cloth who nonetheless had ten thousand a year, and she looked for something wrong — a book that shouldn't be there, a shadow that fell at the wrong angle, a fire that burned without apparent fuel — and found only a room that was precisely, impeccably, exhaustively *correct*.

"You know," she said, after a silence that the curate did not rush to fill, which was either the patience of a good listener or the patience of something that had all the time in the world, "I've never been able to work out whether you're very good or very bad."

"In my experience," said the curate, pouring tea with hands that were steady and clean and exactly the right temperature, "the distinction matters less than people suppose."

Prudence went home. She did not tell Agnes or Dorcas about the conversation. She went home and she sat in her kitchen and she drank her own tea, which was merely tea, and she listened to the village settling around her like a house adjusting to a new foundation — creaking here, shifting there, holding.

In the morning the curate called on the sick with broth, and Mrs. Flood said his manners were impeccable, and the Henshaw boy read a chapter of *Ivanhoe* unprompted, and the roof fund stood at eleven pounds four shillings and sixpence, and everything in Hoxham-in-the-Weald was fine, which is not the same as everything being right, but the difference had never been the sort of thing that paid the bills.
