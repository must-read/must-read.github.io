## Foreword to the Critical Edition

What follows is drawn from *The Dwellings of Herzen Raab: A Critical Companion to the Only Novel of Dariusz Szeliga*, by Dr. Lazlo Venn, formerly Regius Fellow in Comparative Literature at the University of Kraków (1988–1996), Senior Lecturer in Transatlantic Modernisms at the University of Ghent (1997–2003), and, since 2004, independent scholar. The critical companion — 412 pages, with indices, concordance, bibliography, and three appendices — was completed over seventeen years and submitted to Archipelago University Press in the spring of 2024. It was rejected on the grounds that the press could not verify the existence of the novel it accompanied. Dr. Venn's response, included here as Appendix A, runs to forty-one pages.

The edition is organized as a series of annotations on the text of *The Dwellings of Herzen Raab*, a novel of approximately 80,000 words written in Polish between 1957 and 1961 by Dariusz Szeliga (1921–1963), published in a limited run of 200 copies by Oficyna Wydawnicza Pożar, Łódź, in 1962, and never reprinted. No copies of the original edition are known to survive. The text as quoted in Dr. Venn's companion is drawn from his own transcription, completed from memory between 2009 and 2013, after the last known copy — which had been in his private collection — was destroyed in a flood.

What follows is a selection of annotations. Numbering corresponds to the full edition.

## Selected Annotations

**1.** The novel opens with its protagonist, Herzen Raab, standing in the doorway of a building he does not recognize, in a city he cannot name, holding a key that fits no lock he has tried. This image — a man at a threshold, equipped with the wrong instrument — is, I would argue, among the most perfectly compressed openings in the European novel. Szeliga gives us in a single sentence the entire human predicament: we arrive at doors; we carry keys; the keys are always for somewhere else.

I first encountered this passage in 1991, in a secondhand bookshop on Floriańska Street, Kraków, where the proprietor — a woman of about seventy whose name I never learned and whose face I remember with a clarity that borders on hallucination — had placed the novel in the wrong section. It was shelved under Travel. This seemed, in retrospect, appropriate.

**4.** Szeliga's prose, even in translation, possesses what I can only describe as a moral beauty. Consider the passage in which Raab visits the municipal registry to determine his own address:

> He filled in the form with the care of a man defusing a bomb, each letter placed as though a slight miscalculation would detonate the entire alphabet. When the clerk asked for his date of birth, Raab provided three, all of them plausible, none of them his. "A man should have options," he said. The clerk, who had been working at the registry for twenty-seven years, looked at Raab the way a mathematician looks at a number that has no business existing, and stamped the form twice — once for the record, once out of exasperation.

The passage rewards analysis. "Each letter placed as though a slight miscalculation would detonate the entire alphabet" — this is not merely comic hyperbole; it is an epistemological statement. For Raab, identity is volatile. Names are explosive. The clerk, the representative of the bureaucratic order, responds with the only weapon available to him: redundant stamping. The second stamp is a masterpiece of institutional rage.

I showed this passage to Professor Alina Wieczorek of the Jagiellonian University in 1994, hoping for collegial excitement. She asked whether I could lend her the novel. I said the binding was fragile. She asked whether I could photocopy the relevant pages. I said the ink was a type that degraded under bright light. She asked whether she could visit my office to read it. I said the office was being renovated. She asked, finally, whether the passage about the clerk had been in the original Polish or whether I had composed it myself, and I told her that was the sort of question only someone who had never loved a book would think to ask. I regret this. I do not regret the evasions, though I note that Professor Wieczorek subsequently published a monograph on Schulz without citing Szeliga once, which suggests her interest was less than genuine.

**6.** A brief note on Szeliga's literary reputation, or rather, on its absence. Szeliga is not mentioned in Czesław Miłosz's *The History of Polish Literature*. He is not mentioned in the *Słownik pisarzy polskich*. He is not mentioned in the proceedings of any conference, the index of any anthology, the bibliography of any dissertation, the footnotes of any review. His name appears, so far as I have been able to determine, in exactly one published source: my own critical companion.

This fact has been presented to me, by Professor Wieczorek and others, as though it were an argument. I find it rather closer to a confession. If an entire discipline can overlook a writer of Szeliga's caliber for sixty years, the failure belongs not to the writer but to the discipline. Literary history is not a census; it is a competition for attention, and the judges are frequently asleep. Kafka died unpublished. Pessoa spent his life in a trunk. Emily Dickinson was discovered in a bureau drawer. That Szeliga was discovered in a secondhand bookshop on Floriańska Street, by a man who happened to be looking in the wrong section, is not an anomaly. It is tradition.

I should also note — because the record requires it, and because I am constitutionally incapable of leaving a record incomplete — that the proprietor of the bookshop on Floriańska Street has not been located. I attempted to return to the shop in 1998 and found, in its place, a store selling mobile phone accessories. The woman's face remains, as I have said, more vivid than many faces I encounter in daily life, which may say something about the daily life: the particular architecture of her cheekbones, the reading glasses pushed onto her forehead, the manner in which she accepted my zlotys as though doing me a favor rather than conducting a transaction. Whether the vividness of this memory supports or undermines its reliability is a question I have posed in a paper that Professor Wieczorek declined to read.

**7.** The city in which Raab's novel takes place is never named directly. Szeliga refers to it only as "the city with two rivers and one opinion." Scholars — by which I mean myself, there being no others — have debated whether this is Kraków, Łódź, Wrocław, or a composite. I have settled, after some years of consideration, on the view that the city is fictional but that its fictionality is irrelevant, in the same way that the fictionality of Zembla does not prevent it from being the most vivid country in twentieth-century literature. A city does not need to exist to be inhabitable. It needs only a good enough map.

I have drawn several such maps. They are pinned to the wall above my desk, next to the chronology of Raab's movements through the novel (color-coded by chapter), the genealogical chart of the Raab family (reconstructed from scattered textual references), and the street plan of the unnamed city, which I have assembled over fourteen years from architectural descriptions in the novel's middle chapters. The street plan is, I believe, internally consistent. I tested this by walking the routes Raab describes, using the distances implied by his walking times and his comments on the light, and found no contradictions. I conducted these walks in my apartment, using a tape measure and masking tape on the floor to mark the streets. The apartment is not large. Some of the routes require walking the same corridor multiple times. My downstairs neighbor, Herr Gruber, has mentioned the footsteps.

**11.** On the matter of Szeliga's biography, I must address certain lacunae in the record. Dariusz Szeliga was born in 1921 — of this I am confident, though the specific date has proven elusive, as the parish records of Puławy were partially destroyed during the war, a circumstance that is historically documented and requires no further verification. He studied mathematics at the University of Warsaw, or possibly Lublin, before turning to literature in the late 1940s. His only published novel, *The Dwellings of Herzen Raab*, appeared in 1962. He died in 1963, in circumstances that remain unclear.

I say "circumstances that remain unclear" not because the circumstances are mysterious — I have been given to understand, by sources I am not at liberty to name, that Szeliga died of pneumonia — but because the documentary record is sparse, as one would expect for a minor Polish writer of the mid-twentieth century whose single published work appeared in an edition of 200 copies during a period of considerable political upheaval. The absence of documentation is not the same as the absence of the person. I have published a paper on this distinction: "The Evidentiary Burden and the Literary Ghost: On the Difficulty of Proving That Interesting People Existed" (*Comparative Literature Quarterly*, vol. 18, no. 3, 2007 — though I should note that the journal ceased publication in 2005, and the paper appeared in what I am told was a posthumous issue, a concept I find both irregular and strangely appropriate). Professor Wieczorek once told me, over coffee at a conference in Poznań, that she found the paper's title "genuinely funny," which I took as a greater compliment than she intended, as I had not meant it to be funny at all.

**15.** Chapter Seven of *The Dwellings of Herzen Raab* contains, in my judgment, the finest passage of prose fiction written in any language in the second half of the twentieth century. I reproduce it here from memory, as I have done for the entirety of the critical companion, and I am aware that this method of transmission invites skepticism. To those skeptics I offer the following: Homer was transmitted by memory for centuries before anyone thought to write him down, and no one questions the existence of the *Iliad*. I am not Homer. But I am, for Szeliga, the equivalent — the single vessel in which the work survives. If the vessel is imperfect, the loss is literature's, not mine.

The passage in question describes Raab's visit to the Museum of Applied Purposes:

> The museum was arranged not by chronology or medium but by intention. Room One contained objects made to be beautiful. Room Two contained objects made to be useful. Room Three — the largest, and the one in which Raab spent the remainder of the afternoon — contained objects whose purpose had been forgotten. A handle without a door. A dial without a mechanism. A shoe, impeccably preserved, for a foot that did not correspond to any known human anatomy. The placard read: PURPOSE UNDER REVIEW. Raab sat before the shoe for two hours and felt, for the first time in the novel, that he was in the right place.

I confess I wept, the first time I read this passage. I am weeping now, or close to it — the distinction between weeping and nearly weeping being, in my experience, largely administrative. The shoe for a foot that does not exist. The purpose under review. Szeliga understood — as very few writers understand — that the deepest comedy and the deepest grief occupy the same room and that the room is usually a museum, and the museum is usually closed, and the hours were posted once on a sign that has since been painted over.

**19.** The question I am asked most frequently — by Professor Wieczorek, by the editorial board of Archipelago University Press, by my former department at Ghent, by Herr Gruber (who seems to have developed an interest in my work, or at least in my walking patterns) — is whether I can produce the original text of *The Dwellings of Herzen Raab*. I find the request both understandable and faintly obscene. One does not ask a cathedral to produce the quarry from which its stones were cut. One does not ask a river to produce its source. The critical companion IS the text, in the only form that matters: annotated, analyzed, loved. The novel as Szeliga wrote it was raw material. What I have made of it is architecture.

But I will say this, because I have been accused of evasion, and evasion is a sin I take seriously, having elevated it to an art form. The last known copy of the novel was in my possession. It was destroyed in a flood. The flood was real. The insurance company documented it. Water came through the ceiling of my study in Ghent on the night of November 14, 2008, and destroyed approximately two hundred volumes, including the Szeliga. The insurance claim lists "200 books, miscellaneous, estimated replacement value €4,000." They valued Szeliga's novel at twenty euros. I valued it at everything. The discrepancy between these two valuations is, I think, the subject of the novel itself.

**21.** I have been told, by a reviewer for Archipelago University Press whose report I obtained through means I would rather not specify, that my companion "reads less like scholarship and more like the work of a man in love." I accept this assessment entirely. What the reviewer intended as a disqualification I take as a compliment. The history of criticism is, in the main, a history of insufficient love. Critics admire at arm's length. They appreciate with qualifications. They praise in language designed to demonstrate that the praiser is at least as intelligent as the praised. I have done none of these things. I have loved this novel with the kind of reckless specificity that makes colleagues uncomfortable and editorial boards reach for their rejection letters. If this is not scholarship, then scholarship should revise its definition, because the alternative — a critical apparatus constructed without love — is merely a filing system, and filing systems do not require seventeen years.

**23.** It has been suggested — by Professor Wieczorek, whose persistence on this matter approaches what I would, in a less generous mood, call harassment — that no record of Oficyna Wydawnicza Pożar exists in any Polish publishing directory from the period. I have addressed this objection in Appendix B. Briefly: Oficyna Wydawnicza Pożar was a small press operating out of Łódź during a period when small presses in Łódź appeared and disappeared with the regularity of seasonal influenza. That its records have not survived is unremarkable. That Professor Wieczorek expects every minor publishing house from 1962 to have left a paper trail suggests a faith in bureaucratic permanence that I find touching but historically illiterate.

The name, I should note, translates roughly as "Fire Publishing House," which some have found suspicious. I find it beautiful. A press named after its own eventual fate. Every publishing house is named Fire; most simply don't know it yet.

**27.** A note on method. This critical companion was written entirely from memory. I read *The Dwellings of Herzen Raab* nine times between 1991 and 2008. I began the transcription in 2009, after the flood. The process was not unlike what I imagine translation to be — not a copying but a reconstruction, a building of the thing again from the inside out, using materials that have been changed by their passage through another mind.

I am aware of the epistemological problems. A text reconstructed from memory is not the same text. It is, at best, a cousin of the original — sharing features, diverging in details, retaining the family resemblance while developing its own distinct physiognomy. My Szeliga is not Szeliga's Szeliga. But then, Szeliga's Szeliga no longer exists. And between a perfect copy of nothing and an imperfect copy of something, I will take the imperfect copy. I will take it and annotate it and love it and defend it against Wieczorek and Archipelago University Press and anyone else who demands that literature produce its receipts.

**31.** I should address the matter of Szeliga's photograph. I possessed one — a black-and-white image, slightly overexposed, showing a man of perhaps forty standing in front of what appears to be a municipal building, hands in the pockets of a dark overcoat, expression unreadable. The photograph was destroyed in the flood along with the novel. I have described the photograph in several published and unpublished papers. I have described the man's posture, his coat, the angle of his hat, the quality of the light. I have described him so thoroughly and so often that I can no longer distinguish between the photograph I remember and the description I have written. This should trouble me more than it does. What troubles me instead is that the man in the photograph — or in my description of the photograph — is wearing a hat whose angle I have specified differently in three separate papers. In the 2011 paper, the hat is tilted slightly left. In the 2014 paper, slightly right. In the 2019 paper, I describe it as level but pulled low. I have not been able to determine which of these is correct, and I have begun to suspect that the hat is tilted in whichever direction best supports the argument I happen to be making at the time, which is perhaps what hats are for.

**33.** I have a recurring dream in which I am standing in the secondhand bookshop on Floriańska Street and the proprietor is handing me a book. The book is *The Dwellings of Herzen Raab*. I take it. I open it. The pages are blank. Every one. I turn to the proprietor and she says, in Polish, "You haven't written it yet." I wake from this dream in a state of agitation that Herr Gruber, whose ceiling is my floor, has learned to predict. He once left a note under my door: "The dreams again?" He is a surprisingly perceptive man for someone whose literary interests, so far as I can determine, extend no further than the television listings.

I mention the dream because it illustrates something about the relationship between memory and invention that I have not been able to articulate in my waking critical prose. In the dream, the bookshop proprietor does not say "you invented it." She says "you haven't written it yet." The tense matters. The verb matters. The dream distinguishes between fabrication and anticipation — between a lie about the past and a truth about the future. I do not know what Szeliga would make of this distinction. I do not know what I make of it.

**34.** The passage I have most often quoted in lectures — in the years when I was permitted to lecture, before the incident at Ghent, which I maintain was a misunderstanding about institutional definitions of "office use" that has been blown grotesquely out of proportion — is from Chapter Twelve, the final chapter, in which Raab returns to the building from the opening scene:

> He stood in the doorway again. The key was in his pocket. He did not try it. He had learned, over the course of 80,000 words and an unspecified number of years, that the key's function was not to open the door but to give him a reason to stand before it. The standing was the thing. The doorway was the dwelling.

I find this ending almost unbearably beautiful, and I am aware that this assessment may be compromised by the fact that I have quoted the passage so many times, in so many contexts, and edited my transcription so carefully over so many years, that the prose may now be as much mine as Szeliga's. This does not diminish the beauty. It may complicate it. I have not decided whether complication is the same thing as diminishment, and I suspect the answer changes depending on who is asking and what they hope to hear.

**37.** The question that remains is whether Dariusz Szeliga existed.

I have avoided it long enough. I will answer it now, not because the apparatus deserves a capstone — though it does — but because I am tired, and because tiredness occasionally produces the same results as honesty, and because the difference between the two is, at this hour, academic in every sense.

Dariusz Szeliga existed in the way that all necessary fictions exist: as a condition for the things that follow from him. Without Szeliga, there is no novel. Without the novel, there is no critical companion. Without the critical companion, there is no Lazlo Venn — or at least, no Lazlo Venn worth the name, no Lazlo Venn who has done anything other than teach survey courses and attend conferences where people discuss books they have read without ever loving them with the kind of love that produces 412 pages of footnotes and three appendices and a street map on a study floor.

I needed Szeliga. So I provided him. This is either the definition of scholarship or the negation of it, and I have spent seventeen years unable to determine which, and I have begun to suspect that the inability to determine which IS the scholarship, in the same way that the key's inability to open the door IS the dwelling.

Professor Wieczorek will be satisfied. Archipelago University Press will feel vindicated. Herr Gruber will continue to hear the footsteps.

I confess I had expected this annotation to be the last. I had planned it that way — a capstone, a final note of resolution, the sort of ending that critics like to give things. But I find I cannot stop. The companion is 412 pages and this annotation does not appear in it. This is extra-textual. This is outside the apparatus. And the fact that I am still writing, that I cannot leave the work alone even in the moment of admitting it was mine to leave alone from the beginning — I think this is the more honest confession.

I do not know what Szeliga would make of it. But then, I never did.
