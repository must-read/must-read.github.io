The gloves arrived at Johnson Space Center in Building 31 on a Tuesday in August, six weeks before the capsule was scheduled to land. They were Hypalon over butyl rubber, custom-fitted, ordered from a supplier in Pennsylvania who made clean-room gloves for semiconductor fabrication and occasionally for NASA when the application required tolerances that off-the-shelf wouldn't meet. Elin Lindqvist signed for the shipment herself. She opened the outer packaging in the anteroom, logged the lot number, and inspected each pair under a stereo microscope at 40x magnification, turning the fingers slowly, looking for pinholes, for inclusions in the rubber, for any imperfection that would allow a molecule of her skin oil or a particle of terrestrial dust to migrate through the barrier and reach the sample.

There were fourteen pairs. She rejected two — a hairline void in the left index finger of one, a slight asymmetry in the wrist seal of another — and logged the rejections in the contamination-control database with the notation she always used: *unacceptable per CCR-0091, Section 4.2.3.* The remaining twelve pairs she sealed in nitrogen-purged bags and stored in the anteroom cabinet. She did not think about what her hands would be doing inside those gloves in six weeks. She thought about the wrist seal thickness, which was 0.8 millimeters, and whether 0.8 millimeters was enough.

It was the kind of question that had no satisfying answer. The seal had been tested. It met specifications. The specifications had been written by Elin herself, four years earlier, based on permeation studies she'd run on seven candidate materials, measuring the diffusion rates of water vapor, oxygen, and selected organic compounds through elastomer membranes of varying thickness at the temperature and humidity range expected inside the glovebox. The number — 0.8 millimeters — was the result of those studies. And still she looked at the gloves and thought: is it enough.

---

She had been working on OSIRIS-REx contamination control since 2016. Seven years. The job title on her badge said Contamination Control Engineer, Astromaterials Research and Exploration Science Division, which was a lot of words that meant she was the person responsible for making sure nothing from Earth got into the sample and nothing from the sample got lost to Earth. The mission had collected approximately 250 grams of material from the surface of an asteroid called Bennu — a rubble-pile near-Earth asteroid, 500 meters in diameter, composed of carbonaceous chondrite, the oldest class of material in the solar system. Fragments of the original solar nebula. Dust from before the sun ignited.

The sample was falling toward Utah at that moment, September 2023, sealed inside a capsule inside a spacecraft that was on its final approach trajectory, two million miles out and closing. In six weeks the capsule would separate from the spacecraft, enter the atmosphere over the Pacific at 27,650 miles per hour, deploy a drogue chute at 100,000 feet, a main chute at 10,000 feet, and drift the last seven minutes onto the salt flats of the Utah Test and Training Range, a military installation in the Great Basin Desert where the nearest civilian structure was a gas station twenty-three miles to the south.

Elin's job was everything that happened after the capsule touched the ground.

---

Seventy-two hours before landing. She was in Utah, in a temporary clean room constructed inside a hangar at the Test and Training Range. The hangar was a corrugated-metal building used by the Air Force for helicopter maintenance, and the clean room had been built inside it like a surgery theater inside a barn — positive-pressure HEPA-filtered enclosure, stainless-steel work surfaces, nitrogen supply lines running from bulk liquid-nitrogen tanks outside the hangar through insulated pipes and into the room through sealed penetrations in the wall. The floor was epoxy-coated concrete. The air was Class 100 — meaning no more than one hundred particles of 0.5 microns or larger per cubic foot. For context: ordinary outdoor air in the Utah desert contained roughly thirty-five million particles per cubic foot. A hospital operating room was about ten thousand. Class 100 was the standard for semiconductor wafer fabrication, for pharmaceutical fill lines, for any process where a single misplaced particle could ruin the product.

The product, in this case, was 250 grams of asteroid.

Elin ran the particle counter along the work surfaces at eight predetermined locations, logged the readings, compared them to the previous day's readings, and noted a slight elevation at Station 3, near the nitrogen inlet. She traced it to a fitting that had been hand-tightened rather than torqued to specification. She torqued it. She ran the counter again. The reading dropped. She logged the correction.

This was her day. This had been her week. She checked the clean room. She briefed the recovery team — the helicopter crew, the ground crew, the sample-handling technicians who would meet the capsule on the salt flat and transport it to the hangar. She inspected the nitrogen supply system: two 3,000-gallon liquid-nitrogen dewars, each containing enough nitrogen gas, once vaporized, to maintain a positive-pressure nitrogen atmosphere inside the capsule containment vessel for forty-eight hours. She verified the flow rate — 14.2 liters per minute through the primary line, 8.6 liters per minute through the backup — and recorded the regulator pressures and the dewar levels. She checked the witness plates: small discs of high-purity aluminum and sapphire positioned at critical locations inside the clean room and inside the containment vessel, designed to capture any particulate or organic contamination that might occur during sample handling. If a witness plate showed contamination, Elin would know where and when the breach had occurred. If the plates were clean, the sample was clean.

The witness plates were her instruments. Her gauges. The capsule would land or not land, the heat shield would hold or not hold, and those outcomes were beyond her jurisdiction. But the witness plates were hers. She had specified their material, their size (25 millimeters in diameter, 1 millimeter thick), their placement, and the analytical protocols for examining them after the sample was secured. They were the documentary evidence of her seven years of work. They would prove either that she had done her job or that she had not.

---

The principal investigator was on television that morning. Elin saw it on the screen in the break area outside the hangar — a portable television someone had brought, propped on a folding table between a coffee maker and a box of granola bars. He was explaining the significance of the mission. He said the word *pristine* twice in ninety seconds. He said *the building blocks of life.* He said *a window into the early solar system.*

Elin poured herself a coffee. She watched him speak. She knew him — not well, but well enough to have been in meetings with him, to have exchanged emails about contamination thresholds and analytical priorities. He was a good scientist. He could communicate clearly on camera, which was a skill she did not have and had never been asked to develop. He did not mention contamination control. He did not mention the clean room, the nitrogen system, the witness plates, the seven years of protocol design that would determine whether the sample he was calling *pristine* would actually be pristine when it reached his laboratory.

She did not expect him to mention these things. He was doing his job, which was to explain to the public why this mission mattered. Her job was not public-facing. Her job was, in a precise sense, the opposite of public-facing: her job was to create a sealed environment from which the public — and every contaminant the public carried — was excluded.

She finished her coffee and went back inside. There was a humidity sensor to recalibrate.

---

What the asteroid material looked like: dark. Granular. In the photographs Elin had seen from the spacecraft's cameras during collection — the images taken by the TAGSAM (Touch-and-Go Sample Acquisition Mechanism) head camera while the collection device was pressed against Bennu's surface — the material appeared black, fine-grained in places, rocky in others. Some fragments were a centimeter or larger. Most were sub-millimeter. It looked like road gravel from a quarry that worked with basalt, the kind you'd find in the bed of a pickup truck in any rural county in America.

It was 4.56 billion years old. It had formed in the solar nebula, the cloud of gas and dust that collapsed under its own gravity to produce the sun and the planets and everything else in the system. The material in the OSIRIS-REx canister was a fragment of that cloud — matter that had never been part of a planet, never been melted by geologic heat, never been weathered by water or atmosphere. It had been sitting on the surface of a small, loose assemblage of rubble in a moderately eccentric orbit between Earth and Mars, doing nothing, being rock, for longer than the Earth had existed.

The amino acids in the sample — glycine, alanine, possibly others — had formed by abiotic chemistry in the nebula or on the asteroid's parent body, before the asteroid was an asteroid. They were not evidence of life. They were evidence of the conditions that could produce life, given a few billion years and a planet with liquid water and a magnetic field. The amino acids did not know this. The amino acids were molecules. They had no aspirations.

Elin knew the amino acids would be the headline. *Building blocks of life found on asteroid sample.* Every news cycle would lead with it. And every amino acid in that sample would be a contaminant suspect unless she could prove, through the witness plates and the nitrogen atmosphere records and the chain-of-custody documentation, that it had arrived from Bennu and not from the thumb of a technician in Utah.

One microgram of human skin oil contains approximately 10^14 amino acid molecules. Elin had done this calculation once, years ago, to illustrate a point in a contamination-control review. The scientists in the room had gone quiet. She remembered the silence. It had been the right kind of silence — the kind that meant they understood, for the first time, that the distance between their discovery and a false positive was thinner than a glove.

---

Forty-eight hours before landing. She briefed the helicopter crew. There were two helicopters — a primary and a backup — and six crew members, none of whom had ever participated in a sample-recovery operation. They were military. They were accustomed to briefings. They were not accustomed to being told that the exhaust from their engines was a contamination vector and that the approach pattern had been designed to keep the helicopters downwind of the capsule landing zone at all times, even if that meant a longer flight path, even if that meant arriving later.

"The capsule is not damaged," Elin told them. She was standing at the front of a small room in the hangar, a whiteboard behind her showing the approach pattern, the landing ellipse, the positions of the ground recovery team. "It does not need rescue. It needs custody. Your job is to deliver the ground team to the capsule site and to transport the capsule to this hangar without introducing particulate contamination. That means no approach from upwind. No hovering over the capsule. Engine shutdown at two hundred meters minimum. Questions."

A pilot raised his hand. "What if the capsule lands off the designated zone? What if we need to adjust approach?"

"The approach pattern accounts for a landing deviation of up to four kilometers from the target point. If the deviation is larger than four kilometers, contact range control and I will issue a revised pattern." She did not say: *and if the deviation is larger than four kilometers, something has gone wrong with the parachute system and we may be recovering debris rather than a sample.* She did not need to say it. The helicopter crews had been briefed on the failure modes. They knew what a nominal landing looked like and what a non-nominal landing looked like. The difference was whether the main chute deployed.

She went through the rest of the briefing — the protective coverings for the capsule, the cradle for transport, the contamination barriers that would be installed around the capsule before it was moved. She showed them how to handle the covering: which side was the clean side, which side faced the environment, how to seal the seams with kapton tape without touching the inner surface. Two of the crew members would handle the covering. Four would handle the cradle. All six had been fitted with clean-room smocks and nitrile gloves, which they would put on at the landing site before approaching the capsule.

She did not tell them that the smocks and gloves were, in a sense, the entire point. That the capsule's heat shield had survived 2,900 degrees Celsius of atmospheric entry and that the parachute system had been tested forty-one times and that the trajectory calculations had been verified by three independent teams and that all of those engineering achievements existed to deliver 250 grams of material to a flat piece of Utah desert, and the only thing standing between that material and the terrestrial biosphere was a set of procedures written by a woman from Minnesota who checked gloves for pinholes under a microscope.

She did not tell them because it would have sounded like a speech, and Elin did not give speeches.

After the briefing, one of the pilots lingered. He was young — late twenties, maybe thirty — with the specific, carefully maintained fitness of someone who passed regular flight physicals. He asked her how long she'd been working on this.

"Seven years," she said.

"Seven years. On the clean-room stuff?"

"On the contamination-control protocols. The clean room is one component."

He nodded, absorbing this. "And what happens after? After the sample's processed and shipped to the labs and everything? What do you work on next?"

She considered the question. She had considered it before, in idle moments, and had arrived each time at the same unsatisfying answer: she did not know. The OSIRIS-REx contamination-control program was the largest project of her career. It was, in some sense, the only project of her career — everything before it had been preparation, and she had not yet thought seriously about what would come after. There would be other missions, other samples. The Mars Sample Return campaign was in development. Japan's MMX mission to Phobos was scheduled to launch in 2026. But those were years away, and the protocols would be designed by whoever held the position when the work began, which might or might not be her.

"I'll figure that out next week," she said, which was a lie, but a small one, and the pilot seemed satisfied.

---

That evening she walked outside the hangar. The desert at the Test and Training Range was flat in a way that resisted description — not the rolling flatness of the Midwest, where the horizon undulated gently and there were trees and fences to give the eye a reference point, but a genuine, geological flatness. The playa. Hard-packed alkaline clay stretching in every direction to distant mountain ranges that looked like cardboard cutouts propped against the sky. The air smelled like mineral dust and sage and, very faintly, of the kerosene that had been used to fuel the generators.

She called her sister in Minneapolis. Her sister asked if she was nervous. Elin said she was not nervous. She said she was busy, which was true, and that being busy precluded nervousness, which was not true but was the kind of thing she said to her sister, who worried.

"Is it going to land okay?" her sister asked.

"The probability of a successful landing is very high."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know. But it's what I can tell you."

She hung up and stood in the parking lot for another minute. The stars were coming out. The sky over the Test and Training Range was almost perfectly dark — no cities for a hundred miles in any direction, no light pollution, just the atmosphere and whatever was above it. Somewhere up there, two million miles away and closing at five miles per second, a spacecraft was preparing to release a capsule that would fall for four hours and thirteen minutes through the void and the atmosphere and onto this flat, alkaline ground. Inside the capsule was a canister. Inside the canister were the witness coupons and the sample container. Inside the sample container was material from before the Earth.

The material did not know it was coming home. It had never been home.

Elin went back inside and checked the nitrogen levels.

---

Twenty-four hours. The pre-landing readiness review. Twelve people around a conference table in a trailer adjacent to the hangar, each responsible for a different element of the recovery operation. Range safety. Tracking and telemetry. Air traffic coordination. Weather. Medical. Communications. Ground recovery. Helicopter operations. And contamination control, which was Elin, who sat at the end of the table with her laptop open to a spreadsheet that contained 1,247 line items, each representing a specific contamination-control requirement that she had verified, with date and signature, over the preceding six weeks.

She reported green across all items. No open corrective actions. The clean room was at specification. The nitrogen system was at specification. The witness plates were in position. The recovery team had been briefed and fitted. The transport cradle was clean-wrapped and staged.

The range safety officer asked whether she had a contingency plan for the clean room if the hangar lost power. She did. The nitrogen system would maintain pressure on battery backup for four hours. If power was not restored within four hours, a portable generator staged outside the hangar would be connected. If both the primary and backup power failed, the capsule would remain sealed in its transport container, which was itself a controlled-atmosphere vessel, until power was restored.

"And if the transport container is damaged in transit?" the range safety officer asked.

"The transport container is rated for a ten-meter drop. If it is dropped from higher than ten meters, we have a larger problem than contamination."

Someone laughed. Elin did not laugh. She was not making a joke. She was stating a specification.

The weather briefing followed. Clear skies forecast for the landing window, winds from the southwest at eight to twelve knots, temperature at ground level expected to reach 87 degrees Fahrenheit by midday. The meteorologist noted that a low-pressure system over western Nevada might bring increased wind speeds by late afternoon, but the landing was scheduled for early morning and the recovery timeline called for the capsule to be inside the hangar within ninety minutes of touchdown. Elin wrote down the wind direction and speed, not because she hadn't already checked them herself — she had, twice, using both the range's weather station and the National Weather Service forecast for Tooele County — but because if the wind shifted, she would need to recalculate the helicopter approach pattern to keep the rotor wash downwind of the recovery site.

She had a map of the landing ellipse taped to the inside of her notebook, with wind vectors drawn in pencil for eight compass headings. For each heading she had calculated the optimal helicopter approach, the safe shutdown distance, and the time it would take the ground crew to walk from the helicopter to the capsule in clean-room smocks, which restricted stride length and peripheral vision. The walk would take between four and seven minutes, depending on the distance. During those minutes, the capsule would be sitting on the salt flat, exposed to the desert atmosphere — to the mineral dust, the biological particles, the trace hydrocarbons carried on the wind from the distant interstate highway. Four to seven minutes of exposure. She had calculated the expected particulate deposition rate for each wind condition and determined that the contamination risk was acceptable, provided the capsule was covered within ten minutes of the ground crew's arrival.

Ten minutes. That was the margin. The spacecraft had traveled nine billion miles over seven years, and the margin between a clean sample and a compromised one was ten minutes on a salt flat in Utah.

---

Twelve hours. She could not sleep. She lay in her bunk in the temporary housing — a modular building near the hangar, four bunks to a room, the kind of housing she associated with disaster-relief operations and military deployments — and stared at the ceiling, which was particle board with a thin veneer of something meant to look like wood. She thought about the wrist seal on the gloves. She thought about whether the HEPA filters in the clean room had been integrity-tested recently enough — they had, she had tested them on Thursday, and the DOP aerosol challenge had shown 99.97% efficiency at 0.3 microns, which was the specification — but she thought about it again anyway, the way she sometimes rehearsed locked doors in her mind after she had already checked them.

She thought about Primo Levi. She had read *The Periodic Table* in graduate school, in a course on science communication that had assigned it alongside *The Double Helix* and *Silent Spring* and other books that were supposed to teach scientists how to write about what they did. She had liked Levi's book better than the others because Levi did not write about science as if it were a quest or a revelation. He wrote about science as if it were work — physical, procedural, sometimes tedious work done with substances that had properties and those properties had consequences and the person handling the substances was changed by the handling. The chapter on nickel. The chapter on chromium. The elements were not metaphors. They were elements. And they were also everything else.

Elin's element, if she had one, was nitrogen. Colorless, odorless, comprising 78% of the atmosphere she was trying to exclude from the sample. She used it to displace the air that could contaminate. She purged with it, flooded with it, pressurized with it. Nitrogen was the substance of her professional life — the gas that meant safety, that meant the seal was holding, that meant the sample was untouched. And like most of Levi's elements, it was entirely ordinary. You could buy liquid nitrogen from an industrial gas supplier. You could use it to make ice cream. There was nothing rare or precious about nitrogen. Its value was contextual. Its value was that it was not air.

She did not sleep. At 4:00 a.m. she got up and went to the clean room to check the particle counts. The readings were fine. They had been fine every time she checked. The clean room was performing to specification, as it had performed to specification every day since it was commissioned, because she had specified it correctly and the contractors had built it correctly and the HEPA filters were new and the seals were intact and the nitrogen supply was full and the monitoring systems were calibrated and there was, in fact, nothing wrong.

She checked anyway. The checking was not rational. She knew it was not rational. But the checking was also not irrational, because the consequence of a missed contamination event was irreversible — you could not un-contaminate a sample, could not subtract the terrestrial organics once they had been deposited, could not rewind the chain of custody and redo the step where the seal leaked or the filter failed or the technician forgot to double-glove. The asymmetry of consequences justified the redundancy of checking. She believed this. She also knew it was the kind of belief that could, if she was not careful, become compulsion rather than discipline, and that the line between the two was not always obvious from the inside.

She checked the particle counts one more time, logged them, and went to get dressed.

---

Landing day. September 24, 2023. The capsule entered the atmosphere at 8:42 a.m. Mountain Time, over the Pacific Ocean, west of the California coast. Elin was in the hangar, in the clean room, watching a live telemetry feed on a monitor mounted to the wall. She was not watching the television coverage. She was watching the numbers — altitude, velocity, trajectory deviation, capsule temperature. The numbers arrived in a stream, updated every two seconds, and she watched them the way she imagined an anesthesiologist watched vital signs during surgery: not with excitement, not with dread, but with a continuous, low-level alertness that was prepared to convert instantly to action if a number moved outside its expected range.

The capsule crossed the California coast at 8:44 a.m. at an altitude of 83 miles. It was decelerating. The heat shield was absorbing the kinetic energy of a nine-billion-mile journey and converting it to thermal radiation. The peak heating occurred at 8:46 a.m. — the external surface of the heat shield reached approximately 2,900 degrees Celsius, which was hotter than the melting point of steel, hotter than the melting point of iron, approaching the temperature at the surface of some stars. Inside the heat shield, the sample canister remained at ambient temperature. The thermal protection system was doing its job.

At 8:48 a.m. the drogue chute deployed. Elin noted the altitude — 100,200 feet — and the velocity — 900 miles per hour — and the fact that both were within the nominal envelope. The drogue chute slowed the capsule to 200 miles per hour over the next four minutes.

At 8:52 a.m. the main chute deployed at 9,800 feet. Elin watched the descent rate drop from 200 miles per hour to 11 miles per hour in seven seconds. She noted the time. She noted the descent rate. She looked at the trajectory prediction and saw that the landing point was within 1.2 kilometers of the target, well inside the recovery envelope.

At 8:55 a.m. the capsule touched down on the playa.

The room erupted. People were clapping, hugging, some were crying. Elin heard the cheering through the clean-room walls — the observation area outside, the trailer complex, the range-control building half a mile away. She did not cheer. She turned to the recovery-team lead and said, "Initiate recovery sequence. Confirm wind direction and contact helicopter operations for approach clearance."

The team lead nodded and got on the radio. Elin checked the clean-room particle count. Class 100. Nominal. She checked the nitrogen pressure. Nominal. She opened the transport-cradle packaging and verified the clean barrier. She put on her clean-room smock, her hood, her booties, her first pair of gloves, then her second pair of gloves — a double-glove protocol she had specified because a single glove could be compromised by a sharp edge on the capsule's exterior and the wearer would not feel it through the clean-room fabric.

The helicopter was in the air.

---

The capsule arrived at the hangar at 10:14 a.m. Elin watched through the clean-room window as the ground team brought it in on the transport cradle, sealed in its contamination barrier. They moved it onto the clean-room anteroom staging area. Elin went into the anteroom — a transitional space between the hangar environment and the clean room, maintained at Class 1,000 — and supervised the removal of the outer barrier. Then the capsule, still in its transport container, was moved into the clean room itself.

For the next six hours she worked. She documented the capsule exterior for any damage. She removed the back shell — the aerodynamic fairing that covered the top of the capsule during entry — and set it aside for later analysis. She connected the nitrogen purge line to the canister access port and initiated the flow: 14.2 liters per minute of high-purity nitrogen, displacing the air inside the canister housing with an inert atmosphere that would protect the sample from oxidation and moisture contamination. She monitored the oxygen sensor inside the canister housing and watched the reading drop — 20.9%, 18%, 14%, 9%, 5%, 2%, 0.8%, 0.3% — until it stabilized below the 0.5% threshold she had established as the safe level for sample exposure.

She logged the time. She logged the flow rate. She logged the oxygen reading. She collected the first set of witness plates from outside the canister and placed them in pre-labeled sample bags for later analysis. She photographed each step. She noted anomalies — a small scratch on the canister exterior, probably from the landing impact, superficial, no breach of the seal — and logged them with photographs and measurements.

At 4:30 p.m. the canister was secure. The nitrogen atmosphere was stable. The witness plates were collected. The documentation was complete. The sample was still sealed inside the canister — the actual opening would happen at Johnson Space Center, in Houston, in the permanent clean room that had been built specifically for this purpose, a room Elin had helped design, a room where the glovebox waited.

At 6:15 p.m. she called the principal investigator's office in Houston and reported that the capsule was secure, the nitrogen atmosphere was stable, and the initial witness plates were collected and bagged for analysis. The PI's assistant took the call. The PI was at a press event. Elin gave the information clearly and concisely and the assistant repeated it back and that was the end of the conversation. She did not ask about the press event and the assistant did not volunteer details.

She stepped out of the clean room. She removed her smock, her hood, her booties, her outer gloves, her inner gloves. She washed her hands. She washed them for a long time, with soap, under water that was too hot, the way she always washed them after desuiting, because the sensation of clean skin after hours inside gloves was something she had never gotten used to, the prickling return of air to compressed fingers, the particular sensitivity of hands that had been enclosed.

---

The capsule was shipped to Houston two days later, inside its controlled-atmosphere transport container, inside a shock-mounted crate, inside a temperature-controlled truck, inside a convoy that included a chase vehicle and a communications van. Elin flew commercial, coach, from Salt Lake City. She ate a bag of pretzels on the plane and did not think about the capsule, which was somewhere on Interstate 80, heading east, surrounded by eighteen-wheelers and families driving to Colorado for vacation.

Three weeks later. Building 31 at Johnson Space Center. The curation laboratory. Elin was inside the nitrogen glovebox — or rather, her hands were inside the glovebox, inserted through the ports in the glovebox wall, encased in the Hypalon-over-butyl gloves she had inspected under the microscope in August. The twelve pairs minus the two she rejected, so ten pairs, and she was wearing pair number three, which she had selected that morning based on a rotation schedule she kept in a notebook.

Inside the glovebox, visible through the polycarbonate window, was the opened canister. And inside the canister, loose in the collection tray, was the material from Bennu. Dark. Granular. Some fragments the size of rice grains, some smaller, some — a few — the size of peas. It looked like what it was: rock. Old rock, ground fine by four and a half billion years of thermal cycling and micrometeorite impacts on an airless surface in the inner solar system, but rock.

She was transferring material from the collection tray to the storage containers — small, pre-cleaned quartz jars that would hold allocated portions of the sample for distribution to research teams around the world. She used a scoop and a set of forceps, both made of surgical-grade stainless steel that had been cleaned by a protocol she had written: sequential ultrasonic baths in acetone, methanol, and ultrapure water, followed by baking at 500 degrees Celsius for twelve hours to remove any residual organic contamination. The tools were as clean as human fabrication could make them. The gloves were as clean as human fabrication could make them. The nitrogen atmosphere was as clean as human fabrication could make them.

And still there was a distance. Between her fingertips and the asteroid material: two layers of glove, then the stainless steel of the scoop. Between her breath and the sample: the glovebox wall, the positive-pressure nitrogen atmosphere, the HEPA-filtered room air. Between her body and the thing she was protecting: every protocol she had written, every seal she had tested, every specification she had argued for in review meetings where engineers with tighter budgets wanted thinner gloves or fewer witness plates or a lower-grade nitrogen supply.

The distance was the work. The distance was what she had spent seven years building. And now, with her hands inside the glovebox and the sample dark and loose under the lights, the distance held.

She scooped a fragment — two, three millimeters across, black, rough-surfaced — and transferred it to a quartz jar. She noted the mass on the analytical balance: 0.047 grams. She sealed the jar. She logged the transfer. She reached for the next container.

Outside the glovebox window, through the clean-room observation port, she could see a monitor showing a cable-news segment. The principal investigator was on screen again, holding a photograph of the sample. He was saying something about the early solar system. She could not hear him through the glass. She did not try to hear him.

Her hands moved inside the gloves. The gloves moved inside the glovebox. The glovebox held its nitrogen atmosphere at 0.2% oxygen, steady, all the gauges reading nominal. The witness plates, collected and analyzed, had shown contamination levels below the detection limit of the analytical instruments. Below the detection limit. Meaning: clean. Meaning: everything she had done had worked. Meaning: the gap between her skin and the sample was exactly as wide as it needed to be, and no wider, and no narrower.

She did not feel triumph. She did not feel vindication. She felt her hands inside the gloves, and the resistance of the glove material against her fingers, and the weight of the scoop, and the slight gritty shift of asteroid material against stainless steel. She felt the particular, unremarkable sensation of doing the thing she had trained to do, in the room she had helped build, with the tools she had specified, according to procedures she had written, for a sample that did not know her name and would outlast her and everyone she had ever met by a margin so large it was not a number but a category of time.

She transferred another fragment. Logged the mass. Sealed the jar. The nitrogen hissed softly through the supply line. The particle counter on the wall read 42 particles per cubic foot. The clock on the wall read 2:17 p.m.

She reached for the next container.
