## River-Side

Freeboard is the distance between the surface of the water and the top of the wall that is holding it. On the Mississippi River levee south of Donaldsonville, Louisiana, in Assumption Parish, on a Tuesday in October when the river stage at the Donaldsonville gauge reads 14.7 feet, the freeboard is approximately nine feet. Nine feet of compacted earth and clay between the brown surface of the river and the place where the levee ends and the air begins. Nine feet is a comfortable margin. Nine feet is the distance across a small bedroom. Nine feet is the height of a ceiling in a house built before the Second World War, and many of the houses behind this levee were built before the Second World War.

In the spring of 2024, during a flood that crested at 23.1 feet at the Donaldsonville gauge, the freeboard at this section was reduced to less than one foot. Eleven inches. The width of a man's shoe. Volunteers and inmates from the parish jail were stacking sandbags along the crest at two in the morning, working under floodlights, layering the bags in a herringbone pattern that the Army Corps of Engineers had diagrammed in a field manual that most of them had never seen. They were adding to the levee the way the levee had always been added to — by hand, in crisis, in the dark.

The Mississippi River levee system is the longest continuous earthwork on the planet. It extends approximately 3,500 miles along both banks of the river, from Cape Girardeau, Missouri, to the Head of Passes below Venice, Louisiana, where the river fans into the Gulf. If you include the tributary levees on the Arkansas, the Red, the Yazoo, the Atchafalaya, and the dozens of smaller rivers that drain into the Mississippi Basin, the total mileage exceeds 12,000. Twelve thousand miles of earth wall, most of it built between 1882 and 1968, most of it designed to withstand a flood roughly equivalent to the catastrophic event of 1927, when the river broke through in 145 places and inundated 27,000 square miles.

The 1927 design standard was called the "project flood." The Army Corps of Engineers calculated it as the worst possible combination of simultaneous tributary floods, peak rainfall over the entire basin, and maximum snowmelt from the headwaters. Every levee in the system was engineered to contain the project flood with a specified margin of safety. The margin was three feet of freeboard — three feet above the calculated high-water mark. Three feet. The height of a kitchen counter.

There are several things wrong with this design, and they compound.

First: the project flood was a statistical artifact, not a physical limit. It was derived from the hydrological record available in 1927 — roughly 150 years of observations, some of them unreliable. The Mississippi Basin has been producing floods for approximately 10,000 years, since the Laurentide Ice Sheet retreated and the modern river system took shape. Basing the design flood on 150 years of data is like basing the speed limit on the fastest car you've personally seen. You may not have been watching on the right day.

Second: the levees have changed the river. When a river floods a natural floodplain, it spreads laterally, sometimes for miles. The velocity drops. The carrying capacity drops. Sediment settles. The floodplain absorbs the water like a sponge, slowly releasing it over weeks. When levees confine the river to its channel, the water has nowhere to spread. It rises vertically instead of horizontally. A flood that would have been twelve feet deep and three miles wide becomes twenty-four feet deep and a quarter mile wide. The levees create the conditions they were designed to prevent.

Third, and this is the one that keeps hydraulic engineers awake: the channel is rising. The Mississippi carries 500 million tons of sediment per year, eroded from the Rockies, the Great Plains, the farms of Iowa, the parking lots of Minneapolis. In a natural system, that sediment would deposit on the floodplain during floods, building land, rebuilding the delta. In the leveed system, the sediment drops in the channel. The channel bed rises. The flood stages rise with it. The levees must be raised to match. This has been happening for 150 years. At the Old River Control Structure, where the Corps maintains the distribution of flow between the Mississippi and the Atchafalaya, the flood stages have risen roughly twelve feet since the levees were built. Twelve feet. The height of a room.

The arithmetic is not difficult. The levees were designed with three feet of freeboard above a flood calculated from 150 years of limited data, on a river whose flood stages are rising because the levees themselves have altered the hydraulics. Every decade of successful flood control narrows the margin. The freeboard shrinks. Not because the levees are deteriorating — though some are — but because the river, confined, is growing taller inside its cage.

I drove the levee road south from Donaldsonville on a morning when the river was low, maybe eleven feet at the gauge, and the freeboard was a generous thirteen feet, and the whole structure looked absurdly overbuilt — a massive earthen rampart guarding against a river that seemed modest and contained. The levee at this point is twenty-four feet above the surrounding grade. It is wider at its base than a four-lane highway. It has a twenty-foot crown, paved for vehicle access, and side slopes of one vertical to three horizontal, planted in Bermuda grass to prevent erosion. Viewed from the crown, the river is a brown plate, calm, wide, deceptively slow. Barges go by in silence. The far bank is a mile away and furred with willows.

The levee is composed of compacted clay, sourced from borrow pits dug parallel to the levee alignment. The clay is placed in twelve-inch lifts, each lift compacted to 95 percent of standard Proctor density, a measure of soil compaction named for Ralph Proctor, a field engineer with the Bureau of Reclamation who published his compaction test in 1933. The clay must meet specific plasticity requirements — too sandy and it erodes, too plastic and it cracks. The ideal levee clay is a silty clay of medium plasticity, classified as CL in the Unified Soil Classification System, with a liquid limit between 30 and 50 and a plasticity index between 10 and 25.

I am laying out these numbers because they matter and because the precision of the engineering is itself part of the story. A levee that fails because it was poorly built is a construction failure. A levee that fails despite being built to specification is a design failure. And a levee built precisely to specification, maintained according to schedule, inspected annually by qualified engineers, that is overtopped because the river has exceeded the conditions the specification was written for — that is a failure of imagination. The engineering was correct. The premise was wrong.

The premise was that the river could be known.

## Land-Side

I turned around on the levee crown and looked the other direction, away from the river, and it was a different country. Not geologically different — the alluvium was the same, the clay, the silt, the groundwater table. But arranged differently. Inhabited. On the river side, there was the river, and the batture — the strip of wooded land between the levee and the water — and beyond that, nothing human. On the land side, there were houses.

They were close. Closer than you'd think possible, living next to a structure that exists because a catastrophic volume of water is likely to overwhelm it. Some were within a hundred yards of the levee toe. Small houses, mostly. Vinyl siding, metal roofs, elevated foundations — not elevated enough. Yards with plastic furniture and truck tires and children's bicycles left on their sides in the grass. At the end of one driveway, a hand-lettered sign for boiled crawfish, with a phone number. In front of another, three white crosses with plastic flowers, a roadside memorial that had nothing to do with flooding but that I could not stop seeing as a marker of a different kind of reckoning.

Assumption Parish has a population of about 22,000, down from 25,000 at the 2000 census. The median household income is $42,000 — roughly $20,000 below the national median. The poverty rate is 22 percent. The population is 33 percent Black, concentrated in the unincorporated communities along the river — Plattenville, Bayou L'Ourse, Superville, Belle Rose. These are the communities behind the levee. Not behind it in the sense of supported by it, though they are. Behind it in the sense of hidden by it. If you drive the River Road on the levee crown, you see the river on your left and, on your right, below you, a landscape that appears to recede, as though the levee were the rim of a bowl and the houses at the bottom.

That optical effect is not only optical. The land is sinking.

Subsidence in the Mississippi Delta is measured in millimeters per year, and the numbers vary by location, but in Assumption Parish, the rate is between eight and twelve millimeters annually. That is roughly half an inch per year, or five inches per decade, or two feet per century — if the rate stays constant, which it will not, because the rate is accelerating. The causes are multiple: compaction of the alluvial sediments under their own weight, withdrawal of groundwater and hydrocarbons from beneath the surface, tectonic adjustment to the absence of the glaciers that once depressed the northern half of the continent and whose removal is still causing the southern half to sink. The levee itself contributes to the subsidence — its weight compresses the soil beneath it, lowering the surrounding land relative to the levee crest, creating the illusion that the levee is growing taller when in fact the ground is dropping away.

In 2012, a sinkhole opened in Bayou Corne, ten miles north of Donaldsonville, when a salt dome cavern operated by Texas Brine Company collapsed. The sinkhole began as a crack in the earth, then a depression, then a hole roughly an acre in size, filled with brine and liquid hydrocarbons, slowly eating the surrounding land. Trees tilted and fell into it. The water in the bayou began to bubble with methane. Three hundred and fifty residents of the community were evacuated and never returned. Texas Brine paid settlements. The state issued orders. The sinkhole continued to grow. It is still growing. Last measured at approximately thirty-seven acres — the size of a moderately ambitious parking lot.

The sinkhole has no direct connection to the levee system, but it shares a geology and a logic. The same alluvial sediments that make ideal levee clay also make unstable ground. The same extraction economy that created the need for levees — cotton, sugar, petrochemicals, all requiring dry land in a wet landscape — also hollowed out the substrate. The land is sinking because the weight on top of it was increased and the material beneath it was removed. That is not a natural process. It is an economic one, and the people living inside it did not choose it, and many of them do not fully understand it, and the ones who do understand it cannot afford to leave.

I spoke with a woman in Plattenville who had lived behind the levee for forty years. Her name was Odette. She was seventy-one. She sat on her porch in a lawn chair, and she could see the levee from where she sat — the grass slope rising above the roofline of her neighbor's house, a green wall against the sky. She told me she had never been on top of it.

"I don't go up there," she said. "What for? To see the water?"

She told me about the flood of 2016 — not the river flood, the rain flood. In August 2016, a low-pressure system stalled over southern Louisiana and dropped more than two feet of rain in three days. The river rose, but the levees held. The flooding came from behind — from the interior, from the drainage canals that couldn't handle the volume, from the bayous that backed up because their outlets to the river were blocked by floodgates closed to prevent the river from coming in. The system designed to keep the river out also kept the rain in. Odette's house took three feet of water.

"The pump stations couldn't keep up," she said. "They only have so much capacity. And the water had nowhere to go because they close those gates when the river is high. So the river is high on one side of the levee and the rain is rising on this side and we're in between."

Between. It is a word that describes the condition precisely. The communities behind the levee exist in a between — between the river and the swamp, between the designed flood and the undesigned flood, between the protection of the levee system and the vulnerability it creates. The levee holds back the Mississippi. It does not hold back the rain, the subsidence, the saltwater intrusion, the groundwater rise, the insurance cancellations, the slow economic death of a parish that has been losing population for twenty-five years. The levee addresses one threat and is silent about the others, and the silence is heard, by the people who live behind it, as safety.

## River-Side

At the Old River Control Structure, 65 miles upriver from Donaldsonville, the Army Corps of Engineers maintains the single most consequential piece of infrastructure in the United States. This is not hyperbole. The structure — actually a complex of three separate structures built between 1963 and 1990 — regulates the distribution of flow between the Mississippi River and the Atchafalaya River. Without it, the Mississippi would have abandoned its present channel sometime in the late twentieth century, diverting its full discharge down the shorter, steeper Atchafalaya route to the Gulf. New Orleans, Baton Rouge, and every city and industrial facility between them would be left on a tidal bayou. The port system would collapse. The petrochemical corridor — known locally as Cancer Alley, a name that contains its own commentary — would lose its cooling water, its process water, its transportation corridor.

The Old River structures prevent this by limiting the Atchafalaya to 30 percent of the combined flow of the Mississippi and the Red River. That 30 percent figure was established by Congress in 1954 and has been maintained ever since. It is not a hydrological number. It is a political number — a compromise between the upstream interests that wanted to keep the Mississippi in its channel and the downstream interests along the Atchafalaya that wanted more water for navigation. The river does not recognize the compromise. The river wants to go west. Every flood season, the pressure differential across the control structure increases, and the Corps opens gates and runs water through the auxiliary structure and monitors the vibrations in the concrete and writes reports that assess the probability of failure using language carefully calibrated to avoid the word "imminent."

In April 1973, during a flood that produced the highest water in the recorded history of the structure, the low sill control structure — the original 1963 facility — suffered a catastrophic partial failure. The flood scoured the foundation, undermining the concrete apron on the downstream side. Seven of the eleven bays showed significant structural damage. The engineers who inspected it afterward found that the structure had come within inches of complete collapse, which would have caused the immediate and irreversible capture of the Mississippi by the Atchafalaya. They described the event, in their reports, as "near failure." The structure was repaired. A second auxiliary structure was built in 1986 to share the load. The system was redesigned.

But the river is still going west. The gradient advantage of the Atchafalaya — shorter distance, steeper slope — increases with every foot of sediment that aggrades in the Mississippi's channel. The structures are holding. For now. The "for now" is the entire content of the situation. Everything downstream of Old River — every refinery, every chemical plant, every port, every neighborhood, every levee, every house behind every levee — exists in the "for now." The temporal frame of human habitation in southern Louisiana is not geological time. It is not even historical time. It is maintenance time — the interval between inspections, between repairs, between the last crisis and the next one.

At the Donaldsonville levee, maintenance time has a specific rhythm. The levee is inspected twice annually — once before flood season, once after. Inspection consists of a visual survey of the levee slopes, the crown, the toe drains, and the relief wells, supplemented by periodic instrument readings from piezometers embedded in the levee body that measure pore water pressure. Elevated pore pressure indicates seepage, which indicates potential piping — the internal erosion of soil particles that can hollow a levee from within and cause it to collapse without overtopping. Piping failures are more dangerous than overtopping because they occur without warning. The water doesn't come over the wall. The wall comes apart.

The inspection reports are public documents, and I read several years' worth. They are written in the language of routine — "no significant distress noted," "vegetation control adequate," "animal burrows repaired." The word "adequate" appears frequently. It is a word that occupies the exact midpoint between confidence and concern, and its repetition across dozens of reports creates a tone of managed anxiety, like a doctor's chart that says "stable" on every visit but never says "healthy."

## Land-Side

There is a church in Belle Rose, on the land side of the levee, called Greater Zion Baptist. It was built in 1947 on a concrete slab foundation, and the slab has cracked in several places, and the cracks have been filled with epoxy, and the epoxy has cracked, and the building lists slightly to the south, toward the lower ground, toward the direction in which the entire parish is slowly tilting. The congregation is smaller than it was. The young people leave. They go to Baton Rouge, to Houston, to Atlanta, to places where the ground does not betray the architecture.

The ones who stay are not staying out of ignorance. I want to be precise about this, because the narrative that accrues to places like Assumption Parish — rural, poor, Black, coastal — is a narrative of ignorance, and it is wrong, and the wrongness is important. Odette knew her house was sinking. She knew the levee was not a guarantee. She knew about the sinkhole in Bayou Corne, ten miles away, and she knew that the salt domes under her own property were not fundamentally different from the one that collapsed. She knew about the buyout program that FEMA offered after 2016, and she knew that the buyout offered her $68,000 for a house she'd lived in for four decades, in a market where $68,000 would purchase, in Baton Rouge, a down payment and a commute and the loss of everything she recognized as her life.

She stayed because the alternative was worse. Not because she didn't understand the risk. Because she understood the risk and also understood that the systems designed to mitigate it — the buyout programs, the relocation assistance, the elevation grants — were designed by people who did not know or did not care what it costs to leave a place. The cost is not only financial, though it is financial. The cost is the garden you have tended for thirty years, the church where you were baptized, the cemetery where your mother is buried in a ground that will, eventually, be underwater. The cost is the neighborhood that functions as an organism, where someone checks on you when you're sick and brings food when you're grieving and knows your name and your mother's name and the name of the man who built your porch. The cost is the self, the specific self that is produced by living in a specific place among specific people, and that cannot be recreated in a FEMA trailer park or a subsidized apartment in a city where no one knows you.

The people who design relocation programs understand displacement as a logistics problem. They count units. They calculate square footage. They measure the distance from the new site to the nearest grocery store and the nearest school and call it "access." They do not measure the distance from the new site to the cemetery, or the church, or the particular bend in the bayou where your grandfather taught you to fish, because those distances are not logistical. They are existential. And the people who bear them — the people who are moved — understand something that the people who move them do not: that being safe and being home are not the same thing, and that a country which forces you to choose between them has failed you twice.

Assumption Parish has lost 3,000 people since 2000. Not to a single catastrophe. To the steady erosion of livability — the jobs that left when the sugar mills closed, the insurance that was canceled when the flood maps were redrawn, the schools that consolidated when the enrollment dropped, the roads that washed out and took longer each time to repair. The levee holds. The parish empties anyway. The catastrophe is not the flood. The catastrophe is the century of decisions that makes the flood inevitable and the departure rational and the staying a kind of defiance that the rest of the country, when it notices at all, calls stubbornness.

## River-Side

In the geological record, the Mississippi River has changed course roughly every thousand years. The process is called avulsion, and it is driven by the same sediment mechanics that cause the channel to aggrade within the leveed system. As the river builds its channel higher — depositing silt on the bed and the natural levees — it perches above the surrounding floodplain, and eventually a flood breaches the bank and the water finds a shorter, steeper path to the Gulf. The old channel is abandoned. The new channel becomes the Mississippi.

The geological evidence for this is visible in the landscape if you know what to look at. Southern Louisiana is a palimpsest of abandoned channels — Bayou Lafourche, Bayou Teche, the Atchafalaya itself — each one a former course of the Mississippi, each one marked by a ridge of slightly higher ground (the old natural levee) flanked by backswamp. The bayous are the scars. The ridges are the memory. The current channel, running from Baton Rouge to New Orleans to the birdfoot delta, is the most recent path, occupied for roughly 600 years — a moderate tenancy by the river's standards. The Atchafalaya, 150 miles to the west, offers a route to the Gulf that is half the distance and three times the gradient. The river has been trying to take it for at least a century. The Corps has been stopping it for sixty years. Sixty years against a geological process that has been operating for 7,000.

I stood on the levee south of Donaldsonville and tried to hold two timescales in my mind at once. The first was the timescale of the river — millennia, avulsions, the slow continental drift of sediment toward the Gulf. In that frame, the levee was nothing. A momentary scab. The river would shed it the way a dog sheds water, with a shudder and a redirection and an indifference so total it was not even contempt. In the geological frame, the levee was not a defense. It was a delay, measured in decades, in a process measured in epochs.

The second timescale was human. In that frame, the levee was everything. It was the reason Donaldsonville existed, the reason the sugar mills had been built, the reason the chemical plants lined the river between Baton Rouge and New Orleans, the reason 2 million people lived in New Orleans, the reason the port system handled 500 million tons of cargo per year, the reason the United States had a functioning interior waterway connecting the Midwest to global markets. Remove the levee — conceptually, hydraulically — and you remove the premise of settlement. You return southern Louisiana to the river, and the river returns it to the swamp, and the swamp returns it to the Gulf, and the Gulf accepts it without comment.

The two timescales cannot be reconciled. That is the problem at the center of every attempt to control the Mississippi, and it is a problem that does not have a solution, only a management strategy. You cannot make the river permanent. You can only maintain the infrastructure that creates the impression of permanence, and you can maintain it for a while — a century, maybe two — before the maintenance becomes untenable, or the river exceeds the design, or the money runs out, or the political will dissolves, or the climate shifts the rainfall patterns and the flood frequency in ways the original engineers did not model because they could not have imagined them.

The word for all of this is "risk." But risk, in the engineering sense, is a probability multiplied by a consequence. The probability of levee failure at Donaldsonville in any given year is low — the Corps estimates it at less than 1 percent for a well-maintained section. The consequence is high — property damage, displacement, potential loss of life. The product — the risk — is a number that can be compared to other numbers, ranked, budgeted against. Risk is manageable.

What is not manageable — what the risk calculation excludes by design — is the question of who bears it. Risk, in the engineering formulation, is distributed evenly across the protected area. Everyone behind the levee faces the same probability of flooding. This is mathematically true and morally false. The probability is the same. The consequence is not. A family in a $400,000 house on higher ground with flood insurance and a second car and relatives in Baton Rouge faces a consequence that is categorically different from a family in a $68,000 house on subsiding ground without flood insurance, without a car, without anywhere to go. The same flood, the same probability, the same levee failure — and the outcomes are as different as the lives were before the water arrived.

## Land-Side

The history of the levee system in Louisiana begins with forced labor and has never entirely left it. The first levees were built by enslaved Africans under French colonial rule in the early eighteenth century. The Code Noir of 1724 required slaveholders to construct and maintain levees along the river frontage of their plantations. The labor was brutal — digging clay by hand, hauling it in baskets, compacting it with wooden tampers — and it was performed by people who had no stake in the land the levee protected, because they were the property of the people it protected.

After the Civil War, the levee labor shifted to convict leasing. Black men, arrested under vagrancy laws designed to criminalize the condition of being free and unemployed, were leased to levee contractors who worked them in conditions that differed from slavery primarily in their legal categorization. The death rates were appalling. The levee boards knew. The state knew. The arrangement continued because the levees had to be built and the labor had to be cheap and the people who benefited from the levees were not the people who died building them.

I mention this history not to make a metaphorical point but to make a structural one. The levee system, from its origin, was built by extracting labor and life from the people it least protected. That extraction did not end with convict leasing. It continued through the twentieth century in the form of land-use decisions — where the refineries went, where the waste ponds went, where the levees were highest and where they were lowest, where the pump stations were maintained and where they were allowed to degrade. Cancer Alley — the 85-mile stretch of petrochemical plants between Baton Rouge and New Orleans — sits behind the levees, on land that was cleared and drained and protected by the labor of enslaved people, and the communities downwind and downstream of those plants are, overwhelmingly, the descendants of the people who cleared and drained and protected it.

This is not coincidence. This is continuity. And the levee — standing between the river and the landscape it enabled — is the physical fact that makes the continuity possible. Without the levee, no plantation. Without the plantation, no refinery. Without the refinery, no Cancer Alley. Without Cancer Alley, no elevated cancer rates in St. James Parish, no litigation, no community meetings in church basements where people hold up photographs of their dead and ask the representatives of multinational corporations to explain the particular cruelty of being poisoned by an industry that exists because your ancestors were forced to make the ground it stands on habitable.

The levee doesn't cause any of this directly. The levee is dirt. Clay, silt, compacted to 95 percent standard Proctor density, planted in Bermuda grass, inspected twice a year. The levee is infrastructure. But infrastructure is not neutral. Infrastructure is a decision about what is possible, and decisions about what is possible are decisions about who benefits, and in Louisiana, the decisions have been consistent for three hundred years, and the levee is the physical form of that consistency — the shape it takes in earth and grass and river water.

## River-Side

During the flood of 2011, the Morganza Floodway — a controlled spillway north of the Old River structures — was opened for only the second time since its construction in 1954. The opening was authorized by the president of the Mississippi River Commission, a major general in the Army Corps of Engineers, who made the decision after determining that the flood would otherwise overtop the levees protecting Baton Rouge and New Orleans. The Morganza Floodway is designed to divert floodwater into the Atchafalaya Basin, relieving pressure on the mainline levees. When it was opened, 25,000 people lived in the floodway's inundation zone. They were evacuated. Their property was flooded. Their sacrifice — the Corps uses the word "sacrifice" in its technical documents, without apparent discomfort — protected the cities downstream.

The sacrifice areas are built into the system. They are designed. The Morganza Floodway, the Bonnet Carré Spillway above New Orleans, the Birds Point-New Madrid Floodway in Missouri — these are places where flooding is planned, where the levees are deliberately lower or absent, where the land is designated for inundation so that other land can remain dry. The people who live in sacrifice areas know they live in sacrifice areas, mostly. They receive compensation, sometimes. They return and rebuild, usually. They accept the bargain because the alternative — no levee system at all — would be worse.

But the word "sacrifice" should not pass without scrutiny. A sacrifice, in the moral sense, requires consent. The ram on the altar does not consent. The soldier at the front did not choose the war. The family in the Morganza Floodway did not design the system that designates their home as expendable. They were placed there by the same accumulation of economic and racial history that placed Odette behind the levee in Plattenville — the restricted housing, the limited employment, the land that was available because it was risky and therefore cheap. The sacrifice is not freely given. It is extracted, as the labor was extracted, as the land was extracted, as the wealth of the Mississippi Valley was extracted from the people who worked it and deposited, like alluvial silt, in the cities and portfolios of people who never see the river except from an airplane.

## Land-Side

I went back to Odette's porch in the afternoon. She had made coffee — the dark roast, chicory-laced coffee that is one of the few things in Louisiana that has remained constant across three centuries of upheaval — and she poured it into mismatched cups and set them on the railing and sat back down.

"My grandmother was born in this parish," she said. "My great-grandmother. My people have been here since before the Civil War. Since during. You understand? We didn't come here. We were brought here. And then we stayed because it was the only here we had."

She looked at the levee. From her porch, it was a ridge of green against the sky, maybe two hundred yards away, high enough to block the view of the river. She could not see the water from her house. She had never been able to see the water from her house. The levee stood between her and the thing that could destroy her, and it also stood between her and any visible reminder that the thing existed.

"They came around after 2016," she said. "FEMA people. Clipboard people. Told me I was in a flood zone, which I already knew, because I was standing in a flood. Told me I could apply for a buyout. I asked how much. They said they'd assess the property. It took eight months. Eight months to tell me my house was worth sixty-eight thousand dollars. I said, sixty-eight thousand? I've put more than that into this house in thirty years. They said that's the fair market value. I said, fair to who?"

Fair to who. It is a question the levee system does not ask, because the levee system is not a moral instrument. It is a hydraulic instrument. It manages water. It does not manage justice. It does not measure the differential impact of its failure on populations separated by income, by race, by the accumulated weight of three centuries of decisions made by people who will never stand on Odette's porch and look at the green ridge against the sky and know that behind it is a volume of water sufficient to erase everything she has.

The levee does not know any of this. The levee is earth. But the people who built it knew. The people who maintain it know. The people who set its height and determine its alignment and decide which sections are reinforced and which are deferred — they know. They know that the levee south of Donaldsonville protects land that is sinking, in a parish that is shrinking, behind a wall that was designed for a river that no longer exists because the wall itself has changed it. They know that the people behind this section of levee are poorer and Blacker and older than the people behind the levees protecting the petrochemical plants upriver. They know that when the freeboard narrows to eleven inches and the sandbags go up at two in the morning, the people filling the sandbags are not the people who profit from the land the sandbags protect.

They know all of this, and the levee holds, and another year passes, and the reports say "adequate," and the maintenance continues, and the river rises another fraction of an inch in its channel, and the land sinks another fraction of an inch behind the wall, and the distance between the two — the freeboard, the margin, the gap between the water and the air — closes by an increment so small it is invisible in any single year and irreversible across a lifetime.

---

## River-Side

The Mississippi will take the Atchafalaya. Not this year. Probably not this decade. But the math is the math, and the math says that the gradient advantage increases every year, and the structures age, and the floods test them, and one day a flood will come that is larger than the design flood — not because it is unprecedented but because it was always possible and the design chose not to accommodate it — and the structures will fail, and the river will go west.

When that happens, the levee south of Donaldsonville will become irrelevant. Not because it will fail — it may hold — but because the river inside it will diminish. The flow that now passes Donaldsonville at 600,000 cubic feet per second will drop to a fraction of that. The channel will silt in. The water will turn brackish as the Gulf tide pushes upriver through the reduced flow. The refineries will close. The port will close. The purpose of the levee — to contain the Mississippi — will dissolve, because the Mississippi will no longer be there to contain.

And the people behind the levee, in Plattenville and Belle Rose and Bayou L'Ourse, will still be there. On sinking ground, in a parish without a tax base, behind a wall that protects them from nothing, in houses their grandmothers built in a country that promised the ground would hold.

---

## Land-Side

I asked Odette what she thought would happen.

"Same thing that always happens," she said. "The water comes, or it doesn't. The levee holds, or it doesn't. Somebody on television says it's a hundred-year flood, which means it's not supposed to happen for another hundred years, which means everybody acts surprised when it happens again in five. And the people up in Baton Rouge, they make their decisions, and we live with them. That's how it's always been."

She finished her coffee and set the cup on the railing.

"My granddaughter," she said. "She's in Houston now. Nursing program. Smart girl. She calls me every Sunday. She says, Grandma, why don't you come here. I say, baby, I am here. I've been here. Where am I going to go that's more here than here?"

The levee stood behind her, green and enormous, holding back a river she couldn't see. On the other side, the brown water moved south, carrying the topsoil of thirty-one states toward a gulf that was rising to meet it. Between the water and the woman, nine feet of compacted clay — inspected, maintained, adequate.

Freeboard.

The distance between the surface of what is coming and the top of what we have built to hold it back. Measured in feet. Measured in years. Measured in the lives of the people who live in the margin and call it home, because no one has offered them anything else, and the margin is shrinking, and the river does not care, and the people who built the wall are not the people behind it, and the wall was never built for the people behind it, and everyone knows this, and the wall holds, and another year passes, and the freeboard narrows, and the reports say adequate, and the distance closes, and the water rises, and the ground sinks, and the margin between what is coming and what we have built is the distance across a small bedroom, the height of a ceiling, the width of a man's shoe — and then, one spring, it is nothing at all.
