The flyer was printed on goldenrod paper — the color of notices about street sweeping and rabies clinics. It was pinned to the community board at the public library, between a missing cat poster and an advertisement for gutter cleaning, and it said: *Adult Recorder Ensemble — Beginners Welcome — Tuesdays 7–8:30 PM*.

"Beginners welcome" is the saddest two-word phrase in the English language, after "open casket" and "some assembly." It means: we know what you are, and we'll take you anyway. I stood there looking at this flyer for longer than was reasonable — long enough that a man returning audiobooks gave me the look you give someone who might be lost or might be about to make a scene. I tore off one of the phone number tabs at the bottom. There were seven others. None had been torn.

I told no one. The humiliation, if it was going to be a humiliation, would be private first. I did not mention it to my wife, my coworkers, the barista who makes my coffee every morning and already knows more about my habits than most of my friends. There are things you do in middle age that you can't explain without sounding like you're either having a crisis or joining a cult, and signing up for adult recorder class sits comfortably in both categories.

The recorder arrived from Amazon two days later in a box that could have held a large candy bar. A Yamaha YRS-24B, cream-colored plastic, $8.99 including shipping. I took it out of the packaging and held it and felt approximately nothing — no surge of possibility, no dread, just the mild confusion of holding an object that weighs less than your phone and is supposedly a musical instrument. It had the gravitas of a hotel pen. I put it on the kitchen counter next to the fruit bowl, where it sat for two days looking like something a child had left behind after a visit.

The room, the first Tuesday: fluorescent lights, folding chairs, the institutional cologne of old coffee and floor wax. A community center that doubled as a polling place in November and a blood drive site in March. Eight adults, seven recorders. The eighth person, a man in a flannel shirt who looked like he'd come directly from refinishing a deck, had forgotten his and was given a loaner from a plastic bin near the door. The bin also contained three badminton shuttlecocks and what appeared to be a single ski glove.

I cataloged my classmates the way you catalog the other passengers on a delayed flight — with the false intimacy of people stuck in a room together for reasons no one would have chosen if anything better were available. There was a retired postal worker named Gil who held his recorder like a cigar and kept adjusting his chair as though the problem were the chair. A young woman in hospital scrubs who had clearly come straight from a shift and who sat with the particular stillness of someone conserving the last of their energy for something voluntary. A man in a blazer — a blazer, to recorder class — who introduced himself as "a tax accountant, if anyone needs one," as though the rest of us had been waiting for exactly this information. And a teenager, maybe fifteen, who had been brought by her mother and who was already better than everyone. You could tell because she held the recorder with the casual contempt of a person for whom this instrument was not a challenge but a chore, the way a chess grandmaster might hold a checkerboard.

I sat in the middle of the second row. Close enough to be seen, far enough to pretend I wasn't trying. This is, I have come to believe, the defining posture of my adult life.

The teacher arrived. She was not what I had prepared for. I had prepared for one of two types: the washed-up musician with residual bitterness, or the cheerful retiree running out the clock on a hobby. She was neither. A woman in her late fifties with short gray hair and a lanyard, and on the lanyard, a small plastic recorder charm, the kind of thing you'd find in a gumball machine. I found this ridiculous. I would later find it something else, though I'm not sure what.

She introduced herself as Diane. She did not make a joke. She did not acknowledge that the recorder is funny, or that we were funny for being there, or that the situation had any comedy in it at all. She was earnest in a way that had no irony in it whatsoever, and encountering a person with zero irony is like encountering a person who doesn't carry a phone — technically possible, vaguely unsettling, and secretly admirable in a way you'd never admit.

"Let's start with B," she said.

She lifted her recorder and played a note. It was clean. It had a center to it, a quality of intention that I didn't know a recorder could produce. I had last heard a recorder at my nephew's school concert, where thirty second-graders had played "Mary Had a Little Lamb" with the collective musicality of a car alarm. What Diane played was not that. What Diane played was a sound that belonged in a room on purpose. The rest of us got quiet in a way that rooms don't usually get quiet for a recorder.

"Your turn," she said.

Eight adults raised cream-colored plastic to their lips. What emerged was a sound I can only describe as eight people simultaneously stepping on a duck. Not the same duck — eight different ducks, at eight slightly different moments, producing a chord that existed in no known musical tradition. Gil's duck was flat. The teenager's duck was fine, actually, which made the rest of the ducks worse by comparison. My own duck was sharp, and I learned something about myself in that moment, which is that even my failures have an anxious, overachieving quality — trying too hard, overshooting, arriving at the wrong note from the wrong direction.

"That's a start," Diane said, and she meant it. That was the thing. She meant it.

I looked down at my fingers on the holes — three on the left hand, four on the right, plus the thumb underneath, a configuration I had read about on the bus ride over in the way a person reads about skydiving the morning of. The problem, I was discovering, was that I couldn't feel whether my fingers had sealed the holes. I could see them covering the openings, but when I closed my eyes and played, the note split into a shriek — my left ring finger hovering a millimeter above the tone hole, close enough to look right, far enough to ruin everything.

"Don't look," Diane said. "Feel."

But the feeling was lying. I had been operating these hands for forty-five years — typing, cooking, buttoning shirts, opening jars, gesturing during arguments about parking — and I had accumulated, over those decades, a bodily confidence that I now understood to be fraudulent. Proprioception, the word is. The body's sense of where it is without looking. I'd been walking around believing I knew where my fingers ended and the air began, and the recorder had revealed, in the time it takes to play a single B, that I did not. You cover a hole with the pad of your ring finger and the hole says: not quite. A millimeter. You are a millimeter away from where you think you are, and a millimeter, it turns out, is everything.

"The recorder has been around since the fourteenth century," Diane said, while we struggled. She said this the way a museum docent mentions a date — not to impress, but because the fact is there and seems worth having. "A man named Silvestro Ganassi published the first manual for it in Venice in 1535. A hundred and fifty pages on how to make it imitate the human voice."

I looked at the $8.99 Yamaha in my hands. A Venetian court musician, nearly five hundred years ago, had written a treatise — a treatise — on how to make this instrument sound like a person singing. The ambition of it. The seriousness. And here I was in a community center with fluorescent lighting, unable to produce a clean B, holding the plastic descendant of something that had once mattered enough for a book. The recorder is the only instrument with a pedigree it can't live up to.

"Let's try B again," Diane said.

We tried B again.

Week two: "Hot Cross Buns." Three notes. B, A, G. A melody so simple it barely qualifies as a melody — more of a suggestion, a melodic rumor. The tax accountant got it on his third attempt and looked at the rest of us with an expression I recognized from every meeting I've ever been in where someone finishes their part of the presentation first. The retired postal worker got it on his sixth try. I got it on my eighth, and the pride I felt — the genuine, chest-expanding, nearly tearful pride — was so wildly disproportionate to the achievement that it immediately became embarrassing. It was the feeling you get when you parallel park on the first try and want to tell someone, except the someone would have to care about parallel parking, and the thing you'd actually accomplished was "Hot Cross Buns" on a plastic recorder, and you are a person with a mortgage and a dentist and opinions about wine.

I practiced at home that week, in the car, in the driveway, with the windows up and the engine off. "Hot Cross Buns," over and over, in a parked Subaru, at nine-thirty at night, like a man conducting a stakeout on his own mediocrity. I did not practice in the house. The house has other people in it, and those people have opinions, and I was not yet ready to have my relationship to this instrument witnessed by anyone who knew me before I became a person who practices the recorder in driveways. There is a particular loneliness to being bad at something in a car by yourself. It is not the loneliness of isolation. It is the loneliness of knowing that you are choosing this and cannot say why.

Week three: the teenager stopped coming. I was relieved, and then ashamed of the relief, and then briefly ashamed of the shame, which is the kind of recursive emotional nesting doll that makes adults so exhausting. Gil started bringing homemade oatmeal cookies that were dry but generous — the work of a man who bakes by conviction rather than talent, which is a posture I was beginning to recognize. The woman in scrubs — her name was Patrice — told me during a break that she was a nurse in the ICU. "I took this class," she said, peeling the plastic wrap off a cookie, "because I needed something where nobody's life depended on whether I got it right."

I didn't know what to say to that. I wanted to make a joke — something about the recorder being incapable of sustaining life, something that would fold her sentence back into the comedy where I could manage it. But the joke wouldn't form, and I nodded instead, and she went back to her chair and played a G that was almost perfect.

Week four: we attempted a simple duet. The tax accountant and I, side by side, playing what was supposed to be the same thing at the same time. We were not playing the same thing at the same time. He was slightly ahead, I was slightly behind, and what came out of our two recorders was not unison but a wobble — the musical equivalent of two people trying to walk through a doorway simultaneously and both stepping left.

"You'll find each other," Diane said.

I wrote that down when I got home. Not because it was profound — it wasn't, or it shouldn't have been. It was a music teacher telling two adults that their rhythm would improve. But I kept hearing it as something else. *You'll find each other.* The question was whether she was right or whether she'd been saying it for eleven years because it was easier than saying: some people wobble forever.

There's a way we talk about being a beginner that treats it as a location on a map — a town you drive through on the way to somewhere worth stopping. Beginner, intermediate, advanced. The words assume a direction. You begin, and then you move, and the moving is the point. But I had been sitting in Diane's folding chair for four Tuesdays, making sounds that ranged from "almost a note" to "furniture being dragged across a gymnasium floor," and I was beginning to suspect that beginnership is not a town you drive through. It might be a country. Some people live there. Diane, who had been teaching this class for eleven years — whose lanyard charm was worn smooth from handling, who said "That's a start" the way other people say "good morning" — had found something in the permanent state of starting over that I could not identify and could not dismiss. It wasn't patience. It wasn't kindness, though she was kind without performance. It was something I don't have a word for, and the not-having-a-word-for-it was, if I'm honest, more threatening than anything else I'd encountered in that room, including my own

"Let's try the B-flat," she said.

The last class I'll tell you about — not the last class, because there were more, but the last one that belongs in this essay — we played "Ode to Joy." A simplified arrangement. Diane had handed out photocopied sheet music with the notes written above the staff in letters, the way you'd label a map for a child, which is what we were. A new woman had joined — a grandmother type who said she'd always wanted to try and whose reason for waiting until now she did not provide and no one asked for, because by week five we had all learned that the reasons people give for being here are never the real reasons.

It took us eleven tries. On the twelfth, we got through it. The sound that came out of our seven recorders bore the same relationship to Beethoven that a stick figure bears to a person. You could tell what it was supposed to be, but only because you already knew. It had the shape of "Ode to Joy" without any of the joy, or rather with a different kind of joy — the joy of people who have done something badly but completely, which is its own category and which I don't think has a name, though it should.

Gil put down his recorder and said, "That was something."

"That was something," the tax accountant confirmed. He was smiling. I realized I'd never seen him smile before, and that the blazer had been gone since week three, replaced by a fleece pullover that made him look like the same person with less to prove.

Patrice didn't say anything. She was looking at her recorder with an expression I'd seen on the faces of people leaving movie theaters after a film they haven't decided about yet.

"Same time next week," Diane said.

We packed up. I put the Yamaha in my bag — in the outside pocket, where it fit perfectly, the way it would fit in a child's backpack, because it was designed for a child's backpack. Gil offered me a cookie for the road. The tax accountant gave me his card. I took both. He'd given me a card every week. I now had five of them and no tax questions, but throwing away someone's card felt like a small act of cruelty I wasn't prepared to commit, so they lived in my coat pocket, accumulating.

I was still, by any measure that mattered, a beginner. I could play "Hot Cross Buns" and a halting version of "Ode to Joy" that would not be recognized as joyful by anyone who hadn't been in that room. My left ring finger still drifted above the tone hole when I wasn't looking. I had not solved the proprioception problem. I had not solved any problem. What I had done was show up five times to a room with fluorescent lights and folding chairs and make sounds that a Venetian court musician in 1535 would have classified as an insult to his life's work, and I had done this in front of strangers, and I was going to do it again next Tuesday, and the Tuesday after that. I had five business cards from a tax accountant in my coat pocket and no tax questions, and I still couldn't play B-flat.
