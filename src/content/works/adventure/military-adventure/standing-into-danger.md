## I. The Captain

The French corvette was hull-up on the larboard quarter by four bells of the afternoon watch, and Captain James Renwick had been expecting her for eleven hours.

Not her specifically. He had been expecting something — a sail, a signal, a piece of the picture that would confirm or deny the intelligence he had received at Plymouth, which was itself incomplete, as intelligence always was. The Dorade, eighteen guns, last reported victualling at Brest in October. Possibly bound for the West Indies. Possibly operating as a commerce raider in the Western Approaches. Possibly — and this was the word that sat in Renwick's stomach like ballast improperly stowed — possibly accompanied.

He stood at the weather rail of the quarterdeck with his glass to his eye and studied the corvette's sail plan with the methodical attention he brought to everything. A neat ship. Well-trimmed. Carrying a press of canvas for a vessel working to windward in a freshening southwesterly, which told him something about her captain: either confident or pressed for time, and the two conditions produced identical sail plans. Eighteen guns, if the intelligence was correct. The Dorade carried twelve-pounder long guns and could throw a broadside of ninety-six pounds. His own ship, the Tenacious, twenty-eight guns, threw two hundred and fifty-two pounds per broadside and had the weather gage. The arithmetic was in his favour. Renwick had spent his career ensuring the arithmetic was in his favour, because he had learned early that the captain who trusted to luck was the captain who eventually ran out of it.

The quarterdeck was quiet. Mr. Hale, the first lieutenant, stood at the larboard rail and said nothing, which was his habit when the captain was working through a tactical problem. Mr. Flood, the master, was at the binnacle, checking the compass bearing against the chart he had been annotating since dawn with the small, precise handwriting of a man who believed that a well-drawn chart was a species of prayer. The midshipmen kept to their stations and watched Renwick the way young dogs watch the lead hound: for signals, for permission, for some indication of what was about to happen.

Renwick lowered the glass. The corvette had altered course — two points to starboard, tightening her angle relative to the Tenacious. This was either preparation for an engagement or preparation for flight, and for a vessel of eighteen guns facing a vessel of twenty-eight, flight was the rational choice. That she had not fled already told him something. He filed it away with the other fragments — her sail trim, her heading, her presence in this stretch of water at this time of year — and waited for the picture to resolve.

It did not resolve. It never did. This was the thing Renwick had learned in twenty-three years of service that no amount of study or experience could compensate for: the picture never resolved completely. You gathered intelligence, you observed, you calculated, you applied everything you had learned from every engagement and every book and every conversation with every officer who had fought before you, and at the end of it all you were left with a picture that was perhaps ninety percent complete, and the remaining ten percent was darkness, and in that darkness was the thing that would kill your men.

He turned to the first lieutenant. "Mr. Hale. Clear for action, if you please."

## II. The First Lieutenant

Hale had been waiting for the order the way a coiled spring waits for the catch to release: not with impatience but with the specific tension of a mechanism held in readiness. He touched his hat — "Aye aye, sir" — and turned to the business of transforming the Tenacious from a vessel at sea into a vessel at war.

The process was a system. Hale did not use that word, would not have recognized it as such, but the thing itself was as systematic as any procedure in a factory or a hospital. The marine drummer beat to quarters. The bosun's mates blew their calls — the particular shrill pattern that meant clear for action, distinguishable from every other pattern in the bosun's vocabulary of whistles, though to an untrained ear they were all the same shriek. And then the ship moved.

Hale oversaw it from the waist, walking the length of the gun deck as the crew fell into the choreography they had drilled three times a week since leaving Plymouth. The bulkheads of the captain's great cabin came down, the men moving through the captain's quarters with the practiced efficiency of stagehands dismantling a set between acts, rolling the carpets, stowing the chairs, running the stern chasers into their ports. The carpenter and his crew went below to the orlop to prepare shot plugs and fothering material. The gunner's mates opened the magazines and began passing cartridges through the felt-lined scuttles. Sand was spread on the gun deck to keep footing when the blood came.

There were one hundred and eighty-seven men aboard the Tenacious, and in clearing for action every one of them had a station, a task, a place in the sequence. Hale tracked the sequence the way a conductor tracks an orchestra: not by watching every player but by listening for the absences, the missed entries, the places where the rhythm broke. A gun crew on the larboard side was slow in casting loose their eighteen-pounder — the breechings were stiff with salt, the gun captain a replacement who had come aboard at Plymouth and had not yet fully learned the particular stiffness of this particular gun's tackle. Hale noted it, said nothing. The time to correct a gun crew was during drill, not during clearing for action.

The marines formed up on the poop deck, their red coats vivid against the grey sky — absurdly vivid, Hale always thought, as if designed to attract fire, which in a sense they were. Lieutenant Elcott was checking his men's muskets with the fastidious dissatisfaction of a man who trusted nothing he had not personally inspected.

"Ship cleared for action, sir," Hale reported to the quarterdeck. Eleven minutes. Two minutes slower than their best time and one minute faster than their average. He added none of this. He reported readiness and returned to his station.

The corvette was closer now. Perhaps three miles. Hale could see her hull clearly — painted black with a white stripe along the gun ports, common enough in the French Navy. She had set her topgallants, which meant she had decided not to flee. Hale did not know what this meant, because the determination of what things meant was the captain's business, and the first lieutenant's business was to make certain that whatever the captain decided, the ship could execute it.

He checked the gun deck one more time. The guns were cast loose, the cartridges were up, the shot was in the garlands, the slow match was lit in its tubs in case the flintlocks failed. The readiness was the thing Hale could control, and he controlled it with a thoroughness that left nothing to chance except the things that were always left to chance, which were the things that killed you.

## III. The Pressed Man

Thomas Dalton had been aboard the Tenacious for thirty-one days and still could not sleep through the change of watch without startling awake with the conviction that the ship was sinking. He was from Shoreditch. He had been a weaver's assistant. He had been taken off a merchantman in the Downs by a press gang that had treated the entire transaction with the bored competence of men collecting debts, and now he was a landsman aboard a twenty-eight-gun frigate in the Western Approaches, assigned to the afterguard, which meant he hauled on ropes when told to haul and let go when told to let go, and the distinction between the ropes was a mystery he was solving by the slow accumulation of bruises and curses.

He did not understand the ship. He understood that there were ropes and sails and guns and that these things worked together to make the ship move and fight, and he understood that the men around him understood these things the way he understood the threading of a loom — from the inside, from the hands. The topmen going aloft in weather that would have kept a sensible man on the ground, their feet finding the ratlines without looking, their bodies accommodating the ship's roll the way a dancer accommodates music. The gun crews moving through the loading sequence — sponge, cartridge, wad, shot, wad, run out — with a coordination that was not drilled into them so much as grown, the way a tree grows around a fence post.

Clearing for action. Dalton had drilled this twice before, and each time the transformation of the ship struck him as astonishing. Three hundred tons of wood and hemp and iron converted from a habitation into a weapon in minutes. The captain's cabin — which Dalton had seen once, when he was brought before Renwick for the formality of being read into the ship's books — vanished. The partitions came down, the furniture was struck below, and where there had been a room with books and a table and a decanter there were now two twelve-pounder guns pointing out through open ports at the grey water.

He was assigned to the number twelve gun, starboard battery, as a loader. His job was to take the cartridge from the powder boy and place it in the muzzle when the sponger had finished. He had practiced this many times and could do it adequately, though the gun captain — a Cornishman named Trelawney who had been at sea since the age of nine and who regarded Dalton with the weary patience of a man teaching a goat to dance — had more than once corrected his grip, his stance, his timing, and, on one occasion, his breathing.

What Dalton noticed, which the others did not seem to notice because it was the medium they lived in the way fish live in water, was the silence. Not literal silence — the ship was never silent, the rigging hummed and the hull creaked and the sea worked against the copper sheathing with a sound like something large being dragged — but conversational silence. The men did not talk about what was coming. The French corvette was visible to anyone who looked over the rail, and every man on the ship had looked, and no one was discussing what it meant. Not from ignorance — Dalton could see in their faces that they knew, that the knowledge of what was coming was distributed evenly through the crew like heat through metal — but from some agreement, unspoken and apparently universal, that acknowledging the coming violence aloud would be an offence against something Dalton could not name.

He found this more frightening than the corvette.

## IV. The Surgeon

Mr. Fenton laid out his instruments on the midshipmen's sea chest that served as his operating table and counted them the way he always counted them: by touch, with his eyes closed, testing the edge of each blade with his thumb. Three catlings of graduated size for amputation. A capital saw with a spare blade. Tenaculum forceps. Bullet extractors. Tourniquets of leather and brass. Ligature silk. A case of needles. Two buckets — one for water, one for what the water would eventually contain.

The cockpit was on the orlop deck, below the waterline, which made it the safest place on the ship and also the worst: airless, lightless except for the two lanterns he had hung from the deck beams, stinking of bilge water and tar and the particular sweetish smell of the bread room forward, where the ship's biscuit was stored and where the weevils conducted their own slow campaign of attrition. Fenton had set up in this space eleven times across three ships and four years of war, and each time the space shrank a little in his estimation, not because the dimensions changed but because his knowledge of what would happen in it grew more detailed.

His loblolly boy — a marine private named Sykes who had the temperament for the work, meaning he did not faint and could hold a man down — was filling the water bucket from a cask they had rolled forward from the hold. The Tenacious did not carry a chaplain, which suited Fenton. Chaplains in the cockpit during an engagement occupied space, generated noise, and provided comfort of a variety Fenton regarded as counterproductive: a man being told that God loved him while his leg was being removed might legitimately wonder about the quality of that love.

Fenton was a competent man, which he had concluded was worth more to the people in his care than either religion or compassion. Compassion made you slow. It made you linger over a case that could not be saved while a case that could be saved bled to death in the queue. Fenton triaged by probability of survival, and he was ruthless about it, and the men who survived because he was ruthless about it did not thank him, because they did not know about the men he had set aside.

He finished counting. Everything was present. Everything was clean, or as clean as the orlop deck permitted, which was not very clean by the standards of the Edinburgh surgical school where he had trained but was clean enough by the standards of a frigate at war. He sat on a powder keg and waited.

Waiting was the surgeon's engagement. While the officers manoeuvred and the gun crews loaded and the marines formed up, the surgeon sat in his hole below the waterline and listened to the ship above him — the feet, the shouted orders, the particular quality of silence that preceded the first broadside — and he prepared himself for the receipts. This was the word he used, privately, for what would arrive: the receipts. The officers conducted the transaction. The surgeon received the invoice. And the invoice was always, without exception, higher than anyone had estimated.

## V. The Master

Mr. Flood took the sounding at six bells and found twelve fathoms, sand and broken shell, which agreed with his chart to within a quarter of a fathom and confirmed their position as four leagues south-southwest of the Ile d'Ouessant — Ushant, as the English insisted on calling it, and Flood, though English himself, preferred the French name because it was the name on the chart he trusted, a French chart captured from a prize off Lorient in '97 and annotated in Flood's hand with every sounding, current, and tidal set he had observed in six passages through these waters.

The chart told him things the lookout could not. The set of the current here ran east-northeast at three-quarters of a knot on the flood, which favoured the corvette's approach. The wind was backing — had been backing since the forenoon watch, slowly, the kind of shift you measured not in minutes but in hours, a southwesterly moving toward south-southwest, which would, if it continued, give the corvette a freer wind and reduce the Tenacious's advantage of the weather gage. The depth was shoaling gradually — not dangerously, not yet, but the Ushant approaches were a tangle of rocks and overfalls and the tide race off the Chaussee de Sein to the south could turn a following sea into a confusion of breaking water in which no ship could fight effectively.

Flood had served under captains who ignored charts and captains who worshipped them, and Captain Renwick was neither. He was a captain who read charts the way a physician reads a pulse — as data, as one input among many, to be weighed against the evidence of the eye, the barometer, and the accumulated experience of a life at sea. Renwick asked questions. He asked what the bottom composition suggested about the current. He asked whether the glass was falling or steady. He asked what Flood's chart showed about the approaches from the northeast, where the corvette had appeared, and whether the depth there would permit a vessel of deeper draught — a frigate, say, or a small ship of the line — to follow the same track.

This last question was the one that stayed with Flood as he returned to the binnacle and laid out his parallel rulers on the chart. A vessel of deeper draught following the corvette's track. Renwick had not elaborated, and Flood had not asked, because asking the captain to explain his tactical reasoning was not done, but the question implied a possibility that Flood found uncomfortable: that the corvette might not be alone.

The intelligence from Plymouth had been vague. Flood had not seen the dispatches — the dispatches were the captain's business — but he had heard, in the way that information percolated through a ship despite every effort to contain it, that the Dorade had been reported operating in company. In company with what was the question that the dispatches had apparently not answered. Or had answered, and the answer had been equivocal. Or had answered, and the answer had not reached Plymouth before the Tenacious sailed. There were many possible explanations for the absence of a piece of intelligence, and they ranged from bureaucratic delay to catastrophic oversight, and there was no way to distinguish between them from the quarterdeck of a frigate four leagues south of Ushant with a French corvette closing on the larboard quarter.

Flood took another sounding. Eleven and three-quarter fathoms. The bottom was shoaling faster than the chart predicted, which meant either the chart was wrong — possible but unlikely, given six passages of annotation — or the tide was running harder than expected, pushing them shoreward. He adjusted the dead reckoning and said nothing to the captain, because the deviation was within the margin he considered normal, and because the captain had enough to consider without being told that the sea floor was six inches closer than expected.

## VI. The Dinner

The dinner in the great cabin was laid at the second dog watch, four hours before the engagement, when the corvette was still hull-down and the evening light was doing the thing that evening light does in the Western Approaches in autumn: turning the sea from grey to copper and making everything — the sails, the rigging, the faces of the men — look as though it had been varnished with something warm and slightly false.

The cabin had been reassembled — bulkheads re-erected, the captain's table laid with the silver-plate service that was Renwick's one indulgence, inherited from his father, who had been a captain before him and had died ashore. The steward had produced a creditable meal: a haunch of fresh pork from the last provisions taken aboard at Plymouth, roasted with the ship's remaining onions; a pudding of suet and raisins that was more suet than raisin but was hot; a Stilton cheese that had survived the voyage in reasonable condition; and two bottles of a Madeira that Renwick had been saving for a moment that deserved it.

Hale was present, and Flood, and Lieutenant Elcott of the marines, and Fenton the surgeon, who attended the captain's table by invitation and who drank his wine with the concentrated appreciation of a man who expected to be very busy in the morning. The conversation avoided the corvette with an elegance that was itself a form of communication. Flood described the coastal geology of Ushant — the gneiss and granite, the caves where seals bred, a species of lichen he had observed on the northern cliffs that grew in a pattern resembling a chart of the tidal currents, which he thought was coincidental but which the naturalists might find interesting. Renwick asked questions about the lichen with apparent genuine interest, which may have been genuine, because Renwick was interested in most things, or may have been the captain's performance of normality, and the two were indistinguishable.

Elcott told a story about a fellow marine officer who had, during the blockade of Cadiz, attempted to train the ship's goat to stand sentry and had succeeded to the point where the goat would present hooves at the approach of any officer above the rank of lieutenant, which had been amusing until the goat headbutted the post captain and had to be confined to the manger. The table laughed. The laughter was real — it was a good story, Elcott told it well — and the reality of the laughter existed simultaneously with the knowledge that the men laughing would be trying to kill other men in the morning, and neither condition diminished the other.

Fenton said little. He ate methodically, drank his wine, and watched the others with the diagnostic attention that was his professional habit and that made him, at social occasions, a slightly unnerving presence. He was cataloguing, though he would not have described it that way. He was noting the position of each man at the table relative to the hull, and calculating — not consciously, but in the way that a surgeon's mind calculates without being asked — the likely trajectory of splinters if a ball struck the stern at this angle, and which of the men at the table would be most exposed.

The pudding was served. Renwick poured the last of the Madeira and raised his glass.

"Gentlemen. To a fair wind and a clean action."

They drank. The toast was the conventional one and the convention was the point: a formula of words that committed nothing, predicted nothing, and acknowledged the coming engagement without naming what it would cost. Renwick set down his glass and looked at each man at the table, and in his look was the thing he could not say, which was that he had brought them here and would lead them into action in the morning and that some of them might die because of decisions he was about to make on the basis of information he did not fully possess, and that this was the compact of service, and the compact did not include an apology.

"That will do, I think," he said. "Mr. Hale, you have the watch."

The cabin cleared. The bulkheads came down again. The silver was stowed. The space that had been, for an hour, a room where men ate and laughed and discussed lichen became again a bare platform between two twelve-pounder guns, and the evening darkened over the water, and the corvette's lights appeared on the horizon like something a man had dropped and could not recover.

## VII. The Engagement

Dawn came grey and without ceremony. The wind had shifted in the night — south-southwest now, as Flood had feared, reducing their windward advantage — and the corvette had used the dark hours to close the distance. She was two miles off the larboard bow at first light, running on a parallel course, and as the light strengthened Renwick could see through his glass that she had run out her guns.

"She means to fight," Hale said.

"She does." Renwick studied the corvette's position. Two miles. At their current rates of closure, they would be within effective range in forty minutes. The sea state was moderate — a long swell from the southwest, four or five feet, enough to make gunnery interesting but not impossible. The sky was overcast and low, which limited visibility to perhaps six miles, which was relevant if there was another ship in the area, and Renwick did not know whether there was another ship in the area, and this was the thing he could not resolve.

He had the dispatches. He had read them three times. They described the Dorade's movements since September, drawn from signal intelligence and the reports of fishing boats and the occasional captured document, and they were detailed and they were thorough and they contained, somewhere in the confident prose of the intelligence officer who had compiled them, a gap. Not a stated gap — no one had written "we do not know." The gap was structural: the dispatches tracked the Dorade from Brest to the Bay of Biscay and back, noting her cruising pattern, her likely armament, her captain's known tendencies — and they said nothing about what else had sailed from Brest in October, because either nothing else had sailed or the intelligence officer did not know or the information had been omitted because it was not considered relevant to a twenty-eight-gun frigate engaging an eighteen-gun corvette, which was, by the arithmetic, a foregone conclusion.

The arithmetic. Renwick had the arithmetic. Two hundred and fifty-two pounds against ninety-six. He also had the weather gage, though it was eroding. He had a well-drilled crew, fresh provisions, sound rigging, and a clean copper bottom. He had every advantage except certainty, and certainty was the one advantage that no amount of competence could manufacture.

"We will engage from the windward, Mr. Hale. Close to pistol shot. I want her taken, not sunk."

"Aye aye, sir."

The Tenacious bore down. Renwick stood at the weather rail and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the ship's preparations had not already said, and because the captain's silence before an engagement was itself a communication: it told the crew that the captain was confident, which was the performance, and under the performance was a man running calculations that would not balance because the final variable was missing.

---

Thomas Dalton stood at his station behind the number twelve gun and tried to remember the sequence. Cartridge, wad, shot, wad. Or was it cartridge, shot, wad? No. Cartridge, wad, shot, wad. Trelawney had drilled it into him until it was almost automatic, and the almost was the gap where fear lived. Around him the gun crew moved with the unhurried precision of men who had done this before, and their calm was more frightening than panic would have been, because panic he could have shared, but their calm was a country he had not been admitted to.

The corvette was close now. Close enough that Dalton could see individual men on her deck — small figures moving against the pale wood, their movements purposeful and strange, like the movements of insects observed through glass. They were men who intended to kill him. He found this difficult to conceptualize. The weaving trade had its hazards — the shuttle could take an eye, the loom could crush a finger — but these were accidents, mechanical failures in a system designed to produce cloth. The corvette was a system designed to produce death, and the men operating it were skilled at their work, and their skill was pointed at him.

"Steady, lad," Trelawney said. Not unkindly. The way you would speak to a horse.

---

The first broadside was the Tenacious's. Renwick gave the order at a range of two hundred yards, and the ship erupted. Fourteen guns fired in a rippling sequence from bow to stern — not a simultaneous discharge, which was the ambition but never the reality, because each gun captain fired on the uproll and the uproll reached each gun at a slightly different moment, and the effect was a cascading thunder that started at the bow with a sharp crack and built into a roar that shook the deck underfoot and filled the air with smoke so thick and bitter that Dalton could not see the man next to him.

The corvette replied. Her broadside was lighter — the difference between ninety-six pounds and two hundred and fifty-two was audible, a sharper, thinner sound — but it was accurate. Dalton heard the shot arrive before he understood what the sound was: a ripping noise, like heavy canvas being torn, and then impacts — wood splitting, something metallic ringing against something else metallic, and a sound he had no reference for, a wet percussive thud that he would later understand was a twelve-pound iron ball striking a human body.

The gun crew reloaded. Sponge. Cartridge. Wad. Shot. Wad. Run out. Dalton performed his part of the sequence and his hands shook and Trelawney said nothing about the shaking, because the cartridge went where it needed to go, and the gun fired, and that was the standard.

---

In the cockpit, Fenton received the first casualty four minutes into the engagement: a marine private with a splinter wound through the left thigh, the splinter a foot long and as thick as a man's thumb, driven through the muscle and lodged against the femur. Fenton cut it out in thirty seconds, packed the wound, applied a tourniquet, and handed the man to Sykes. The marine was conscious and silent, his face white, his hands gripping the edges of the sea chest with a force that was itself a kind of scream.

The second casualty was a boy — one of the powder monkeys, fourteen years old, named Peake. A ball had struck the gangway above the gun deck and the planking had shattered downward, and a piece of oak the size of a man's fist had hit Peake in the chest. Fenton examined him and found a staved rib and a contusion but no penetration, and sent him to the corner of the cockpit where the walking wounded accumulated, and Peake sat with his back against the hull and breathed in shallow gasps and said nothing, because the custom of the ship was to say nothing, and customs are stronger than pain.

The third, the fourth, the fifth. A splinter wound to the forearm, a ball graze across the scalp, a crushed hand where a gun had recoiled over a carriage chock that had not been properly set. Each casualty was a data point, and the data points told a story: the corvette was aiming high, at the rigging and the men on deck, which was the French practice, and the pattern of wounds — splinters, fragments, the secondary missiles produced by shot striking wood — confirmed that the Tenacious's hull was intact but her upper works were being methodically dismantled.

Fenton worked. He did not think about the engagement above him except as it manifested in the bodies brought down the ladderway. The gun thundered overhead. The deck shook. Dust and fragments fell from the beams. Each body that arrived was a sentence in a language Fenton read fluently: the engagement is close, the engagement is sustained, the enemy's gunnery is competent, the rate of casualties is increasing.

---

Flood felt the ship stagger.

Not a hit — or not only a hit. The mainmast had been struck, he saw it from the binnacle: a ball had caught it twelve feet above the deck, gouging a channel in the wood deep enough that Flood could see the pale interior timber from forty feet away. The mast was not down. It was wounded. The splinting crew was already aloft, the bosun directing them with the focused urgency of a man who knows that a mast can stand for hours after such a wound or collapse in the next gust, and there is no way to know which.

The ship's motion had changed. Flood felt it through his feet — a slight yaw, a reluctance in the helm, as though the Tenacious was resisting the rudder. The damaged mainmast was altering the balance of the sail plan. The ship was harder to steer, which meant she was harder to position, which meant the captain's ability to control the geometry of the engagement was degrading.

Flood checked the depth. Ten fathoms. They had drifted shoreward during the engagement — the current, the wind shift, the loss of steering precision, all pushing them toward the coast. The rocks off Ushant were charted, but the charts were approximations, and ten fathoms could become five fathoms could become a reef in less distance than the corvette's broadside could cover.

He reported to the captain. "Ten fathoms, sir. Shoaling."

Renwick nodded. His face showed nothing. He was absorbing the information the way he absorbed all information — filing it, weighting it, integrating it into the picture that was never complete.

---

The engagement lasted fifty-three minutes. Afterward, Hale would reconstruct it from the log entries and the reports of the gun captains and the evidence of the damage, and the reconstruction would be orderly, sequential, a narrative that moved from cause to effect with the clarity of a well-drawn chart. But the engagement itself was not orderly. It was noise and smoke and the ship lurching under him and men screaming and the guns firing and the intervals between the guns' firing growing longer as the crews tired and shorter as the range closed, and through all of it the corvette was there, a dark shape in the smoke, her guns flashing orange, and the shot coming in — he could hear it, the different sounds of shot hitting different things: the deep thud of a ball burying itself in the hull, the crack of wood splitting, the whine of a ball ricocheting off iron, and the other sound, the one he would not describe in the log.

The corvette's foremast went at twenty-seven minutes. The Tenacious's gunnery had been concentrating on her bow — Renwick's order, methodical as always, working the target from one end to the other — and the foremast came down in a tangle of rigging and canvas that dragged in the sea and pulled the corvette's head around, and for a moment she was broadside-on and helpless, and Renwick ordered the helm over and brought the Tenacious across her stern and raked her, and the raking broadside went the length of the corvette's gun deck and did the thing that raking broadsides do, which Dalton, at his station, could not see but could hear: wood, metal, and something else, a sound that was not any material he knew, and then voices, many voices, in a language he did not speak but whose meaning was the same in any language.

The corvette struck her colours at fifty-three minutes.

## VIII. The Receipts

Fenton's tally: seven dead, twenty-three wounded, of whom four would not survive the night. The dead included a midshipman named Ridley, age fifteen, who had been standing on the quarterdeck when a ball took his head, and the second captain of number four gun, a man named Lobb, who had been hit by a splinter through the abdomen and who had taken forty minutes to die in the cockpit while Fenton worked on men he could save. The dying included the marine private with the thigh wound, whose tourniquet had slipped during the engagement and who had lost too much blood, and a topman named Weeks, who had fallen from the mainmast when the rigging parted and who lay on the orlop deck with his back broken and his eyes open and his hands folded across his chest as though he were waiting for something with great patience.

Fenton washed his instruments. The water in the bucket was the colour of rust. He counted the instruments again — by touch, eyes closed — and confirmed that all were present, and laid them in their case, and sat on the powder keg, and breathed.

Above him, through the deck, he could hear the sounds of a ship securing from action. The pumps working. Hammering — the carpenter's crew, patching the hull strikes. Voices, indistinct, giving orders that were now administrative rather than tactical. The engagement was over and the receipts were in and the sum was thirty casualties out of one hundred and eighty-seven, which was sixteen percent, which was within the range Fenton considered normal for a close engagement of this duration, which was a calculation he performed and then set aside because the dead were not a percentage, they were specific men whose names he knew.

## IX. The Captain

Renwick sat in the space where his great cabin had been. The bulkheads were still down. The stern chasers were still run out, their muzzles jutting through the open ports into the evening air. The stern windows were shattered and the wind came through them and moved the smoke that still lingered in the ship.

The corvette was taken. A prize crew under Hale was aboard her, securing the prisoners and assessing the damage. Her captain was dead — killed in the raking broadside, according to the French lieutenant who had surrendered the ship, and who had conducted the surrender with a formality that Renwick found both admirable and unbearable, because formality was another species of the performance that military life required of men who had just been trying to kill each other.

The butcher's bill was on the table in front of him. Not the physical table — that was stowed below — but a crate that served, and on it the list, written in the purser's careful hand: seven dead, twenty-three wounded. Renwick read the names. He read them twice. He knew each man. Not well — a captain of a frigate knew his crew the way a farmer knows his acres, by the work they did and the problems they presented, not by the interior of their lives — but he knew them, and the knowing was the cost.

The dispatch he would write to the Admiralty was already forming in his mind. A clear victory. An eighteen-gun corvette taken by a twenty-eight-gun frigate. The Gazette would print it. His name would appear. The word "victory" would be used.

Renwick took from his coat pocket the folded dispatches he had received at Plymouth. He spread them on the crate and read them again — the fourth time, or the fifth; he had lost count. The intelligence was detailed. The Dorade's movements, her armament, her captain's record. And the gap. The gap that was not a gap, that was an absence so shaped by the surrounding text that it acquired the outline of a presence. What else sailed from Brest in October. The dispatches did not say. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps a store ship, or a transport, or a vessel bound for Martinique on a separate mission entirely. Perhaps a frigate. Perhaps a thirty-six-gun frigate that had been in company with the Dorade and had separated, and was now somewhere in these waters, beyond the horizon, and would appear tomorrow or next week or never.

He did not know. He would never know. He had engaged the corvette on the strength of the intelligence he possessed, which was ninety percent of the intelligence he needed, and the engagement was won, and seven men were dead, and twenty-three were wounded, and the question of whether the missing ten percent would have changed his decision — whether knowing what he did not know would have made him stand off, or call for support, or choose a different angle of approach that would have kept the mainmast out of the corvette's arc of fire, or adopt a tactic that would have ended the action in thirty minutes instead of fifty-three — this question would remain unanswered because the information required to answer it did not exist in his possession and might not exist anywhere, and the absence of the information was not a failure of intelligence or a failure of command but the permanent condition of command, which was to act in darkness and call it decision and live with the arithmetic.

The wind came through the shattered stern windows. The ship moved under him — the particular motion of a ship with a wounded mainmast, a slight irregularity in the roll, a hesitation, as though the ship itself were compensating for an injury it could not describe. Renwick folded the dispatches and put them back in his pocket. He took the butcher's bill and read the names a third time. Seven dead. He said their names aloud, in the empty space where his cabin had been, because no one was present and the saying was for himself and for them and for no purpose he could articulate.

Then he stood, and went on deck, and assumed the expression that command required, and gave orders for the night, and the ship sailed on.

## X. The Pressed Man

Dalton washed the gun. The sponge went into the bore and came out black and he dipped it in the bucket and wrung it and did it again. His hands had stopped shaking. He did not know when they had stopped — sometime during the engagement, when the sequence of loading had replaced the fear with a mechanical rhythm that was not courage but was sufficient, and the sufficiency was all that was asked.

He did not know what the engagement had meant. He knew that men were dead and that the French ship was taken and that this was called a victory and that the word seemed to him to describe something other than what he had experienced. He had experienced noise, and fear, and the cartridge going into the muzzle, and Trelawney's voice saying "fire" and the gun leaping back and the smoke and then the sequence again, and at no point in this experience had the word "victory" appeared, because the word required a vantage point he did not possess — the captain's vantage, or the admiral's, or the historian's — a view from above, where the geometry of the engagement was visible and the outcome could be assessed against the cost and the result entered in a ledger.

From where Dalton stood, at the number twelve gun on the starboard side, there was no geometry. There was a tube of iron and a sequence of actions and a French ship in the smoke and men he did not know dying in ways he could not see, and the only thing he knew with certainty was that the cartridge went into the muzzle and the gun fired and he was alive and the man next to him was alive and the man next to him was alive, and this was not victory but it was something, and he did not have a word for it.

He wrung the sponge and set it aside and looked out through the gun port. The sea was dark now. The corvette was visible as a cluster of lights, dimmer than the Tenacious's lights, because her masts were down and her lanterns were fewer. Between the two ships the water moved in the way it always moved, indifferent to what had happened on its surface, carrying the smoke away toward the coast of France, which was out there in the darkness, a presence felt but not seen, like so many things.
