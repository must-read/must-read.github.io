Grandma Neri's house sold in eleven days. The realtor said this was fast for Hyattsville, that the market was soft, that they were lucky. Jolene signed the papers in the realtor's office wearing nitrile gloves and the realtor did not ask why. People assumed things. Germophobe, eczema, religious. Nobody guessed: *because the last time I shook a stranger's hand I spent forty minutes in my car crying someone else's tears about someone else's dead dog.*

The house had been cleaned out weeks before the sale, Jolene and her mother working room by room with boxes and garbage bags, sorting Grandma Neri's seventy-one years of living into three categories: keep, donate, dumpster. Her mother took the photographs and the good china and the quilts. Jolene took nothing. She didn't need objects. She had the floor plan.

She hadn't noticed it growing up. Nobody had. But standing in the empty house on the last day before closing, she saw what Neri had built: a home designed for a woman who could not afford to be touched. The living room chairs positioned four feet apart with a table between them wide enough that two people sitting across from each other could never accidentally brush hands reaching for a coffee cup. The kitchen where the island created a permanent barrier between the cook and anyone who entered — you could serve a meal to twelve people without a single person standing within arm's reach. The hallway narrowed by bookshelves on both sides so that two people passing would turn sideways, creating distance with architecture. The bedroom where the bed was shoved against the wall, accessible only from one side, because Grandpa Oscar had died in 1987 and after that nobody needed the other side and the wall was safer than empty air.

The family called her distant. *Neri keeps to herself.* As if it were a personality trait. As if a woman who spent sixty years engineering her physical environment to avoid skin contact was merely private.

Jolene understood now. She understood because four months after the funeral, during a routine shoulder mobilization on a fifty-three-year-old accountant named Debra, she had pressed her thumb into the trapezius and felt — not the muscle, not the knot, not the fascia resisting and then yielding — but Debra's marriage. The whole leaking structure of it. Twenty-six years of resentment stored in the shoulder like a geological deposit, layer after layer compressed into something so dense it had its own gravity. Jolene's thumb found it and Debra's private misery flooded up her hand, through her wrist, into her chest, and settled behind her sternum like a second heartbeat.

She'd excused herself to the bathroom. Sat on the tile floor. Breathed until the borrowed feeling metabolized — twenty minutes, maybe twenty-five, Debra's resentment dissolving slowly like a tablet in water, leaving behind a residue of bitterness that didn't fade until the next morning.

That was the first time. After that, every patient was an ambush.

---

She didn't quit. That part mattered. She didn't quit because the practice had taken nine years to build and because she was the only Black woman running a physical therapy clinic in Silver Spring, Maryland, and because her patients needed her and because — this was the part she wouldn't have admitted if you'd asked, but it was the truest reason — she was too stubborn to let a dead woman's inheritance dismantle her life.

She bought gloves. Thin, medical-grade, the kind that surgeons wear. Her patients accepted them. She told them it was a new hygiene protocol and they nodded the way people nod when authority makes a minor request. The gloves dampened the signal. Didn't block it — nothing blocked it — but reduced the bandwidth from overwhelming to merely unpleasant, like listening to someone's argument through a motel wall: you got the tone, the shape of the anger, without the specific words.

She learned Neri's tricks. Long sleeves. Strategic furniture placement. When a patient reached to shake her hand goodbye, she'd already be holding a clipboard, a pen, the door handle — her hands perpetually occupied. She cultivated a reputation for professionalism that was actually a reputation for untouchability, and she did not examine the difference.

At home she rearranged nothing. Her apartment in Takoma Park was already built for one person, and she kept it that way: the couch pushed against the wall, the armchair angled toward the television, the kitchen table flush against the window with one chair. Not Neri's calculated distances. Just the ordinary geography of a woman who lived alone and saw no reason to leave space for anyone else.

She was thirty-one. She'd had relationships — two significant ones, both with women, both ended by Jolene before they could survive long enough to require explanation. The first, with a biologist named Kezia, had ended eight months before the ability manifested, and Jolene sometimes wondered whether Kezia's particular sadness during their breakup — a controlled, private grief that expressed itself as excessive politeness — would have been legible through her skin. The second, with a sous chef named Diana, had ended three weeks after the funeral. Diana had reached across the restaurant table to hold Jolene's hand and Jolene had felt Diana's exact feelings for her — affection, desire, a specific hope about the weekend — and the precision of it, the involuntary intimacy of knowing exactly what another person felt, had been so obscene that Jolene pulled her hand away and couldn't explain why and Diana had left the restaurant and Jolene had let her.

After Diana, she wore the gloves everywhere.

---

Sable Acheson blew glass in a converted garage in Brentwood, three miles from Jolene's clinic. The garage had roll-up doors on two sides that she kept open in warm weather, and the furnace ran at 2,100 degrees, and the neighborhood kids sometimes gathered at a distance to watch her work: the gather, the blow, the shaping, the annealing. She did commissions — light fixtures, mostly, for restaurants and hotels — and she taught a Wednesday evening class for beginners that filled every session.

Jolene met her because of the shoulder. Not a patient's shoulder — Sable's. She'd strained her deltoid pulling a four-foot piece from the annealing oven and her doctor had referred her for physical therapy and the referral had landed on Jolene's schedule for a Tuesday afternoon in October.

Sable arrived wearing a tank top that showed the burn scars on her forearms — pale raised ridges and circles where molten glass had kissed the skin and left its signature. She shook Jolene's gloved hand with the unselfconscious grip of someone who routinely handled objects at a thousand degrees and had calibrated her pain tolerance accordingly. She sat on the treatment table and said, "It's the left one, the posterior delt, and I already know I should stop lifting fifty-pound molds by myself but I'm not going to stop, so whatever you can do that lets me keep being stupid, I'd appreciate."

Jolene liked her. That was the first problem. She liked her before she touched her, which meant the liking had nothing to do with the ability and everything to do with the way Sable talked — direct, self-aware, funny in the way that people are funny when they've stopped trying to be. She had locs piled on her head and held with a leather cord and her hands were extraordinary: strong, scarred, calloused across the palms in patterns that told the story of her trade. Glassblower's hands. Hands that had learned to hold fragile things at their most dangerous temperature.

The first touch was through gloves. Jolene palpated the deltoid, found the strain, began working the tissue. Through the nitrile she felt the usual dampened signal — but it was wrong. Not wrong like Debra's marriage was wrong, or like the man with the torn rotator cuff whose rage came through his shoulder like a hot wire. Wrong like a radio tuned between stations. She could feel something, a structure, an emotional presence, but it didn't resolve into anything she recognized.

She pressed harder. The signal remained unreadable. Not absent — she could feel that Sable was there, feeling things, carrying a full inner life — but the emotional landscape was organized in a grammar Jolene had never encountered. Like hearing a language with consistent syntax and no familiar vocabulary. Coherent, organized, and completely alien.

"You okay?" Sable said. "You stopped."

"Just assessing the tissue."

"You've been assessing for about a minute without moving your hands."

Jolene resumed the mobilization. She finished the session in twenty-eight minutes, scheduled the follow-up for Thursday, and walked Sable to the door with both hands clasped behind her back.

In the bathroom afterward, sitting on the tile floor — her floor, the one she knew, with the grout stain near the baseboard from a bleach spill in 2024 — she waited for the borrowed emotion to metabolize. Nothing came. Not because the signal had been too faint. Because whatever she'd absorbed from Sable's shoulder didn't follow the usual pattern. It sat in her hand, a warmth she couldn't name, and it didn't dissolve.

---

Thursday's session was longer. Sable talked about glass while Jolene worked the deltoid: how different compositions responded to heat, how borosilicate held its shape longer but didn't catch light the same way, how the gather was the most important moment because once the glass was on the pipe everything that happened next was negotiation. "You're not in control," she said. "You're in conversation. The glass has opinions."

Jolene listened and pressed her thumbs into the posterior deltoid and the warmth from Tuesday was still in her right hand and the new signal layered on top of it, a second frequency harmonizing with the first. She couldn't read either one. She could feel them both. The sensation was like standing in a room where two people were speaking a language she didn't know, and understanding from their intonation that what they were saying mattered enormously.

She took the glove off her left hand. She told herself she needed to feel the tissue directly, which was true — the strain had a deeper component, possibly a partial tear, and the glove blunted her clinical touch. She told herself it was medical. She pressed bare fingers into Sable's shoulder.

The signal came through clean and full and incomprehensible. Not louder — the glove hadn't been dampening it, she realized. There was no dampened version. This was the signal. Vast, organized, moving in patterns she could track but not translate, like watching weather systems from orbit. She felt warmth but couldn't call it affection. She felt something swift and rhythmic that might have been joy or might have been fear or might have been the emotional equivalent of a color she had no name for. She felt depth — layers beneath layers, each one structured, none of them legible.

And running through all of it, a current she'd never felt from anyone: interest directed at her. Not desire, not yet, or if it was desire it was a kind she didn't recognize. An orientation. Sable's emotional attention, pointed at Jolene with a coherence that was almost physical, like a beam of light you could feel on your skin.

Jolene held the touch for seven seconds longer than clinical necessity required. Then she pulled away and put the glove back on and said, "Slight partial tear. I'm going to adjust your program."

"Whatever you say." Sable's voice was easy. She didn't seem to know what had happened. She didn't seem to know that Jolene was carrying a new frequency in her hand that hummed like a tuning fork and would not stop.

---

That night, Jolene moved the armchair. Not far — eighteen inches from the wall, angled slightly toward the couch instead of the television. She did this at eleven p.m. while brushing her teeth, holding the toothbrush in her left hand and pushing the chair with her right, the hand that still hummed. She didn't think about it. She went to bed and in the morning couldn't remember moving it.

But there it was: the chair, eighteen inches from where it had lived for three years, creating a space that looked like a conversation.

She noticed and moved it back.

---

The sessions continued: Tuesday, Thursday, Tuesday. The partial tear responded well. Sable's range of motion improved. Jolene wore the gloves and felt the signal through them and stopped pretending the gloves made a difference with this particular patient.

She learned things. Not through the touch — through ordinary conversation, the kind that happens between a therapist and a patient who are beginning to like each other. Sable was thirty-four. She'd studied at the Pilchuck Glass School in Washington State, then worked for a production studio in Baltimore for six years before going independent. She lived alone. She had a cat named Murano, which she acknowledged was "the most predictable name a glassblower has ever given a cat" with the tone of someone who'd made peace with her own predictability. She brought Jolene a small glass paperweight after the third session — pale green, with a spiral of air bubbles trapped inside — and said, "For your desk. It's a second, there's a crack on the bottom, but the bubbles are good." Jolene took it with gloved hands and felt nothing from the glass but everything from the gesture.

She was falling. She knew the mechanics of falling because she'd done it twice before, and the mechanics hadn't changed: the attention, the anticipation, the way she arranged her day so that Tuesday and Thursday had weight and the days between them were waiting rooms. What had changed was the ability. Falling for Kezia and Diana had been ordinary gravity. Falling for Sable was falling with an open channel, her body receiving a constant stream of data she couldn't decode. It was like learning to love someone in a language she didn't speak — the emotional equivalent of recognizing tenderness from tone of voice alone, without understanding a single word.

She moved the armchair again. And the kitchen table — pulled it six inches from the window, leaving room for a second chair. She didn't buy a second chair. But the space was there, a gap shaped like a possibility.

She started sleeping on the far side of the bed. The wall side had been hers for years, the Neri side, the safe side. She didn't notice the shift for three nights. On the fourth she woke at 2 a.m. with her hand extended into the empty space beside her, palm up, as if waiting.

---

The migraines started after the sixth session.

Not ordinary headaches — these had a quality. A scent. Jolene would be driving home from the clinic and a migraine would arrive like a door opening and it would smell like hot glass and flux and the specific ozone that Sable had described as "what air smells like when it's been too close to a furnace." The pain was real, clinical, the kind that responded to nothing and lasted exactly ninety minutes. But the smell was borrowed. The smell was Sable's workspace infiltrating Jolene's skull.

Then: phantom muscle memory. Standing at the kitchen counter making coffee, her hands would perform a rotation she'd never learned — a quick, sure twist of the wrist, exactly the motion Sable used when shaping a gather on the marver. Jolene's hands, doing Sable's work, without her permission or understanding.

Then: the emotion at 3 a.m. She'd wake with something sitting on her chest — not anxiety, not grief, not any feeling she could name. Something structured and unfamiliar, coherent but unreadable, warm but not comforting in the way warmth usually comforted. Something that belonged to Sable's emotional grammar, metabolized imperfectly into Jolene's body, sitting in her ribcage like a guest who'd arrived early and didn't know where to put their coat.

She was accumulating. Every session, every touch — even through gloves — left a deposit. Sable's emotional signature was not metabolizing the way other people's did. It stayed. It layered. It built, the way glass builds in the furnace, gather upon gather, each layer trapping the one before it.

She called her mother. "Did Grandma Neri ever mention headaches?"

"Your grandmother had headaches every day of her life. She kept aspirin in her pocket like mints. Why?"

"Did they smell like anything?"

A long pause. "Jo, headaches don't smell."

"No," Jolene said. "You're right. Never mind."

---

The ninth session was the last clinical one. The partial tear had healed. Sable's range of motion was complete. There was no medical reason for another appointment. Jolene stood in her clinic at 4:45 p.m. on a Thursday writing the discharge note and knowing that she was writing herself out of the only legitimate reason she had to touch this woman.

"So that's it?" Sable said, pulling on her jacket.

"The tear is healed. You should be able to return to full activity."

"That's not what I asked."

Jolene looked at her. Sable was standing by the door with her scarred hands in her jacket pockets and an expression that, on anyone else, Jolene would have been able to read through a handshake — but which on Sable's face was as legible and as untranslatable as everything else about her.

"What are you asking?"

"I'm asking if this is where you say goodbye or if this is where you admit that you've been holding my shoulder about forty percent longer than therapeutic necessity requires since the third session."

Jolene felt the blood move in her face. A blush, actual and embarrassing, and underneath it the humming in her right hand intensified.

"I noticed," Sable said. "I'm a glassblower. I know what it looks like when someone's holding on to something hot because they can't bring themselves to let go. You want to get dinner?"

---

Dinner was a Vietnamese restaurant in Hyattsville, ten minutes from Grandma Neri's old house. Jolene didn't choose it for that reason but she noted it, the way you note a coincidence that feels like an instruction.

She didn't wear gloves. She couldn't. There was no clinical excuse, no hygiene protocol, no professional barrier to hide behind. Her bare hands held the menu and the chopsticks and the water glass and she was careful not to touch Sable and Sable was careful not to notice.

They talked for two hours. About glass, about bodies, about the strange parallel of their professions — Sable shaping something molten and fragile, Jolene repairing something hurt and resistant. About Sable's parents in Richmond, about Jolene's mother in Laurel. About nothing that mattered and everything that contextualized the mattering that was happening underneath the conversation like a river under ice.

At the end of dinner Sable reached across the table and took Jolene's hand.

There was no glove. There was no warning. Sable's calloused palm closed around Jolene's fingers and the full-bandwidth signal hit.

Jolene had spent months receiving fragments — shoulder touches, brief contacts, dampened-through-nitrile impressions of Sable's inner life. Those fragments had been bewildering enough. The full signal was something else. It was not louder. It was not more intense in the way that turning up a volume knob produces more of the same sound. It was a different category of experience, the way a symphony is not a louder version of a single note.

She felt Sable's emotional landscape in its entirety: vast, structured, moving in patterns she could finally — not understand, but apprehend. Like watching a murmuration of starlings and grasping, not the rules of the movement, but the fact of the beauty. There was warmth that was not affection and speed that was not anxiety and depth that was not sadness and, running through all of it, directed at Jolene with a focus that felt almost gravitational, a feeling she had no word for in her own emotional vocabulary. A word from Sable's language. A word that meant something like *I see you and I am not turning away.*

It lasted four seconds. Jolene pulled her hand back. She knocked over the water glass. She said, "Sorry, sorry," and mopped the table with her napkin and did not look at Sable's face.

"Jo."

"I'm fine."

"You're shaking."

She was shaking. Her right hand, the one Sable had held, was trembling in a way she couldn't control. Not from fear. From the residue. Sable's signal was in her hand, in her wrist, traveling up her arm like something poured, and it was not metabolizing. It was settling. Finding spaces in her body she hadn't known were empty and filling them with a warmth that smelled like hot glass and ozone.

"I need to tell you something," Jolene said. And she didn't know what she was going to say until she said it, which was not about the ability, not yet, but was maybe the more frightening confession: "I think about you every day. Not in the normal way. In a way that worries me."

Sable didn't lean back. Didn't create distance. She put both hands flat on the table, palms up, and said, "Tell me."

---

She told her. Not everything — not the mechanics, not the inheritance, not Grandma Neri's architecture of evasion. She told her the relevant part: "When I touch people, I feel what they're feeling. Physically. In my body. And when I touch you, I feel something I've never felt before and I don't understand it and it stays."

Sable was quiet for a long time. The restaurant was emptying around them. A waiter cleared the table next to theirs and didn't look at them, which is the gift a good waiter gives to a couple in a difficult moment.

"What does it feel like?" Sable said. "When you touch me. What does it actually feel like?"

"Like a language I don't speak. Every other person I've touched — I can read them. Their anger, their sadness, their desire. I know the vocabulary. With you, I feel structure. Coherence. Something organized and real and completely beyond my ability to translate. And every time I touch you, a piece of it stays in my body. In my hands. In my chest. I get migraines that smell like your studio. I wake up at night with feelings I can't name sitting on my ribcage. My hands do things they've never done — glassblower's motions, turns and shapes that belong to your body, not mine."

Sable looked at her hands on the table. Scarred and calloused. Thirty-four years of handling heat and fragile things. "And this scares you."

"My grandmother had this ability. She spent her entire life avoiding touch. She built her house so nobody could get close to her. She died careful and alone and the family thought she was cold and she wasn't cold. She was protecting herself. And them."

"And you think that's your future."

"I think her future is in my body the way your feelings are in my body. I think I inherited both things — the ability and the fear. And the fear says: every touch costs something the body can't refuse to pay. There's no way to love someone with this and not be consumed by it."

Sable turned her hands over. Palms down, then palms up again. Examining them as if they were an unfamiliar material. "You said when you touch other people, you can read them."

"Yes."

"But you can't read me."

"No."

"And it never occurred to you that the thing you can't read is the thing that won't consume you? That maybe the reason your grandmother was consumed was because she could read everyone — she was drowning in translated feelings, in a constant flood of other people's grief and joy and rage, all perfectly legible, all demanding a response. But I'm illegible to you. Whatever I am doesn't translate into demands. It just — stays."

Jolene hadn't considered this. She sat with it the way she sat with borrowed emotions on the bathroom floor: waiting for it to dissolve or solidify, unsure which would be worse.

"Can I try something?" Sable said.

"What?"

Sable reached across the table again. Slowly. Her hand open, the scars visible in the restaurant light, the calluses thick on her palm. She stopped with her fingers six inches from Jolene's.

"Touch me," she said. "And this time, don't pull away."

---

Jolene took her hand.

The signal hit and she held on. Sable's emotional landscape flooded through the contact — the vast, structured, untranslatable territory of another person's inner life. And this time, because she was holding on, because she wasn't pulling away, the signal kept coming. Deeper. Not louder. Deeper, the way an ocean is deep when you stop swimming on the surface and let yourself sink.

She felt the warmth she couldn't name. She felt the swift rhythmic thing. She felt the depth. And then — something new. A reflection. Her own feeling, her own desire, running through the contact into Sable and coming back changed. Altered by its passage through Sable's emotional architecture, returned in a form she didn't recognize as hers at first. Like looking at her face in a funhouse mirror: definitely her, definitely not her.

She was feeling herself through Sable. Her own emotions, translated into Sable's language, reflected back.

She felt her own desire and it was unrecognizable: not the familiar ache she knew, but something with a different rhythm, a different weight. She felt her own fear and it came back as something quieter, less catastrophic, more patient. She felt something she understood to be love only because of its location — the center of the chest, the old address — but which bore no resemblance to any love she'd felt before. It was her love, run through Sable's grammar, and what came back was a feeling that meant something like: *this will change you and the change will be irreversible and the irreversibility is not the same thing as destruction.*

The current ran both ways. For the first time in her life, the current ran both ways.

Sable's eyes were wet. Not crying — glassy. As if she could feel it too, not the ability, not the empathic channel, but the ordinary miracle of being held by someone who was paying that much attention. She squeezed Jolene's hand. Her calloused fingers pressed against Jolene's palm and the signal surged and Jolene didn't pull away.

Twelve seconds. Fifteen. Twenty. The longest she'd ever held someone's hand since the ability manifested. The residue was accumulating in real time — she could feel it, Sable's untranslatable warmth spreading through her hand, her wrist, her forearm, settling into the tissue the way a stain settles into wood. Permanent. Not metabolizing. Becoming part of her.

She held on for thirty-one seconds before she let go.

---

She went home and moved the kitchen table to the center of the room. Pulled it away from the window, away from the wall, and set it where two people could sit across from each other with their knees almost touching. She found a folding chair in the closet and opened it and placed it at the table across from her usual chair. The folding chair was ugly, metal and vinyl, the kind you buy for parties and then store. She didn't care. The space existed now: two chairs, facing each other, close enough to reach across and touch.

She moved the couch away from the wall. She angled the armchair toward it instead of the television. She stood in the middle of her rearranged apartment at midnight and understood, with a clarity that had nothing to do with the ability and everything to do with the body's knowledge, that she was dismantling Neri's blueprint. Not defiantly. Not dramatically. The way a body heals — slowly, from the inside, without asking permission.

The migraine arrived at 1 a.m. It smelled like hot glass and tasted like ozone and lasted exactly ninety minutes and when it left, the warmth in her sternum remained.

---

The second date was at Sable's studio. The furnace was off — she'd shut it down for a rare day without commissions — but the garage held the heat in its bricks and the air was warm and tasted of silica. Murano the cat observed from the top of the annealing oven, which was also off.

Sable showed her the process. The rods, the pipes, the colors. The glory hole where glass is reheated, the marver where it's shaped, the jacks and tweezers and paddles. She handed Jolene a finished piece, a small bowl the color of amber, and their fingers touched during the transfer and Jolene felt the signal but also something new layered on top of it: a note of deliberateness. Sable had touched her on purpose. Not accidentally, not as a function of handing something over. On purpose. And the purposefulness had its own emotional frequency, an undertone of *I know what this costs you and I'm choosing to pay it too.*

"I've been thinking about what you said," Sable told her. She was standing by the marver, leaning against it, her arms crossed over her scarred forearms. "About your grandmother's house. The furniture. The distances."

"What about it?"

"I think about distance differently because of the glass. When you're blowing glass, distance is everything. Too close to the furnace and the glass gets too hot and slumps. Too far and it cools and cracks. There's a perfect working distance for every piece — close enough to shape it, far enough to keep it alive. It's not about avoiding contact. It's about finding the distance where contact is possible."

Jolene set the amber bowl on the workbench. "My grandmother never found it."

"Your grandmother's instrument was calibrated for a frequency that was everywhere. Everyone she touched was legible. There was no safe working distance because the signal was universal. But I'm — what did you say? Illegible. Which means the signal isn't the same. The working distance isn't the same. Maybe with me, the distance is closer than she ever got to try."

"Maybe the distance is zero," Jolene said. She hadn't meant to say it. It came out of the same place that moved furniture at midnight: the body knowing before the brain.

Sable uncrossed her arms.

The first kiss was a catastrophe in the way that all true things are catastrophes: it destroyed what came before it.

Sable's mouth on hers. The maximum contact. Lip to lip, breath to breath, and the signal screaming through every point of connection. Jolene received Sable's entire emotional landscape at full bandwidth and for the first time it wasn't noise. It was music. Not a melody she could hum but a composition so complex and so layered that she understood why she hadn't been able to read it through a shoulder or a hand: it required the full instrument. The mouth, the breath, the skin of the face where every nerve ending crowds close to the surface. This was what Sable felt like, all of her, all at once: an emotional architecture so vast and so specific that Jolene's entire learned vocabulary — the twenty-eight years of reading human feelings through touch — was revealed for what it was. A primer. An alphabet book. Nursery rhymes.

And underneath the signal: the reflection. Jolene's own desire, her own terror, her own love — all of it transmitted through the kiss into Sable and returned, altered, translated into that alien grammar. She felt herself feeling. She felt her own hunger come back as something that tasted like glass and permanence. She felt her own fear come back quieter, lower, more like a hum than a scream.

They kissed for a long time. The cat jumped off the annealing oven and landed on something that clattered and neither of them moved. Jolene's hands were on Sable's face, her bare palms against the skin of Sable's jaw, receiving the full signal with every finger, and the residue was accumulating faster than it ever had — she could feel it rushing through her arms and down into her chest and settling in her bones with the permanence of something poured and cooled and set.

When they pulled apart, Jolene's hands were trembling again. And Sable's eyes were glassy again. And neither of them said anything for a while.

"I'm being rewritten," Jolene said finally. "You understand that? My body is learning your language and it's not asking me if I want to be bilingual."

"Is it bad?"

"I don't know. I don't know what I'll be when it's finished."

"Maybe it's not finishing. Maybe it's just — ongoing. Like the glass. You don't finish a piece. You get it to a point where it's stable, and then it goes in the annealer, and the heat comes down slowly, over hours, and when it comes out it's changed but it's whole. It's not the same thing it was in the furnace. But it's not damaged. It's annealed."

Jolene heard Neri in her own head: *the body always pays. There's no negotiating.* And she heard the counter-argument, not from Sable but from her own reflected feelings coming back through Sable's architecture: *paying isn't the same as losing.*

She didn't ask Sable to come to her apartment. Sable came anyway, three days later, on a Sunday evening, because Jolene had texted her the address and the text was an invitation even if the words were just *I made too much soup, come over if you want.*

Sable stood in the doorway and looked at the apartment: the rearranged living room, the table pulled to the center of the kitchen, the folding chair facing the good chair. Two seats. Two places. A room reconfigured for proximity.

"You moved your furniture," Sable said.

"I've been moving it for weeks. I didn't notice until recently."

"It looks like a room that's expecting someone."

"It is."

They ate soup at the kitchen table with their knees touching under the small surface. Every point of contact was a transmission, Sable's signal running through denim and skin and bone. The residue accumulated and Jolene let it. Sable's warmth in her kneecaps, in her thighs, moving upward with the patience of something that knew it had time.

After dinner they sat on the couch. Not the armchair, which was for one person. The couch, which Jolene had pulled from the wall for this, for exactly this: two people sitting close enough that their shoulders touched. Sable's shoulder against hers. The signal pouring through. The untranslatable language of another person's inner life speaking continuously in a grammar Jolene was beginning — not to understand. To recognize. The way you recognize a song before you know the words.

"I get these migraines," Jolene said. "They smell like your studio."

"I know."

"You know about the migraines?"

"I know that something's happening to you. When we touch, something changes in your face. Not pain, not pleasure. Something between. Like you're hearing a sound I can't hear. I don't know what it is. I just know it's real and it's costing you something."

"It's costing me everything I used to be."

"Is that a complaint?"

Jolene considered. The migraine warmth was in her sternum. The phantom muscle memory was in her hands — she'd caught herself making the marver rotation twice that week, shaping invisible glass with Sable's muscle memory, her body practicing a craft it had never learned. The 3 a.m. emotion had shifted: still unnameable, still foreign, but no longer sitting on her chest like a weight. Now it curled against her, occupying the space in her ribcage the way Murano occupied the top of the annealing oven. Present. Warm. Choosing to be there.

"No," she said. "It's an inventory."

Sable put her head on Jolene's shoulder. The signal surged. Jolene felt the vast incomprehensible architecture of this woman's feelings, full and organized and alien and beautiful, and she felt her own feelings reflected back through that architecture — distorted, translated, irreversibly changed. She was becoming something new. Not Sable, not Neri, not the Jolene who had worn gloves to shake a realtor's hand four months ago. Something that contained all of those people and also the residue: the hot-glass migraines, the phantom rotations, the unnamed 3 a.m. feeling that was learning to live in her body as if it belonged there.

The armchair faced the couch. The kitchen table held two chairs. The bed — she'd check later, but she already knew — the bed would be pulled from the wall, both sides open, the Neri side abandoned. Her body had been building this room for weeks, closing the grandmother's distances one piece of furniture at a time, constructing a closer architecture. A blueprint not of avoidance but of admission.

Sable's breathing slowed against her shoulder. The signal quieted — not gone, never gone, but settling into something like a resting frequency. The residue in Jolene's body hummed at the same pitch. She was carrying pieces of this woman in her bones, in her hands, in the migraines that smelled of glass and the dreams she was beginning to have in a language she was beginning to almost recognize.

Was she still herself? The question orbited like a satellite. She didn't catch it. She didn't need to. The center held: two women on a couch, shoulder to shoulder, in a room that had been quietly rebuilt around the possibility of closeness. The edges were dissolving — Jolene's edges, the careful perimeters Neri had bequeathed her, the distances she'd maintained with gloves and clipboards and strategically occupied hands. Dissolving, not painlessly. The body was keeping the tab. The body always keeps the tab.

But the body was also doing something Neri's body had never done: leaning in. Closing the distance. Choosing the cost.

Outside, Takoma Park did what it does on a Sunday night: porch lights, someone practicing trumpet badly three blocks over, the 14 bus hissing to a stop on Carroll Avenue. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a world where people touch each other without consequence, without revelation, without being fundamentally altered by the contact. Jolene lived adjacent to that world now, in a room she'd built with her own two hands — the hands that hummed, the hands that dreamed in someone else's language, the hands that had pushed a chair eighteen inches from the wall because they knew, before she did, that she was done with distance.

Sable shifted against her. The signal pulsed. The residue deepened. Jolene stayed.
