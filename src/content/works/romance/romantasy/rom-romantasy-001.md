She felt him before she heard him.

Not the footsteps — those came second, soft on the limestone corridor outside the infirmary, the gait of a man who had learned to move without announcing himself. What came first was the sensation low in her sternum: a tightening, like a fist closing around something warm. Not pain. Presence. Tallis Dane was somewhere in the east wing, and her body knew it before her mind had any say in the matter.

Seren set down the pestle. The yarrow she'd been grinding had gone to powder five minutes ago; she'd been working the stone out of habit, the way her hands always moved when the bond was pulling. Three months since the binding ceremony and she still couldn't tell the difference between her own restlessness and his. The court physicians said the distinction would sharpen with time. The court physicians said many things she had stopped believing.

The door opened without a knock. Of course without a knock.

"You're bleeding," she said.

Tallis paused in the doorway. He was good at pausing — at occupying a threshold as though he'd chosen it rather than been caught in it. Dark coat buttoned to the throat, hands at his sides, jaw set in the particular way that meant he was dampening. She could feel it: the deliberate flattening, like a hand pressed over a bell. Underneath it, the hot throb of something cut.

"It's not mine," he said.

"It is now."

He looked at her. The look lasted one beat too long, which was the only kind of look he ever gave her. Then he crossed the infirmary and sat on the examination table without being told. He unbuttoned his coat. Beneath it, his shirt was dark with someone else's blood on the left side and his own on the right, where a blade had caught him across the ribs. A shallow wound. She'd felt worse from him — the week before, a dislocated shoulder that had woken her from sleep with a burst of nauseating wrongness, the joint screaming its displacement through the bond like a child screaming for its mother.

"Shirt off."

He pulled it over his head. The motion sent a flare of pain through the bond — not the sharp note of the wound itself but the duller ache of torn tissue being stretched. Her hands flinched toward him before she could stop them. Healing instinct. The bond turned her into a compass that always pointed at his damage.

She examined the cut. Three inches long, half an inch deep at the center, tapering at both ends. Clean edges — a good blade. His skin was warm under her fingers, and she hated that she noticed the warmth, hated that the bond gave warmth a texture she could taste: copper and woodsmoke and the faint residue of exertion, as though his body had been working hard and the effort had a flavor.

"Who was it?"

"Nobody you need to know about."

"I felt you kill someone tonight."

The words came out before she'd decided to say them. That happened sometimes — the bond eroding the membrane between thought and speech, making her blunt in ways she'd never been before the binding. She had been careful, once. Measured. A healer trained at the Alcazar of St. Ines, where the first thing they taught was that a healer's hands must be steady and a healer's mouth must be shut.

Tallis didn't deny it. He sat very still on the table, shirtless, the cut glistening, and she felt him rearranging something inside himself — not the dampening this time but a more deliberate restructuring, like a man tidying a room before someone enters it.

"You felt the fight," he said. "Not the ending."

"I felt the ending. The way everything in you went quiet all at once, like a door slamming. And then the — " She stopped. The aftermath. The adrenaline receding and beneath it something vast and cold that she could only call satisfaction, though it was worse than satisfaction. It was the feeling of a task completed by a body that was very good at completing tasks.

"That's the part you need to stop doing," she said. "The quiet after. It comes through like a flood."

"I'll work on it."

"You'll work on it." She pressed the wound together with her fingers, and her healing gift reached for the edges of the cut — not through her hands but through the bond itself, the magic finding the shorter route, traveling the connection between their nervous systems like water finding the lowest point. The tissue began to knit. She watched it happen: the parting flesh remembering itself, drawing closed like a curtain at the end of something.

He drew a breath. She felt it twice — once through her ears and once in her own lungs, the bond doubling the sensation so that his intake of air registered as a small expansion in her chest. She'd learned to ignore most of the minor transmissions. Breath. Heartbeat. The slow tidal rhythm of a body at rest. But when she was touching him, everything amplified. His relief as the wound sealed became her relief. His skin cooling as the inflammation subsided became a coolness along her own ribs, phantom and precise.

"Done," she said, and stepped back. The distance helped. Three feet and the bond quieted to its usual low hum — a frequency she'd come to think of as the sound of him existing. Always there. Always the faint awareness of another person occupying a room in her chest that she had never agreed to furnish.

He put his shirt back on. Blood-stiffened, but he wore it without complaint. She'd noticed early that he never complained about discomfort — not the pain, not the hours, not the cold limestone corridors of the palace in winter when the heating spells failed on the lower levels where spymasters were apparently expected to live. The absence of complaint was itself a kind of information. A man who does not complain about cold is a man who has accepted cold as the temperature of his life.

"Seren."

She was washing her hands in the copper basin. The water turned pink.

"The binding records — do you know where they're kept?"

She shook the water from her fingers. "The College archives. Why?"

"Curiosity."

The grain of that word: smooth. He was telling the truth about the curiosity. But beneath it, something else — a texture she'd come to recognize as planning. Tallis Dane did not ask idle questions. Every inquiry was a reconnaissance of its own, the gathering of material he might need later for purposes she would not learn until the purposes were already accomplished.

"Goodnight, Tallis."

"Goodnight."

He left. She felt him go — down the corridor, through the east wing, down two flights of stairs to his quarters. She felt the moment he undressed, the shirt peeling away from the new scar with a small pull that registered in her own ribs as a memory of adhesion. She felt the moment he lay down. She felt the moment he stopped dampening, as he always did just before sleep, and the full undiluted wash of his exhaustion poured through the bond like ink dropped in water — spreading, staining, impossible to contain.

She stood in the infirmary with wet hands and his tiredness in her bones and thought: I will not survive this the way I am. Something will have to change, or something will have to break, and I do not yet know which one I prefer.

---

The court called it the Tethering.

Lord Chancellor Everad had explained the political value in terms so reasonable they almost disguised themselves as something other than orders. The healer and the spymaster, linked. She could feel when he was in danger — a built-in alarm for the crown's most valuable operative. He could feel when she was working a difficult healing, could gauge whether she needed protection or time or reinforcement. The strategic applications were, the chancellor said, considerable.

He did not mention the rest of it. He did not mention that the bond transmitted desire.

Seren had discovered this on the eleventh day, in the council chamber, when Tallis was standing six feet away discussing troop movements with the Master of the Northern Reach and she had been struck — there was no other word — by a wave of want so acute it stopped her breath. It was not hers. She knew this because she had been reviewing the apothecary requisitions and thinking about the rosemary supply and not about him at all, and then suddenly she was thinking about nothing except the particular curve where his neck met his shoulder, and the thought had arrived with a physical signature she recognized as foreign: his, not hers, transmitted through the bond with the same fidelity as his pain.

He had been looking at her across the room. When their eyes met, the want doubled — hers joining his in the bond's circuit, amplifying, reflecting, feeding back — and for two seconds the council chamber disappeared and there was only the sensation of being looked at by someone who could not look away, and knowing that your want was being felt in real time by the person causing it.

She'd left the room. She'd left the room and gone to the infirmary and ground yarrow for an hour, because yarrow was for wound-closing and she needed to close something.

They had not discussed it. This was the arrangement: they did not discuss what the bond showed them. They discussed logistics. When he would report for injury treatment. When she would need advance warning of a particularly demanding healing so that the drain on her reserves — which the bond made his reserves too, now — could be managed. They were professional. They were a political asset operating within defined parameters.

The parameters did not account for the night she woke at three in the morning and felt him dreaming about her.

Not the content. She couldn't read his thoughts. But the tenor of the dream — warm, slow, suffused with a tenderness so careful it ached — came through the bond like light through stained glass: fragmented, colored, unmistakable. She lay in her bed in the healer's quarters and felt herself being loved by a man who was asleep and could not censor what he felt, and she was so angry she pressed her face into the pillow and bit down on the linen until the anger became something she could carry.

Because the anger was hers. That was the one thing the bond had not stolen. Her fury at the binding, at the crown that ordered it, at the magic that enforced it, at Tallis for being the specific person her nervous system had been soldered to — all of that belonged to her in a way nothing else did anymore. She could not be certain her desire was authentic. She could not be certain her tenderness was not an echo of his. But the anger — the white, structural fury of a woman whose interior life had been made into government property — that was Seren's and no one else's.

She held it the way she held a scalpel: with purpose and without apology.

She held it and she did not put it down and she did not let it soften into something more comfortable, because comfortable anger was not anger at all but resignation wearing a braver face, and Seren was not resigned. She was furious. She would be furious for as long as the bond lasted, which the court physicians said was permanent, which meant she would be furious for the rest of her life, and she could live with that. Fury was at least a room she had built herself.

Six weeks later, the crown asked them to weaponize it.

"Reconnaissance," Everad said, which was the word he always used when he meant something worse. They were in his study, the three of them — Seren, Tallis, and the Lord Chancellor — and the fire was too hot and the room smelled of the beeswax Everad used on his boots, and Seren's gift was reaching reflexively toward the burn on the chambermaid's wrist she'd noticed on the way in and wondering why no one had treated it.

"The Saltmarche delegation arrives in three days. Emissary Lorne is suspected of carrying instructions from the pretender to the southern houses. Tallis will attend the reception as a minor functionary. If he can get close enough to observe, the bond will transmit his readings to Seren — his physical responses to Lorne's statements, the subtle cues that indicate deception — and Seren, from the safety of the infirmary, can interpret them."

"I'm a healer," Seren said. "Not a cipher."

"You are a healer who can feel when a man is lying, through the body of another man standing next to him. The distinction is academic."

The distinction was not academic. The distinction was the entire shape of her life: healer or weapon, restorer or instrument, a woman who chose what to mend or a woman told where to point.

She looked at Tallis. He was doing the thing he did in political rooms — the careful stillness, the dampened bond, the face that gave away nothing. But the dampening itself was information. She could feel him pressing down on something, and the effort told her what the silence was hiding: he didn't want this either. He didn't want her used this way.

He wouldn't say so. He never said so. Tallis Dane had been in the crown's service since he was sixteen years old, pulled from a village in the Hartwell Reaches whose only export was timber and boys too clever for their own survival. He had been trained, honed, pointed at the kingdom's enemies with the same care one applies to a blade — and he had loved it, the way Seren had loved healing, because the alternative was to admit that the thing that gave your life structure was also the thing eating it from the inside.

"We'll need to practice," Tallis said. Neutral. Professional.

"Yes," Everad said. "You will."

---

Practice meant proximity.

Practice meant sitting across from him in the infirmary with the door closed while he cycled through emotional states and she tried to catalog what each one felt like through the bond. They started with the obvious ones. Anger was hot and low, settled in the belly like a coal that hadn't been banked properly. Fear was high and bright, a metallic taste behind the teeth that she recognized from her own emergency healings — the body's copper alarm. Contempt was surprisingly cold, a withdrawal rather than an emission, as though the person feeling it had pulled back from the world by a fraction of an inch.

She recorded each one in her clinical hand, building a lexicon of his interior states the way a navigator builds a chart of coastline — not the territory itself, but enough of its shape to avoid the rocks. He cycled through emotions with the discipline of a man who had been trained to produce feelings on command, which she supposed he had. Spycraft was performance. The difference between Tallis and an actor was that an actor went home after.

Deception was the hardest: a particular quality of controlled blankness, the sensation of a surface held deliberately smooth while something churned beneath it. It took her four sessions to isolate it, because deception was the thing he did most naturally, the state he inhabited so completely that its texture was nearly indistinguishable from his resting affect. The lie and the man had grown together, like a vine through a lattice, until she could not tell which was supporting which.

"That," she said. "When you're lying, it feels like holding still too hard. Like the effort of not moving becomes its own kind of movement."

He looked at her with something she couldn't name. "You can feel when I'm lying."

"I can feel when you're working at something. The lying has a specific — " She searched for the word. "Grain. Like wood. The truth is smooth. A lie has grain, and I can feel which direction it runs."

"And if I'm lying about something small? Whether I've eaten, whether I slept."

"The grain is finer. Smaller lies have a tighter weave. But it's still there."

He was quiet for a moment. She felt him considering the implications — not with alarm but with the methodical calm of a man reassessing a tactical situation. He had spent his career being unreadable. Now there was one person in the world who could read him, and that person had been assigned to him by the same crown he served, and the crown expected reports.

"What am I doing now?"

She closed her eyes. Through the bond: warmth. Steadiness. The held-breath quality of attention, as though his entire body were listening. And beneath that, running beneath it like a river under ice, the thing she had been pretending not to notice for three months. Want. Not the crashing wave from the council chamber — this was quieter. Sustained. The long, steady pull of a man who had been wanting something for so long it had become a feature of his landscape, a geological formation, something that had been there longer than either of them had been paying attention.

"You're telling the truth," she said, and opened her eyes, and did not say what the truth was.

---

The Saltmarche reception was a disaster in the way that successful operations are disasters — effective and costly.

Tallis moved through the receiving hall in his borrowed functionary's clothes, unremarkable, forgettable, a man-shaped shadow drifting close enough to Emissary Lorne to read the room through proximity. And Seren sat in the infirmary and felt everything: the wine Tallis did not drink but held for cover, the weight of it in his hand transmitting through the bond as a faint pressure in her own palm. The conversation he could not hear clearly but could feel the shape of — Lorne's voice as vibration in Tallis's chest, and beneath the words, the telltale grain of deception. Lorne was lying. About what, Tallis's body couldn't tell her. But the fact of the lie came through the bond with the clarity of a bell, and Seren noted the time and the duration and the physical signature, and when it was done she wrote her report in the precise clinical hand she'd been trained to use at St. Ines, and the report read like a medical document because that was the only way she could bear to write it.

SUBJECT EXHIBITED SUSTAINED DECEPTIVE AFFECT FROM 21:40 TO 21:55, CONCENTRATED DURING DISCUSSION OF TARIFF RENEGOTIATION. SECONDARY DECEPTIVE CLUSTER AT 22:10, BRIEFER, ASSOCIATED WITH PERSONAL INQUIRIES REGARDING HIS FAMILY. RECOMMENDED ACTION: FURTHER OBSERVATION.

She put down the pen and sat in the dark infirmary and felt Tallis walking back through the corridors toward her, his footsteps translating through the bond as a rhythm she could have followed blindfolded, and she thought: this is what they've made us. A listening device with a heartbeat. An intelligence operation disguised as two people.

He came in without knocking. He always came in without knocking. His face in the lamplight had the scraped-clean look of a man who has been performing pleasantness for three hours and has run out of the raw material.

"I need a drink," he said.

"The infirmary has surgical spirits."

"I need a drink that hasn't been near a wound."

"Everything in this room has been near a wound. Including us."

He sat on the examination table. She noticed he always chose the same spot — the end closest to the window, where the moonlight came in on clear nights and the lamplight didn't reach. She noticed everything about him now. The bond had made an inventory of his habits and stored them in her body, so that his patterns lived in her the way melodies live in a musician — not thought but reflex.

"Lorne is running correspondence for the pretender," he said. "Your report confirmed what we suspected."

"My report." The words tasted wrong. Clinical language applied to an act of intimacy — because that was what the reading had been, whether she wanted to name it or not. She had spent three hours inside his body, feeling the reception through his senses, and now the product of that immersion was a document in the chancellor's hands. Intelligence. Derived from the bond. Derived from her.

"I'll need to handle it," he said.

"Handle."

"Handle."

The word sat between them. She knew what it meant. She would feel it happen — the handling — through the bond. She would lie in her bed and feel him doing the thing the crown required, and her magic would lunge toward him in the aftermath, trying to heal the damage, and she would have to decide again whether to let the healing happen or to clamp down on it, because healing him after he killed meant her gift was complicit, and complicity meant she was no longer a healer in any sense she recognized.

"I'm going to say something," she said, "and I need you to not dampen."

He looked at her. The dampening lifted. The bond opened the way a window opens — not all at once but in degrees, the latch turning, the frame shifting, and then the full rush of whatever weather was on the other side. She felt him: tired, alert, and underneath the operational calm a loneliness so thorough it had become architectural, had become the walls he lived inside, so that he no longer noticed it as a mood but experienced it as the shape of every room.

"This is destroying me," she said. "Not the bond. The pretending. We sit in this room and we catalog deception like it's a medical condition, and we practice reading each other like we're instruments, and the entire time you are wanting me and I can feel it and we say nothing, and the nothing is louder than anything Lorne could transmit in a hundred receptions."

The infirmary was very quiet. She could hear the surgical spirits settling in their bottles, the faint ticking of glass against glass.

"I know," he said.

"That's not enough."

"What would be enough?"

"I don't know. But not this. Not the — " She gestured at the space between them, the three feet of infirmary air that the bond crossed without effort and their bodies had never crossed at all. "The professional distance. As though we're colleagues. As though I don't know what your grief tastes like."

He was looking at her the way he had looked at her in the council chamber — the long, exposed look that the bond made impossible to misread. She could feel what it cost him. The vulnerability landed in her chest like something set down carefully, the way you set down a glass you know will break if you're careless.

"Seren." Her name in his mouth. She'd heard it a hundred times. Through the bond, it arrived differently — not just the sound but the feeling behind it, the particular tenderness he could not suppress when he said it, the way her name lived in him as something gentle in a life that had very little gentleness. "If I stop pretending, I can't start again. I don't have another version. There's the one where I don't say it, and there's the one where I do, and the second one doesn't have a door back."

"I know."

"The crown — "

"The crown can feel what we report. The crown can't feel this." She touched her own sternum, where the bond lived, where he lived. "This frequency. Whatever this is. It's not in the register they're listening for."

"How do you know?"

"Because it's too small. Too specific. It's the sensation of you saying my name and meaning it. No instrument measures that. No chancellor requisitions it. It belongs to us, or it belongs to nothing."

He was quiet for a long time. She could feel him thinking — not the content but the weight of it, the serious, structural consideration of a man who had spent twenty years making decisions that other people survived or didn't. Then the thinking shifted, and what replaced it was something she had only felt once before, in the middle of the night when he'd been dreaming. Tenderness. Unguarded. The real thing, not performed for the bond or shaped by training but simply and helplessly present, the way heat is present in a body — not a choice but a condition of being alive.

He reached for her hand. She let him take it.

The bond flooded. Everything at once — his relief and her anger and his want and her want and the grief of two people who could never be certain whether what they felt was theirs or the magic's, and beneath all of it, quieter than any of the rest, the small stubborn warmth of choosing to reach across the distance anyway. Not because the bond demanded it. Not because the crown required it. But because his hand was there, and hers was here, and the space between them had always been a choice they were refusing to make.

She did not forgive the bond. She did not forgive the crown. She sat in the infirmary with his hand in hers and the surgical spirits ticking in their bottles and the moonlight coming through the window, and she was furious and she was tender and she did not ask which feeling was authentic because the question had stopped mattering, or because the question had never mattered, or because mattering was not the point.

The point was his hand. The point was her hand. The point was the warmth between them that the bond transmitted and the crown could not requisition and the kingdom did not own.

It was too small to be a weapon. It was too specific to be a tool.

It was a private language, and they were its only speakers, and Seren held onto it the way she held onto her anger — with both hands and no intention of letting go.

---

In the morning, she filed her report on the Saltmarche reception. Clinical. Precise. Everything the crown needed to act on Lorne.

She did not file a report on what happened after.

Some intelligence is not for the kingdom. Some frequencies belong only to the people who can hear them. Seren knew this the way she knew most things now — through the bond, through the body, through the particular and unrepeatable sensation of another person's heartbeat in her chest, which was a violation and a gift and a condition of weather, and which she carried forward into the next day and the day after and the day after that, unnamed and unresolved and hers.
