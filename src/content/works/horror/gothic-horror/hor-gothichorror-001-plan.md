### Selected Concepts (2):

**1. Capillary action / rising damp**: Water that climbs through porous walls against gravity, drawn upward by the material's own structure. In the story, this becomes a literal and metaphorical phenomenon -- the hacienda's limestone walls draw moisture from the ground in patterns the narrator interprets as stains that move, that form shapes, that trace the outlines of hands. In the past timeline, the walls are described as perpetually damp from the henequen processing nearby, the fibers soaked and scraped. Rising damp becomes the house's circulation -- water carrying the residue of what happened in the soil upward through the walls, making the foundation's history visible at eye level. The narrator, as an architectural surveyor, would know exactly what capillary action is, which makes her inability to explain why the damp stains seem to change positions more disturbing.

**2. Autophagy (cellular self-digestion)**: The biological process where a cell consumes its own components for survival. The hacienda is engaged in architectural autophagy -- it is cannibalizing its own structure. Rooms that the narrator remembers from childhood visits have been absorbed into other rooms. Walls have been broken through and resealed in different configurations. The house is rearranging itself by consuming and reconstituting its own material. This maps onto the family's history: each generation consumed the previous one's legacy, broke through its boundaries, resealed its secrets into different walls. The narrator's great-aunt, who lived in the hacienda for fifty years, was the last person the house digested. Whether she died there or simply became part of its structure is one of the story's unresolved ambiguities.

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Edgar Allan Poe -- "The Fall of the House of Usher" (Author A)

**Source**: "The Fall of the House of Usher," opening and key passages
**Why representative**: Demonstrates Poe's architecture-as-psychology technique, the fevered accumulation of sensory details, the unreliable narrator whose emotional state distorts perception, and the fundamental Poe principle that house and inhabitant are the same organism.

Key stylistic elements to emulate:
- Triadic listing that builds rhythmic momentum: "an iciness, a sinking, a sickening of the heart"
- The narrator who attempts rational analysis of irrational dread and fails
- Anthropomorphization of architecture: "vacant eye-like windows," the house as watching entity
- The crack in the house = the crack in the mind -- never stated, always felt
- Sentences that spiral outward with qualifying clauses, delay meaning, create the prose equivalent of rooms you cannot find the exit from
- The final collapse where inner and outer worlds merge: psychological dissolution manifests as architectural collapse

In the present timeline of our story, Poe's influence should dominate. The prose should become increasingly fevered, sentences compressing as the house closes in. The narrator's attempt to remain rational -- she is a surveyor, she works in measurements and assessments -- should fail in the same way Poe's narrators always fail: not dramatically, but through the gradual erosion of the distinction between what they observe and what they feel.

### Sample 2: Mariana Enriquez (Author B)

**Source**: Style analysis drawn from *Things We Lost in the Fire* and critical assessment
**Why representative**: Demonstrates Enriquez's signature technique of beginning in contemporary normality and shifting into supernatural territory, her flat affect when describing horror, her use of political violence as the source material for hauntings.

Key stylistic elements to emulate:
- Matter-of-fact dread: the supernatural described in the same register as grocery shopping or commuting. No heightened language. The horror IS the flatness.
- Social realism as the substrate of horror: ghosts emerge from specific political histories (Argentina's Dirty War, disappeared persons, economic collapse), not from metaphysical evil
- Urban/suburban settings where horror is baked into infrastructure: neighborhoods built on mass graves, houses where violence happened and was papered over
- A collective voice that amplifies social resonance -- horror as shared condition, not individual pathology
- The refusal to resolve: Enriquez's stories often end with the horror still present, normalized, absorbed into daily life
- Gothic tropes recontextualized: ghosts as memory and trauma that cannot be shed, that repeat, that are structural

In the past timeline, Enriquez's influence should dominate. Flat, precise, journalistic prose. "A man was beaten for failing to meet his henequen quota. His name was Jacinto Poot. He was nineteen." No commentary, no atmospheric embellishment. The violence is reported. The horror is that it was legal, ordinary, a line item in the hacienda's accounting.

### Sample 3: Henry James -- "The Turn of the Screw" (Work X)

**Source**: *The Turn of the Screw*, frame narrative and governess's account
**Why representative**: Demonstrates James's technique of nested unreliability, his long qualifying clauses that mirror consciousness processing ambiguous experience, and the fundamental structural principle of horror that depends entirely on whether you believe the narrator.

Key passages and techniques:
- The frame narrative: Douglas introduces the manuscript at a Christmas gathering, placing one more layer of distance between reader and events. "It's beyond everything. Nothing at all that I know touches it." -- the deliberate vagueness that heightens rather than diminishes.
- The governess's first vision on the tower: "The gold was still in the sky, the clearness in the air, and the man who looked at me over the battlements was as definite as a picture in a frame." -- simultaneous certainty and doubt. The figure is clear, but the clarity itself is suspicious. Things that clear are either real or hallucinated with conviction.
- James's qualifying clauses: "I caught at a dozen possibilities, none of which made a difference for the better" -- the mind processing ambiguity in real time, each qualification opening another door the reader cannot close.
- The structural principle: NEVER confirm or deny the supernatural. Every event supports both readings simultaneously. The governess may see real ghosts. The governess may be projecting her own desires and fears onto innocent children. James gives the reader no resolution and makes both readings equally supported.

In our story, the Turn of the Screw influence manifests as the frame narrative structure (the legal deposition) and the fundamental ambiguity about whether the house is supernatural or the narrator is unreliable. Every supernatural event MUST have a plausible psychological explanation. Every psychological explanation MUST have a supernatural crack in it.

### Sample 4: Silvia Moreno-Garcia -- *Mexican Gothic* (Work Y)

**Source**: *Mexican Gothic*, thematic and structural analysis
**Why representative**: Demonstrates the house-as-organism that feeds on inhabitants, colonial violence encoded in architecture, beauty and rot as simultaneous conditions, and the specific Yucatan/Mexican Gothic tradition.

Key thematic elements:
- High Place (the house) was built on wealth from exploiting indigenous miners. The house's horror is inseparable from the colonial extraction that funded it. The Doyle family poisoned their workers to feed the fungus that grants them immortality -- a literal biologization of how colonial wealth converts human lives into structural permanence.
- The house is ALIVE -- not metaphorically but biologically. A fungal network connects all inhabitants, dissolves boundaries between self and house. The family is an organism; the house is its body.
- Beauty and rot occupy the same space: ornate Victorian furnishings covered in mold, elegant wallpaper peeling over fungal growth, the hostess serving dinner while the house digests the guests.
- The protagonist (Noemi) is an outsider who enters the house to save her cousin -- and gradually discovers that "saving" someone from the house may be impossible because the house has already incorporated them.
- Colonial violence as ARCHITECTURAL: the house's very structure encodes the violence that produced it. You cannot renovate it away. You cannot sell it. The foundation remembers.

In our story, this influence manifests in the hacienda itself -- a henequen plantation hacienda built on Maya debt peonage. The house's beauty (European architecture, imported tiles, a library of French novels) exists simultaneously with the violence that funded it. The family is consumed by the house across generations. The house's "appetite" may be supernatural or may be the structural inevitability of living inside a monument to exploitation.

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

A 31-year-old architectural surveyor returns to her dead great-aunt's henequen hacienda in Yucatan to complete a condition assessment for the estate sale. The property was built during the Porfirio Diaz era on the labor of enslaved Maya workers, and the family has lived off its diminishing returns for four generations. The surveyor's report -- delivered as a legal deposition after something goes wrong -- becomes the frame narrative, and the reader cannot determine whether the house is genuinely consuming her or whether the guilt encoded in its walls has driven her to consume herself. Two timelines run in parallel: the hacienda's construction in 1891 (told in flat, documentary prose) and the surveyor's three days inside it in the present (told in increasingly fevered, unreliable first person). The connection between the timelines -- what the past built and what the present inherits -- is the story's real subject.

### 2. Protagonist

**Renata Gallardo**, 31, architectural condition surveyor based in Merida. She specializes in heritage properties -- old haciendas the state wants to catalog before developers convert them into boutique hotels. She is practical, trained in materials science, and fluent in the vocabulary of structural failure: subsidence, rising damp, load-bearing compromise, capillary action.

- **What she WANTS**: To complete the survey, file the report, facilitate the estate sale, and never return. She has no sentimental attachment to the property. She barely knew her great-aunt Consuelo, who lived alone in the hacienda for the last thirty years and died there six months ago.
- **What she NEEDS**: To acknowledge that her family's comfort -- her education, her apartment in Merida, her professional credentials -- was built on the same extraction the hacienda represents. The house is not an ancestral embarrassment to be liquidated. It is the structural foundation of everything she is.
- **Her flaw**: Renata believes that naming things accurately makes them manageable. She reduces the hacienda to a technical document: moisture readings, structural grades, square footage. This is her defense against the house's emotional and historical claims on her. She cannot feel what the house wants her to feel because she has replaced feeling with measurement. The house -- or her guilt -- will dismantle this defense.

### 3. Structure (DUAL TIMELINE)

The story alternates between two timelines, marked by distinct prose registers:

**PAST (1891-1893)**: The construction and first years of Hacienda Gallardo. Told in third person, flat, documentary prose (Enriquez register). Short declarative sentences. Specific names, dates, quantities. No interiority -- only observable action. This timeline covers: the laying of the foundation on cleared Maya forest land, the importation of European materials, the installation of the henequen processing machinery, the establishment of the debt peonage system, the first deaths of workers on the property, the first social gathering in the completed casa principal. This timeline is NOT haunted. It does not need to be. The facts are sufficient.

**PRESENT (three days, unnamed year)**: Renata's survey of the hacienda. Told in first person as a legal deposition -- she is explaining to an unnamed authority what happened during her three days in the house. The prose starts technical and controlled (measurement language, surveyor's precision) and progressively deteriorates. Sentences lengthen, then compress. Observations that begin as professional assessments become confessions. By the end, the reader cannot tell whether Renata is describing what the house did to her or constructing a defense for what she did to herself.

**FRAME**: The deposition format. Renata is speaking to someone -- a lawyer, an investigator, an insurance adjuster -- who never speaks back. The reader receives only her account. The frame reminds us constantly that this is testimony, not truth. Every supernatural description is also a plea: *You have to understand, the stains were moving.* Are you describing what happened, or building a case?

#### Beat Structure:

**Opening (Present)**: Renata's deposition begins. She states her qualifications, the purpose of her visit, the date of arrival. Professional, clipped. She mentions, almost as an aside, that she needs to explain what happened to her hand. (The hand is never fully explained.)

**Beat 1 (Past, 1891)**: The clearing of the land. The foreman, a man named Desiderio Gallardo (Renata's great-great-great-grandfather's agent, not the owner himself -- the owner is in Merida), directs Maya laborers to cut limestone from a cenote-adjacent quarry. One worker, Jacinto Poot, age 19, falls into the quarry and breaks both legs. He is carried to the workers' quarters. He never walks again. His debt is transferred to his younger brother.

**Beat 2 (Present)**: Renata enters the hacienda. She describes it in surveyor's language: moisture readings, wall composition, foundation type. But the limestone walls show capillary damp patterns she cannot account for -- the stains rise higher than the water table should allow, and they seem to form shapes that are almost legible, like handprints pressed from inside the stone. She notes this professionally. Then she mentions that the shapes were not there when she first measured the wall. She corrects herself. They must have been. She is here to measure, not to interpret.

**Beat 3 (Past, 1892)**: The casa principal is completed. European tiles, a ballroom with French doors, a library. The owner, Don Augusto Gallardo, arrives from Merida with his wife, Beatriz. The hacienda holds its first party. On the same night, in the workers' quarters, a woman named Teodora Canul dies giving birth. The midwife says the baby came out facing backward, looking at where it had been instead of where it was going. The sheets are burned in the courtyard. The ash drifts toward the casa principal. Nobody at the party notices.

**Beat 4 (Present)**: Night one. Renata sleeps in the master bedroom -- the only room with an intact roof. She wakes to the sound of water moving inside walls. She gets up to check for pipe leaks (there are no functioning pipes -- the hacienda has been disconnected from water for years). The damp stains have risen. They are at eye level now. She takes photographs. Later, reviewing them, the stains don't appear. She attributes this to poor lighting. She mentions in the deposition that she wishes she could show the photographs. She cannot. Her phone was recovered from the property, but the images were corrupted. She says "corrupted." The word hangs there.

**Beat 5 (Past, 1893)**: The debt ledger. A full accounting of what each worker owes, in pesos and centavos. The prose is nothing but numbers and names. Jacinto Poot, who broke his legs, owes 47 pesos for the cart that transported him to the workers' quarters, 12 pesos for the cotton wrapping for his legs, 8 pesos for corn consumed during his recovery. His total debt has tripled since his injury. His brother, who assumed the debt, has added his own. The ledger is a living document. It grows. The debts are structural: they are the mortar between the stones.

**Beat 6 (Present)**: Day two. Renata cannot find the room she slept in. The hallway configuration has changed -- or she has misremembered it. She pulls out her floor plan from the survey and follows it. The floor plan leads her to a wall where there should be a door. She checks her measurements. They are correct. The wall is wrong. Or the measurements were always wrong and she is only now discovering it. She begins to hear a rhythmic sound from deep in the house -- a scraping, like stone being worked. She describes it in the deposition as "consistent with structural settling" but the rhythm is too regular. Too deliberate. Like someone quarrying limestone in a precise, repeated motion.

**Beat 7 (Past, 1893)**: Beatriz Gallardo, the owner's wife, stops leaving the house. She was known in Merida for her social energy. Now she sits in the library and reads the same French novel (Madame Bovary, though she never mentions the title, only describes "the woman who wanted more than what she was given"). She tells a visiting priest that the house speaks to her at night. The priest notes this in a letter to his bishop. He uses the word "impressionable." He does not use the word "haunted." This is the only time in the past timeline that anyone attributes intention to the house, and it is immediately discredited by a man with institutional authority.

**Crisis (Present)**: Night two. Renata's deposition becomes confused. She describes finding a room she has never seen before -- a room below the ground floor, accessible through a door that was previously a wall. The room is empty except for stains on the floor that she recognizes as the same pattern as the rising damp on the upper walls, except here they are pressed into the limestone from above, as though someone lay on the floor and the floor absorbed their outline. She counts the stains. There are many. She begins to measure them -- her professional reflex -- and discovers that her measuring tape has changed. Or she has forgotten how to read it. The numbers are in the wrong order. She says this in the deposition and then immediately says, "I know how that sounds." She asks for water. The deposition notes a pause.

**Ending (dual)**:

PAST: The hacienda is finished. All rooms are painted. The henequen fields stretch to the horizon. Workers move between the rows in the early morning. The casa principal is described in a real estate listing from 1894, found in the Merida municipal archive: four bedrooms, a ballroom, a library, European tile, limestone walls, excellent condition. The listing does not mention workers' quarters. The listing does not mention the quarry. The listing does not mention Jacinto Poot or Teodora Canul or any of the debts. The house is presented as though it built itself.

PRESENT: Renata says she left the hacienda on the morning of the third day. She filed her condition report. She recommended against purchase. In the final paragraph of the deposition, she is asked (we infer from her answer) about the condition of her hand. She says the injury occurred when she fell in the lower room. She says it is healing. She says she can still feel it, the hand, she can feel it even though -- and here the deposition ends. The final line is a clerk's notation: *Deponent requested a break. Interview not resumed.*

The reader does not know what happened to Renata's hand. The reader does not know whether the lower room exists. The reader does not know whether Renata is inside or outside the house. The past timeline's silence -- its refusal to attribute anything supernatural to the hacienda -- makes the present timeline's ambiguity feel earned rather than arbitrary. The horror of the past is certain and documented. The horror of the present is uncertain and narrated by someone who may not be trustworthy. The connection between them -- what was built, what it cost, who inherits the debt -- is the story's real subject.

### 4. Key Scenes

1. **Renata measures the damp stains (Present, Beat 2)**: She applies her professional instruments to the capillary damp patterns on the limestone walls. The readings are anomalous -- moisture levels that suggest the walls are drawing water from far below the water table, or from no water source at all. This scene does double duty: it establishes Renata's character (she responds to the inexplicable with measurement) and introduces the house's central uncanny quality (the stains that move, that form shapes, that rise against the laws of physics she has been trained to trust).

2. **The debt ledger (Past, Beat 5)**: A full page of names and numbers. Jacinto Poot's debts. His brother's debts. The widow Teodora Canul's outstanding balance, which she incurred by dying and requiring burial. The prose is ONLY ledger entries -- no narration, no commentary. The horror is entirely in the numbers. This scene is where Enriquez's influence is most concentrated: matter-of-fact documentation of systemic violence, presented without emotional framing, which makes it more devastating than any gothic embellishment could.

3. **Renata cannot find her room (Present, Beat 6)**: The hallway configuration has changed. She follows her own floor plan and arrives at a wall. This scene is the crisis of her professional identity -- if her measurements are wrong, she is wrong. If the house has changed, her entire epistemological framework is wrong. The scene should be written in Poe's register: increasingly compressed, rhythmic, the syntax itself getting lost in corridors that do not connect.

4. **The lower room (Present, Crisis)**: Renata descends into a room that should not exist. The outlines on the floor. Her measuring tape betraying her. This is the story's deepest point -- literally underground, literally closest to the foundation, closest to whatever the house was built on. The scene should feel like premature burial (Poe) crossed with archaeological discovery (Enriquez). She is buried and she is excavating. She is inside the house and inside the history.

5. **The unfinished deposition (Present, Ending)**: The clerk's notation. The interview not resumed. This is where James's influence is most concentrated: the frame narrative that destabilizes everything inside it. The reader has been receiving Renata's testimony as though it were the story, but the frame reminds us that it was always mediated, always shaped by the need to be believed, always possibly a defense rather than an account.

### 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Opening**: Clinical distance. The reader feels the cool professionalism of a competent woman doing a technical job. There is a faint unease -- why is this a deposition? what happened to her hand? -- but the dominant tone is control.

**Middle**: The control erodes. The reader begins to feel the specific anxiety of trusting a narrator who is starting not to trust herself. The past timeline provides grounding -- its flatness is a relief from the present's growing instability -- but the grounding is itself disturbing, because what the past describes is terrible. The reader is caught between two kinds of horror: the certain horror of history and the uncertain horror of the present.

**Crisis**: Genuine disorientation. The reader should feel, physically, the sensation of being lost in a building where the floor plan keeps changing. The measuring tape scene should produce the specific vertigo of discovering that your instruments of understanding are unreliable. If the tape cannot be trusted, nothing can.

**Ending**: Unresolved dread. The reader finishes the story and cannot determine whether Renata is safe. The clerk's notation -- bureaucratic, indifferent -- should feel like a door closing. The past timeline's clean ending (the real estate listing that erases everything) should feel like a mirror of the reader's own desire to close the file on the hacienda and walk away. The reader wants to do what Renata may or may not have done: leave the house. The story does not confirm that leaving is possible.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Author A (Poe)** -- Most visible in: **Beat 6, the hallway scene.** Renata follows her floor plan and arrives at a wall. The prose here should be pure Poe: rhythmic, compounding, the syntax itself becoming a corridor that does not connect. Triadic constructions. The rational mind attempting to process the irrational and failing not through dramatic breakdown but through the gradual loss of the distinction between observation and hallucination. "I followed the plan. The plan said doorway. The wall said otherwise. One of us was built wrong."

**Author B (Enriquez)** -- Most visible in: **Beat 5, the debt ledger.** Flat, documentary, no emotional framing. Names and numbers. The horror is that this was legal, ordinary, bureaucratic. Enriquez's signature move: the supernatural is absent, but the real is worse. This scene should read like a page torn from an actual archive -- no narrative voice, no commentary, just the accounting that converted human lives into the mortar between stones. Also present in the past timeline generally: the matter-of-fact tone of violence reported rather than dramatized.

**Work X (Turn of the Screw)** -- Most visible in: **The frame narrative structure itself.** The deposition format is our version of Douglas reading the governess's manuscript at the Christmas gathering. It places one more layer of distance between reader and events. Every description of the supernatural is simultaneously a plea for belief. The reader can never fully trust Renata because the deposition format constantly reminds us she is constructing an account, not transmitting reality. The final clerk's notation is our equivalent of James's ending: a bureaucratic frame that refuses to confirm or deny.

**Work Y (Mexican Gothic)** -- Most visible in: **The hacienda itself and Beat 3 (the party/death parallel).** The house built on colonial extraction. Beauty and violence as simultaneous conditions: European tiles above, workers' blood below. The party in the casa principal while Teodora Canul dies in the workers' quarters -- the same night, the same property, two realities occupying the same architectural space. The fungal network in Mexican Gothic becomes our capillary damp: moisture that carries the foundation's history upward through the walls, making what was buried visible at eye level. The house consumes its inhabitants not through biology (Moreno-Garcia's fungus) but through the ambiguous mechanism of inherited guilt given architectural form.

### 7. Title

**Capillary Trespass**

Rationale: "Capillary" references the rising damp that is the story's central uncanny image -- water climbing through walls, the house's history rising from the foundation to become visible. "Trespass" has three meanings: the physical trespass of entering a property (Renata entering the hacienda), the moral trespass of the hacienda's founding violence (the debt peonage, the exploitation of Maya labor), and the structural trespass of the past into the present (the dual timeline's real subject). The title is technical enough to sound like something a surveyor would say, and sinister enough to sound like something the house would do.

Does not start with "The." Does not duplicate any existing title structure.

## Notes for the Writer

1. **Prose register is the primary marker of timeline.** The writer does NOT need headers or dates to distinguish past from present. The reader should feel the shift through the prose itself: flat and documentary for the past, increasingly compressed and unreliable for the present.

2. **The deposition frame should be light.** Not every present-timeline section needs explicit deposition markers. A few key moments -- "I need to explain about the hand," "I know how that sounds," the final clerk's notation -- are enough. The frame should feel like a glass wall the reader keeps bumping into, not a cage.

3. **The past timeline sections should be SHORT.** 200-400 words each. Punchy. Factual. They are interruptions -- the house's memory breaking through the narrator's account. The present timeline carries the emotional weight and takes more space.

4. **Renata's hand is the story's withheld information.** We learn that something happened to it. We never learn what. The reader should be able to construct at least three explanations: she injured it falling in the lower room (her claim), she injured it trying to break through a wall (the house trapped her), or she injured it herself (the guilt, the pressure, the house in her mind). Do NOT resolve this. The ambiguity is the point.

5. **No one in the past timeline attributes supernatural qualities to the house.** The past is documentary. The house is just a building. The violence is just policy. The only exception is Beatriz telling the priest the house speaks to her, and the priest immediately dismissing it. This exception proves the rule: in the past, the institutional framework does not permit the house to be haunted. In the present, the institutional framework (the deposition) does not permit it either -- but Renata keeps trying.

6. **The debt ledger scene should take up at least 200 words and contain NO narrative prose.** Just names, amounts, reasons. Let the reader do the emotional work. This is the Enriquez principle: the facts are the horror. Do not process them for the reader.

7. **Compression.** At 3,600 words, every sentence is load-bearing. No atmospheric padding. The house should feel compressed not because the writer describes compression but because the prose itself is compressed -- no room to breathe, no room to step back, no room to escape into summary or reflection. The reader is inside the house. The house is getting smaller.
