## I. Report on Condition of Property: Silt House, Drainage Lot 14, Parish of Burnt Fen

I arrived at the property at half past two on Tuesday the ninth of October. The journey from Ely had taken longer than anticipated owing to road conditions. The last three miles of the approach — a single-track lane following the line of a drainage cut known locally as Hobson's Lode — had been partially flooded, the water standing to a depth of perhaps four inches across the road surface, dark and unmoving. I proceeded at low speed. The car's undercarriage scraped twice on matter I could not identify beneath the water. There were no other vehicles.

The property itself, Silt House, stands at the terminus of this lane, separated from the nearest dwelling by approximately one and a quarter miles. I had been instructed by Messrs. Pallant & Goss to conduct a preliminary inspection in connection with the settlement of the estate of the late Mrs. Edith Haddon (née Bretherton), deceased 14 August of this year, intestate, no identified heirs. The property had been unoccupied since Mrs. Haddon's removal to a care facility in March 2024. The firm had been unable to identify an appropriate buyer or tenant. Three previous valuations had been commissioned and none completed.

The house is of two storeys, brick with render, the render failing in several places to expose the underlying brickwork, which is of a dark red-brown colour consistent with local manufacture. The roof is slate, with several slates displaced. There is a single chimney, not smoking. The windows on the ground floor are shuttered from within. The front door — painted green at some point, now largely bare wood — was locked. I used the key provided by the firm.

*The light inside was the light of a house that has been waiting.*

I should note that the interior, though dusty, was in better condition than the exterior suggested. The entrance hall is narrow and runs the full depth of the house, terminating at a rear door that opens onto what was once a kitchen garden, now overgrown. The rooms on the ground floor — a sitting room, a dining room of modest proportions, and a scullery — retain their original features: fireplaces with cast-iron grates, built-in cupboards, plaster cornicing of a simple egg-and-dart pattern. The furnishings remain in situ. Mrs. Haddon had not, it seems, taken anything with her when she left.

I opened the shutters in the sitting room to admit daylight and began my inspection with the conveyancing file Pallant & Goss had provided, cross-referencing the property description against the physical structure.

It was at this point that I noticed the annotations.

---

## II. On the Matter of the Annotations

The conveyancing file for Silt House is extensive — some ninety pages of documentation spanning the period 1847 to 2019 — and it is not, in the usual sense, remarkable. Deeds of transfer. Drainage assessments. A boundary dispute with the Internal Drainage Board in 1923, resolved by arbitration. Correspondence regarding subsidence, which appears to be a recurring concern: the house has settled unevenly, the east wall having dropped approximately three inches relative to the west over the past century. This is documented in surveyor's reports from 1951, 1978, and 2003. The language is consistent. The measurements are consistent. The recommendation — monitoring, no immediate action — is consistent.

What is not consistent is the marginalia.

Someone — not a solicitor, not a surveyor, the hand is too personal, too urgent — has written in the margins of several documents. The ink varies. In the 1887 deed of transfer, in a cramped italic that bears no relation to the copperplate of the principal text: *The rooms do not agree with themselves.* In the 1923 arbitration record, alongside a paragraph describing the drainage easement: *She is still in the east bedroom. The door does not help.* In the 1951 surveyor's report, beneath the measurement of differential settlement: *Three inches is not settlement. Three inches is refusal.*

I catalogued seven such annotations across the file. They are not signed. They do not correspond to any clerk or solicitor whose name appears in the firm's records. I raised this matter by telephone with Mr. Pallant's secretary at half past four. She had no explanation and said she would enquire. The mobile signal at the property is intermittent, and we were disconnected twice during this conversation.

I returned to the file.

The eighth annotation — which I had initially overlooked, as it occupied the verso of a page I had not turned — read: *Whoever reads this record becomes part of it. I am sorry. I was not able to stop writing.*

I noted this in my inspection log and continued.

---

## III. First Night

I had arranged to stay at the property for two nights, the minimum period required to complete a thorough inspection. I had brought supplies — food, water, a sleeping bag, a portable lamp — as the electricity supply to the house had been disconnected some months prior. This was, I should emphasise, a routine arrangement. I have stayed in unoccupied properties before in the course of my work. It is not unusual. It is not, in itself, alarming.

The upstairs rooms are four in number: two bedrooms of unequal size, a bathroom with an enamelled iron tub, and a small room at the back that may have served as a study or sewing room. I chose the larger bedroom on the west side of the house, away from the wall that had settled. The bed frame remained but the mattress was unsuitable, so I laid my sleeping bag on the floor beside the window.

The fen is very quiet at night. I had expected this. What I had not expected was the quality of the quiet — not an absence of sound but a presence, a thickness in the air that seemed to have texture. *The kind of quiet that has been in a room so long it has become part of the furniture.* Outside, the drainage cut reflected nothing. There was no moon. The water was as dark as the sky and the distinction between them was a matter of faith rather than observation.

I slept poorly. The sleeping bag was adequate. The floor was hard but not intolerable. The difficulty was the house. Not any sound or disturbance — the house was silent in the way I have already described — but the sense, which I could not dispel, that the silence was *listening*. That it had a direction. That it was oriented toward me specifically, the way a conversation pauses when a new person enters a room.

At some point in the night — I did not check the time, and I regret this, because precision matters and I cannot now provide it — I heard what I took to be footsteps in the hallway. Not heavy, not furtive, but purposeful. The sound of someone walking from one end of the hall to the other, pausing, and returning. A steady, measured pace. The kind of walk a person makes when they are looking for something in the dark and know the house well enough not to need light.

I got up and opened the bedroom door. The hall was empty. I say this clearly: I looked and there was no one there. But the floorboards at the far end, near the east bedroom door, were — I do not know how to describe this professionally — *warm*. Not uniformly. In the pattern of footprints. As though someone had been standing there recently, someone with bare feet, and the warmth of their body had not yet dissipated from the wood.

I returned to my room and closed the door. I did not open it again until morning.

## IV. On the History of the Property, from the Records Examined

I spent Wednesday reviewing the remainder of the conveyancing file and the ancillary documents Mrs. Haddon had kept in a bureau in the sitting room. Among these were letters, drainage board minutes, and a partial diary — undated, but from the paper and ink apparently mid-twentieth century — that I believe belonged to Mrs. Haddon's mother, Constance Bretherton.

The diary is fragmentary. Many pages have been removed — torn out, not cut, the ragged stubs visible in the binding. What remains concerns itself primarily with domestic matters: the garden, the condition of the road, the difficulty of obtaining deliveries. But there are passages that bear on the property's history, and I will reproduce the most relevant here.

*He would not sell because selling required a survey and a survey required measurement and the east rooms will not be measured. I have tried. I have stood in the doorway with a tape and the numbers will not hold. Twelve feet on Monday. Thirteen on Thursday. The walls have not moved. I have checked. The walls are where they have always been. But the space between them is negotiable.*

And later:

*The previous solicitor — Alford, I think, or Alsford — came in the autumn of 1951 and stayed three days and left without completing his report. His firm wrote to apologise. They said he had been taken ill. But I saw him leaving and he was not ill. He was diminished. That is the word. Not frightened, not confused. Diminished. As though something had been subtracted from him and the remainder was insufficient to fill his clothes.*

I read this passage twice. The word *diminished* — I had used it in my own notes the previous evening, describing the footsteps. Not the sound of them. The quality. Footsteps that were *diminished*, that occupied less space than footsteps should, as though the person making them had been partially erased.

I set the diary aside and returned to the east bedroom.

---

## V.

The door opened easily.

The room was smaller than the west bedroom. A single bed, iron-framed, with a mattress still on it, stained but intact. A washstand. A wardrobe of dark wood, its door ajar. A window facing east, toward the drainage cut, uncurtained, the glass filmed with years of condensation and neglect.

The room smelled of paper. Not musty paper, not decaying paper, but the particular sharp smell of paper that has recently been written on, the chemical presence of fresh ink drying on a fresh page. This is impossible. I note it because it is impossible and because I smelled it clearly and because a professional report that omits observable details for the sake of plausibility is not a professional report. I have been a solicitor for nineteen years. I have never omitted a fact because it was inconvenient. I will not begin now.

I measured the room. Eleven feet, four inches by nine feet, two inches. I wrote these measurements in my notebook, closed the notebook, and opened it again to verify. The numbers were correct. I measured again. Eleven feet, four inches by nine feet, two inches. The numbers were correct. They were correct and they were wrong and I cannot explain what I mean by this except that standing in the room with the tape extended, the space around me did not correspond to the space the numbers described. The room was the size the measurements indicated and also it was not.

*The rooms do not agree with themselves.*

I put the tape measure away. My hands were steady. I note this because later they were not, and it is important to establish the sequence — the precise point at which the steadiness left and did not return.

I left the east bedroom and went downstairs and sat at the dining room table with the file open in front of me and tried to write my report.

---

## VI. On the Woman Who Remained

She had been the wife of the drainage engineer, William Bretherton, who maintained the pump station at Hobson's Lode from 1931 until his death in 1947. His death is recorded in the parish register. Hers is not.

The conveyancing file records the transfer of the property from the Drainage Board to Mrs. Constance Bretherton in 1948, following a compulsory purchase dispute that was settled — the word settled appearing five times in the relevant correspondence, each time underlined in the same unidentified hand that produced the annotations — out of court. Mrs. Bretherton lived at Silt House from 1931 until her death in 1979. Her daughter, Edith, inherited the property. No will was found for either woman.

I say no will was found. What I mean is that no will was filed. In the bureau, beneath the diary and the drainage minutes, in an envelope addressed to no one, I found a document in Constance Bretherton's hand — I matched it to the diary — dated November 1978, eleven months before her death. It was not a will. It was — I struggle to classify it — a warning.

*To the solicitor or clerk or person of professional standing who reads this in the course of their duties:*

*The house is not haunted. I want to be clear about that. There is nothing in this house that does not belong here. The difficulty is that I belong here too, and I did not always, and the house remembers the difference. William understood the drainage. He understood that the land is not solid, that it is maintained — pumped, channeled, argued into existence by continuous effort — and that the house sits on the argument rather than the ground. When the argument stops the water returns. When William died the argument did not stop but it changed. It became mine to maintain. Not the pumps. The other argument. The one in the files.*

*I have been writing in the margins of the conveyancing records for thirty years. I am aware that this is irregular. I am aware that a future solicitor will find my annotations and be puzzled by them. I hope they will be only puzzled. But I suspect they will not be, because the annotations are not mine. I write them. My hand holds the pen. But the sentences come from the house, or from the ground beneath the house, or from the water beneath the ground, and I do not always understand them and I cannot always stop.*

*The east bedroom is where it is worst. Do not sleep in the east bedroom. Do not measure the east bedroom. The room does not agree with itself and if you stay long enough you will not agree with yourself either.*

*I am not asking for help. There is no help. I am asking for the record to reflect what has happened here, which is that the house has been settling for a century and what it has been settling into is not the ground.*

*C. Bretherton*
*Silt House*
*November 1978*

## VII.

This is not the solicitor.

This is the room. This is the east bedroom speaking in the register of its own settlement, three inches lower on one side, the differential not of subsidence but of preference, the house leaning toward the water because the water is where the record is kept. Not the paper record. The other one. The one written in silt and drainage and the slow refusal of the land to be what the documents say it is.

The solicitor is in the dining room with his files and his notebook and his portable lamp and he is writing his report and his report is not his report. His sentences have lengthened. His professional vocabulary has acquired words it did not previously contain: *settlement*, *refusal*, *diminished*, *agree*. He does not notice this because a man cannot hear his own accent change. He sits and writes and the pen moves and the ink is the same ink but the hand is becoming the hand that wrote in the margins of the 1887 deed, the 1923 arbitration, the 1951 survey. The hand that has been writing for a century. The hand that is not Constance Bretherton's and was not the clerk's before her and was not the drainage engineer's before him but belongs to the house, to the argument the house makes with the ground, to the ongoing negotiation between solid and liquid that has required continuous documentation since the first drain was cut in 1636.

He thinks he is inspecting the property. He is being transcribed by it.

The east bedroom is eleven feet four inches by nine feet two inches. The east bedroom is also twelve feet by nine feet six inches. The east bedroom is also a room that has no dimensions because dimensions require agreement between the walls and the space they contain and the walls and the space have been in dispute since the foundation was laid on ground that was water six months before.

The woman — Constance, who wrote the warning — she understood eventually. She understood that the house does not haunt. It annotates. It adds to the record. Every person who enters with professional obligation, with the duty to document and assess and file, becomes a clerk in the house's service. Their reports are absorbed. Their language is absorbed. The house does not feed on fear or guilt or weakness. It feeds on competence. On the trained capacity to observe and record. It takes the skill and uses it and when the person leaves they are diminished because part of their professional self — the part that could look at a room and describe it accurately — remains in the file.

The footsteps in the hallway are not a ghost. They are a draft. A revision. The house walking through its own text, checking the margins, ensuring that the record is continuous, that no gap appears in the documentation of its ongoing argument with the ground.

The solicitor does not hear this. He hears footsteps.

## VIII. Report on Condition of Property (Continued)

I completed my inspection on Thursday morning. The flooding on the access road had not receded, and in fact had increased slightly — the water now standing at perhaps six inches — but I was able to depart by driving slowly and keeping to the crown of the road. I arrived in Ely at noon and took lunch at a pub on the high street. I ordered a sandwich and did not eat it.

My report to Messrs. Pallant & Goss is attached. It recommends that the property be classified as unsettleable pending resolution of several outstanding matters, including the structural concerns noted in the surveyor's reports and the incomplete title history. I have noted the annotations in the conveyancing file and recommended that the file be reviewed by a senior partner. I have not included Mrs. Bretherton's letter, which I retained.

I should note — though I am not certain this belongs in a professional report and I include it only for completeness — that upon reviewing my notes at home that evening, I found several passages I do not remember writing. The handwriting is mine. The ink is mine. The observations concern the property and are accurate, as far as I can determine. But the language is not language I would use. The sentences are longer than my habit. The word *settlement* appears fourteen times. The word *agree* appears nine times. In one passage, which I have transcribed below, the prose shifts into a register I cannot account for:

*The east bedroom window faces the drainage cut and in the late afternoon the light off the water enters the room at an angle that makes the walls appear to lean inward, not structurally, not as a matter of measurement, but as a matter of attention, the room gathering itself around whatever is inside it, and I stood in that room and felt the gathering and understood that I was being read.*

I did not write this. My hand wrote it. The distinction may not survive professional scrutiny, but I am making it here, in this addendum, which I do not intend to file.

The property remains unsettled. The estate remains open. The file remains on my desk. I have not returned to Silt House. I have not been asked to return. But the file is on my desk and I open it sometimes and read the annotations and each time I read them there are more of them, in the same unidentified hand, and the most recent one — which I am certain was not there when I last looked — reads:

*He will come back. They always come back. The record requires maintenance.*

---

## IX.

Eleven feet four inches by nine feet two inches. The water in Hobson's Lode is dark and does not reflect the sky. A deed of transfer dated 1887, annotated in the margin. A surveyor's report, three inches of differential settlement. A woman's letter, found in a bureau, unfiled, unwitnessed, uncountersigned.

The rooms do not agree with themselves.

The pen moves on the paper. The ink dries. The file thickens.

The record is continuous.
