## I

The house had been built for two and was now occupied by one, and the arithmetic of this was visible in every room. Two chairs at the table but only the one near the stove showed the wear of daily use. Two hooks by the door but only the left hook held a coat. The bedroom had a bed sized for a marriage and slept at an angle, the sheets pulled toward the window side where Alma Goss lay each night with her face to the glass, watching the dark until the dark resolved into the particular gray that preceded dawn in the Manzano foothills, a gray that was not the absence of light but its first tentative negotiation with a landscape that gave nothing back without a fight.

She had been alone in the house for eleven months. Her husband, Phelan, had gone to Albuquerque for work in January and had not come back by February and had sent a letter in March saying he would not be coming back at all. The letter was two paragraphs. The first paragraph explained that he had found employment at the rail yards and had taken a room on Second Street. The second paragraph suggested she might want to sell the claim. He did not say to whom. He did not say he was sorry, either, which Alma appreciated, because sorry would have asked something of her and she had nothing left to give to a man who had walked away from a house in January and not even bothered to bank the stove.

She had come to New Mexico from Pennsylvania with Phelan four years ago, following a land agent's circular that described the Manzano Basin as "well-watered and suited to cultivation." The first claim was true in the way that a clock is right twice a day — the water came, violently, during the summer monsoons, and then it went. The second claim was not true at all. They had tried wheat, which burned. They had tried corn, which the wind shredded before it tasseled. They had tried, briefly and disastrously, sheep, which died of a disease the veterinarian in Belen could not identify and the neighbors attributed to the soil itself, as though the ground were hostile to the very idea of husbandry.

The house was ten miles from Bernardo, which was the nearest settlement large enough to have a post office and a store and an opinion about her. Bernardo was not a town in any sense that a person from the East would recognize — it was a crossroads with a depot, a scattering of adobes, a church that held services every third Sunday when the circuit priest remembered or the weather allowed, and a general store run by a family named Vigil who had been there so long they regarded the railroad as an intrusion. The country between Alma's house and Bernardo was open and mostly flat, a plain of creosote and chamisa interrupted by arroyos that ran dry ten months of the year and ran furious the other two. In October, she had watched a wall of brown water come down the arroyo nearest the house, carrying with it a fence post, a boot, and what looked like the remains of a chicken coop. The water passed. The arroyo dried. She went out and retrieved the fence post because she needed one.

## II

The first delegation came in November, when the weather turned and the road from Bernardo to the foothills became a subject of concern. Mrs. Padilla, who ran the post office from a counter in her kitchen, drove out in her husband's wagon with a flour sack of provisions — beans, lard, coffee, a tin of peaches — and a set of questions she had been formulating since August.

"You'll freeze," Mrs. Padilla said. She said it while standing in the doorway, looking at the stove. The stove was lit. The room was warm enough. But Mrs. Padilla was not speaking about the present temperature. She was speaking about a future she had already decided upon: Alma, alone, the fire gone out, the pipes frozen, the body discovered in spring when someone finally had reason to come up the road. Mrs. Padilla had a talent for grief that preceded its occasion. She could mourn a woman who was standing in front of her, alive and warm and not especially grateful for peaches.

"I won't freeze," Alma said.

"Phelan's not coming back."

"I know that."

"Then what are you doing here?"

The question had a simplicity that Alma respected. What was she doing here? She was keeping a house. She was keeping it in the way that one keeps a promise or keeps a watch — not maintaining it as a structure, although she did that too, but holding it, the way you hold a place in a line for someone who may or may not return. She could not have said this to Mrs. Padilla because Mrs. Padilla dealt in facts and a woman alone in a house ten miles from a settlement was a fact that pointed in only one direction.

"I have wood," Alma said. "I have water. The well is good."

"The well freezes."

"Then I'll melt snow."

Mrs. Padilla sat at the table — in the unused chair, the one across from the stove — and looked at Alma with an expression that was not unkind but was calculating. She was adding things up. The provisions in the sack, the distance from town, the weight of a woman's stubbornness against the weight of winter in the Manzanos. She arrived at a number and the number did not favor Alma.

"The Vigils' boy could take you to the depot. There's a room above the store. It's nothing fancy but it's warm and you'd be among people."

"I'm among the house," Alma said, and the sentence came out wrong, or came out right in a way she had not intended. She was among the house. She was in the company of its rooms, its sounds, its particular way of settling at night when the wood contracted and the walls made small sharp noises like an old woman cracking her knuckles. The house was company. It was insufficient company, yes, and at times it was antagonistic company — the draft through the bedroom window, the leak in the southwest corner that no amount of tar and canvas would permanently fix — but it was what she had, and it was hers in a way that nothing else was. Phelan's name was on the claim, but Phelan had left. The land office in Santa Fe might dispute her right to be here. The law might have an opinion. But the house itself did not dispute her. The house admitted her every night and held her weight and kept the wind on the other side of the walls, and in return she kept the fire and swept the floors and wiped the window glass with a rag soaked in vinegar so that the light came through clean.

Mrs. Padilla left the provisions and drove back to Bernardo. She told the Vigils. The Vigils told the circuit priest when he came through the following Sunday. The priest told the deputy who worked out of Socorro, forty miles south, when the deputy came through on his monthly round.

## III

The deputy's name was Amado Salazar, and he arrived in December on a horse that was unhappy about the climb. He was a young man, twenty-six, appointed by the county sheriff to handle the territory south of Belen and north of San Antonio, a stretch of country so large and so thinly settled that his primary duties were resolving cattle disputes and looking for people who did not want to be found. He had not previously been asked to look for a person who wanted very much to be exactly where she was.

He dismounted in the yard and stood for a moment, taking in the house. It was a modest frame structure — not adobe, which would have been more sensible, but wood, built by a man from Ohio who understood houses in terms of lumber and had not accounted for the way the dry air would split boards and shrink joints until the walls were gapped like an old fence. Alma had stuffed the gaps with rags and newspaper and, in one place, a length of rabbit fur nailed to the frame with tacks she had pulled from a calendar. The effect was eccentric. It was also, Salazar could see, effective. The house was holding.

Alma came to the door before he knocked. She had seen his dust from the window — you could see dust for miles in this country, and she had learned to read the size and speed of a dust column the way a sailor reads weather. One horse, one rider, no wagon. Not a threat and not a delivery. An errand.

"Mrs. Goss."

"Deputy."

"I'm told your husband has left the claim."

"He left me. I don't know that he left the claim. You'd have to ask the land office whether a man can leave a claim by leaving his wife in it."

Salazar took off his hat. He held it in his hands the way men do when they are thinking and want something to hold while they think. The hat was black and sweat-stained and had a shape that suggested it had been sat on more than once.

"The concern is that you're alone up here."

"The concern is whose?"

"Mrs. Padilla's. The Vigils'. Father Dominguez mentioned it. And frankly, ma'am, I share the concern. This isn't country for a woman alone."

"What country is?"

The question stopped him. He looked at the house, at the yard with its wire clothesline and its stack of juniper wood and its chicken coop with the three surviving hens who had outlasted the coyotes and the hawks and one another's tendency toward cannibalism. He looked at the mountains behind the house, which were not large mountains but were large enough to hold snow from November to March and to send their melt down through the arroyos in April with enough force to rearrange the landscape. He looked at Alma Goss, who was forty-one years old and had the hands of a woman who split her own wood and the posture of a woman who did not explain herself.

"I can't make you leave," he said.

"No."

"But I can tell you it's not safe."

"I know what's safe and what isn't."

He was quiet for a moment. A quail called from somewhere in the chamisa — a sharp, rising note that sounded like a question asked in a language neither of them spoke. Salazar looked at the mountains and then back at Alma, and she could see him trying to fit her into a category he understood. He had categories for drunks and for trespassers and for men who beat their horses. He did not have a category for a woman who had been left and had decided not to notice.

"And you'll stay."

"Yes."

He put his hat back on. He wrote something in a notebook he carried in his shirt pocket — she could see the pencil moving but not the words, and she wondered what category she occupied in his records. A complaint? A welfare check? A problem deferred? He mounted his horse and rode south without looking back, and the dust he raised hung in the air for a long time after he was gone, a column of particulate that caught the late-afternoon light and turned briefly golden before settling back into the general brown of the ground.

## IV

The winter was what winter is in the Manzano foothills: a negotiation between the person and the cold, conducted daily, won temporarily, never resolved. Alma woke each morning to a house that had cooled overnight — the stove banked but not out, the embers still red under the ash if she had done it right, and she usually did it right because she had learned from eleven months of doing it wrong. She raked the ash and fed the embers and built the fire up until the stovetop was hot enough to boil water, and then she boiled water and drank it and boiled more water to wash her face and boiled more water to fill the basin she used to wash whatever needed washing, which was always something.

The well did freeze, as Mrs. Padilla had predicted. It froze in January on a night when the temperature dropped below zero and the stars were so bright they seemed aggressive, as though the sky were making a point about clarity at the expense of warmth. Alma discovered the frozen well at dawn and stood beside it for several minutes, looking down into the dark shaft where the bucket rope hung slack and the water sat sealed beneath a plate of ice. She could hear the ice. It made small sounds — ticking, adjusting, settling into its new solidity. She went back inside and put on her coat and took the ax and went back out and broke the ice. It took forty minutes. Her shoulders ached for three days afterward. The well froze again the following week and she broke it again. This became the rhythm of January: freeze, break, draw, carry, heat. She sometimes thought about Phelan's letter, the second paragraph, the suggestion that she sell the claim. Sell it to whom? The land was not valuable. The house was not valuable. The well was arguably valuable when it was not frozen. What was valuable was the act of being here, and that was not a thing you could sell.

She talked to the house. Not in any way she would have admitted to Mrs. Padilla or the deputy — not out loud, not in full sentences, not as though she expected an answer. But she addressed it. She said *come on* when the stove draft was sluggish. She said *hold* when the wind hit the southwest wall and the whole structure shuddered. She said *good* when the lamp glass warmed under her hands and the light steadied. These were not conversations. They were acknowledgments. The house was there and she was there and the acknowledgment of mutual presence was the minimum social contract of her days.

In February the southwest corner leaked again. Rain ran down the interior wall and pooled on the floor and the pool froze overnight into a shallow lens of ice that caught the morning light and threw it across the ceiling in a pattern so unexpectedly beautiful that Alma stood in the doorway of the room and watched it for five minutes before she thought about the damage. The water was in the wall. The wood would rot. The frost would work into the rot and the rot would spread and the wall would soften and eventually give way in some future season she might or might not be here to witness. She packed the crack with tar and canvas and a handful of oakum she had bought from the Vigils' store in the fall, when she had gone to Bernardo for supplies and endured Mrs. Padilla's questions and the Vigils' studied neutrality and the general assessment of the settlement, which was that she was a woman with a problem she refused to name.

But the light on the ceiling. She kept thinking about the light on the ceiling. The way it had moved — slowly, as the sun moved, tracking across the plaster in a shape that was not a rectangle and not a circle but something organic, something the house and the water and the sun had made together without intending to, the way weather makes things without intending to. It was the most beautiful thing she had seen in months and it was a symptom of failure. The leak was destroying the wall and the wall's destruction had, briefly, filled the room with light. She did not know what to do with this. She tarred the crack and the light did not come back.

## V

Enoch Britten owned the land adjacent to Alma's claim on the north side. He ran forty head of cattle on six hundred acres, which was not enough cattle and not enough acres, and he had the weathered, skeptical look of a man who knew this and did it anyway. He came by in March, when the snow was melting and the arroyos were starting to make noise.

"Your fence is down on the east side," he said. He said it from the yard. He did not come to the door.

"I know."

"My cattle are getting through."

"They're welcome to what's here. There's nothing for them."

"That's the problem, Mrs. Goss. There's nothing for them and they go through your land to the arroyo and then I have to go get them out of the arroyo and last week one broke a leg and I had to shoot it."

"I'm sorry about that."

"I'm not asking for sorry. I'm asking you to fix your fence."

She fixed the fence. It took two days. She used the post she had retrieved from the arroyo in October and three lengths of wire she had salvaged from a spool Phelan had left in the shed, the wire rusted but still functional, the rust working into her palms and leaving marks that looked like burns and took a week to heal. On the second day the wind came up and the wire sang in her hands — a high, thin sound that seemed to come from the metal itself, as though the fence were protesting being strung. She set the last staple and stood back and looked at the line of wire running north across the slope, catching the light in a series of bright points that diminished into the distance like a message in a language made entirely of punctuation.

She fixed the fence and Britten's cattle stayed on Britten's side and Britten did not come back to the yard, but she saw his dust sometimes, moving along the fence line, checking.

He was, she understood, the third form of the town's concern. Mrs. Padilla worried about her body — that it would fail, that it would be found. The deputy worried about his jurisdiction — that something would happen and he would be the one who hadn't prevented it. Britten worried about his cattle. All three concerns were legitimate. All three placed her at the center of a problem she had not asked to be, and all three required her to justify her presence on land she had done nothing to earn except stay on it.

There was a fourth perspective, which was Phelan's, and it arrived by letter in April. A single page, forwarded through the post office in Bernardo, in which he informed her that he was filing to void the claim. The land office required continuous occupation and improvement, and he had not been occupying or improving, and therefore the claim was forfeit unless she wished to file in her own name, which she could not do because women could not file homestead claims in the Territory of New Mexico. This was a fact of law that he presented without comment, the way he had presented his departure without apology — as a condition of the world, regrettable perhaps but not his to change.

Alma read the letter at the table, in the chair near the stove. She read it twice. The second reading was not for comprehension — she had understood everything the first time — but for the tone. She was listening for something in Phelan's sentences, some residue of the man who had carried boards up the hillside and nailed them into a house and slept beside her in that house for three years. She did not find it. The letter could have been written by any clerk in any office about any parcel of disputed ground. She folded it and put it in the drawer with the first letter, and she went outside and stood in the yard and looked at the house from the outside. It was not a beautiful house. It had never been a beautiful house. It was a box of wood set into a hillside, with a corrugated roof and a chimney that listed slightly east and windows that were too small for the walls they were set in, as though the builder had been afraid of what might look in. But it was there. It had survived the winter. She had survived the winter. The well was thawed. The stove was lit. The fence was fixed. The southwest wall was tarred and holding, for now, though she knew the rot was working, always working, behind the canvas and the oakum, in the dark space between the inner wall and the outer wall where she could not see and could not reach.

## VI

She did not file a claim. She did not leave. She occupied a legal twilight — the wife of a man who had voided his claim on land she lived on, in a house she maintained, in a territory that did not recognize her right to hold it. The land office in Santa Fe would eventually send someone. Or it would not. The Territory was large and the land office was small and there were more pressing matters than one woman in one wooden house in the Manzano foothills.

The spring was dry. The arroyos ran for two weeks in April, fed by snowmelt, and then stopped. The well held, but the water tasted different now — minerally, deeper, as though the drought had drawn it from some lower stratum where the rock was older and less willing to part with what it held. Alma could taste the depth each morning. She drank it anyway.

The garden she planted behind the house — beans, squash, a row of chiles she had grown from seeds given to her by a woman at the Vigils' store whose name she did not know and whose face she could not precisely remember — came up sparse and pale, as though the seeds had sensed the precariousness of the arrangement and were hedging their commitments. She watered from the well, carrying two buckets at a time, morning and evening, the path between the well and the garden becoming a packed track in the otherwise wild ground. She could measure her devotion to the garden by the depth of that track. Two inches in May. Three inches by June. The soil compacted under her feet into something that was almost a floor, and she thought again about the house — how the house was just this, the same impulse: the desire to make a small piece of ground answer to the shape of a human need.

Mrs. Padilla came again in June, without provisions this time, driving her own wagon. She had a different look. Not concern. Something cooler.

"The Vigils heard from the land office. They say Phelan voided. They say you have no standing."

"I know."

"Then you know they'll come eventually."

"Eventually isn't today."

Mrs. Padilla looked at the garden. She looked at the fence, repaired. She looked at the house, which had weathered another season and was still standing, still leaking in the southwest corner, still holding Alma Goss inside it each night like a hand holding a stone — not gently, not cruelly, but with the impersonal grip of a thing that does not know it is holding.

"You're a stubborn woman," Mrs. Padilla said.

"I'm a woman with a house."

"It's not your house."

"Whose is it?"

Mrs. Padilla did not have an answer for this. The claim was voided. The house belonged to the Territory, or to the land office, or to the next person who filed. But the house also belonged to the woman who had kept its fire lit through January, who had broken the ice in the well with an ax and carried water up the hill in two buckets and tarred the southwest crack and fixed the east fence and planted beans that were, despite everything, growing. Belonging was not a legal category. Belonging was a physical fact, written in the packed track between the well and the garden, in the wear on the left stove handle, in the impression Alma's body left each morning in the mattress on the window side of the bed.

Mrs. Padilla drove back to Bernardo. Alma went inside. She sat at the table in the chair near the stove and listened to the house, which was making its evening sounds: the wood adjusting to the cooling air, the stove ticking as the metal contracted, the window glass humming faintly in the frame as the wind came down off the mountains and pressed against the walls, testing them. The light was going. The long June light that filled the rooms all afternoon was pulling back, retreating toward the west, and the shadows were coming in from the east like water, slowly, filling the corners first, then the floor, then the walls, then everything.

She lit the lamp. The glass warmed under her hands. The light steadied.

The house said nothing back. The house never said anything back. But it held. And she stayed in it, in the company of its held silence, while outside the country opened in every direction, vast and indifferent and hers for as long as she could keep the fire and the water and the walls between her body and the night.
