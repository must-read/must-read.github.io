## Block Nine

We called it Block Nine because that was its number on the district map, the kind of name that doesn't try to be anything. Some of the kids in Block Twelve called their section Little Garden, and there was a group in Block Six who'd painted a mural of a sun on the perimeter wall and called themselves Sunrise Corridor, but we never named ours anything. Block Nine. It sat in the mouth like a fact. You could love it or not.

I loved it.

The block was eleven buildings arranged around a courtyard that had once been a parking lot. You could still see the painted lines under the patchy grass and packed dirt, the ghosts of parking spaces where Mrs. Adaeze grew tomatoes in plastic buckets and where the little kids played a game that was half soccer and half argument. The buildings were four stories, brown brick, identical from the outside except for what people had done to the balconies. Bisi's family hung laundry on theirs — always bright, always enormous, sheets and wrappers and school uniforms drying in the wind like a country's worth of flags. Yusuf's grandmother had plants on every surface, so many that her balcony sagged and his father had reinforced it with lumber taken from somewhere nobody discussed.

The perimeter wall ran along the north and east sides. The south and west opened onto the avenue, where the transit line ran, and where the Wellness Center stood, white and clean and air-conditioned, with its automatic doors that breathed open and shut all day. You could see the wall from almost anywhere in the courtyard if you looked up, but you learned not to look up the same way you learned not to stare at the sun. Not because anyone told you. Because your eyes found other things to settle on.

## Ogechi, Which Means God's Gift

My name is Ogechi Nwankwo, and on every form the district has ever sent, it says Ogech Nwanko. Two letters wrong, three if you count the missing i. My mother corrected it five times. She wrote letters. She went to the Wellness Center with my birth certificate and stood at the intake window and spelled it out: O-G-E-C-H-I. The woman behind the glass typed something and said it would be updated.

It was not updated.

At school — we called it school, though the sign outside said Youth Development Facility — my name on the roster was Ogech N. I answered to it. Everyone answered to whatever the roster said. Chiamaka became Chiamka. Oluwaseun became O. Seun. Nneka became Neka, which sounded like a different person entirely, like someone from a country that didn't exist.

My mother stopped going to the Wellness Center about the name. She stopped around the same time she stopped correcting people about our surname. It wasn't defeat — or if it was, she didn't show it that way. She just started using my name more at home. *Ogechi, come eat. Ogechi, your shirt is inside out. Ogechi, what are you writing in that notebook?*

She said it like she was polishing it. Keeping it in use so it wouldn't go dull.

## Bisi Does Hair

Bisi Adeyemi could do anything with hair. Cornrows, twists, bantu knots, thread wrapping so tight and even it looked machine-made, though it was just her fingers, quick and sure, moving through sections with the confidence of someone reading a book she'd memorized. She did hair on the bench by the courtyard most afternoons, and whoever sat between her knees became the center of a small gravity — other girls drifting over, leaning on the wall, trading the kind of talk that sounds like nothing and means everything.

"Hold your head still."

"I am holding it still."

"You're not. You're following my hands. Stop anticipating."

Bisi had a client list, unofficial, kept in her head. She knew who wanted their edges laid down for Wednesday assembly, who needed protective styles before the dry season, who was trying something new because of a boy or a mood or just the feeling of wanting to not look like yourself for a week. She charged in favors. A plate of her mother's chin-chin for a full set of medium twists. Help carrying water when the courtyard pump was down. First dibs on the good spot by the wall where the afternoon light came through warm and golden and you could sit in it like sitting in a hand.

She never talked about what came after. None of us did, exactly, but with Bisi the silence had a particular quality — like a drawer she'd closed and put something heavy on top of. One afternoon I asked her if she'd ever thought about doing hair outside the block, professionally, in a real salon. She was midway through parting my hair with a rattail comb and she didn't stop, but her fingers paused for a half-second, the way a song skips.

"I think about what I'm doing now," she said.

And she was right to. The comb moved on. The part was straight and clean.

## Yusuf Draws Maps

Yusuf Diallo kept a notebook full of maps. Not real maps — he'd never been anywhere to map — but invented ones, imagined geographies drawn with a mechanical pencil he guarded like currency. He mapped cities he'd read about in the school library's small, outdated collection. He mapped cities he'd heard about from Mrs. Adaeze, who had lived somewhere else before the district and who sometimes, in the evenings, described a place with a harbor and hills and a market that went on for kilometers. He mapped that place from her descriptions, adjusting it each time she added a detail, erasing the harbor when she mentioned it was actually a river mouth, moving the market south when she said you could see the water from the fish stalls.

He also drew maps of Block Nine. Our block, rendered with the precision of a surveyor — every building, every path worn into the courtyard dirt, the exact location of the pump, the bench, the mural Fatimah had painted on Building Three's east wall (a tree with birds that didn't look like any birds we'd seen, which was the point). He drew the perimeter wall as a thick black line along the north and east, and what was beyond it he left blank. Not white — blank. An absence that was different from emptiness, though I couldn't have told you how.

"What's past the wall?" I asked once, though we could see past the wall. You could see the tops of buildings, the transit line, the edge of something industrial — a facility whose purpose the block had settled into not wondering about, the way you settle into any discomfort if it's steady enough.

"You know what's past the wall," Yusuf said, and went back to shading the contour of our courtyard, paying close attention to the crack in the concrete where the grass came through.

## Chiamaka's Brother

Chiamaka Eze had a brother named Tobenna who had been Transferred three years before I started keeping this notebook. I knew Tobenna only from the way people didn't talk about him — the shape of his absence in conversations, the way Chiamaka's face would do something complicated and brief whenever someone mentioned older siblings.

She kept his things. Not all of them — the district had collected most of his belongings during the Transfer process, which was standard, which was policy, which was a sentence I just wrote as though policy explained anything. But Chiamaka had hidden a few things before the collection: a pair of sneakers he'd customized with fabric paint, the laces replaced with a cord of green and white that matched nothing and meant something only to him. A notebook with his handwriting in it, though what he'd written I never saw and never asked.

She wore the sneakers sometimes. They were too big for her. She stuffed the toes with newspaper and walked in them like someone walking in a story about someone else, and nobody mentioned that the sneakers were too big or that they'd belonged to Tobenna or that Tobenna had been gone for three years and counting.

"He's placed," she'd say, if you forced the subject. The word *placed* like a lid coming down on a pot. Final, functional, discouraging further questions about what was cooking inside.

Once, only once, she said something else. We were sitting on the bench in late afternoon, watching Bisi do hair, watching the little kids argue about whose turn it was, watching the light do what it always did. And Chiamaka said, "He used to sit right here."

That was all. She didn't say she missed him. She didn't say she was angry or scared or sad. She located him in a specific place — right here, this bench, this spot — and the locating was the feeling, and the feeling was enough, and we sat in it without trying to make it into anything else.

## Morning

Mornings on the block had a sound. Not one sound but a layering, a composition that started before light and thickened as the sun came over the perimeter wall and hit the courtyard grass.

The pump handle, first. Whoever was up earliest, filling containers for the day. The handle had a squeak on the upstroke that Yusuf's father kept meaning to oil and never did, and the squeak became so familiar that mornings without it felt wrong, felt like a clock with no second hand.

Then the birds — not many, and not the kind you'd hear outside the district, but a few that had found the courtyard's trees and declared them sufficient. They sang like they sang anywhere. The birds didn't read the district map.

Then voices. Mrs. Adaeze talking to her tomatoes, which she did without embarrassment, as though the tomatoes had asked her a question and it would be rude not to answer. Bisi's younger brother kicking a ball against the wall in the rhythm of someone who was trying to wake up his legs. Chiamaka's mother singing something from a song I never caught the whole of, just a phrase that repeated — *ndo, ndo, ndo* — which means *sorry* or *patience* or *endure*, depending on who you ask.

By seven the courtyard was full in the way a bowl fills with water — not suddenly, but with a steadiness that meant by the time you noticed, it was already there. Kids heading to the Youth Development Facility in uniforms that were clean if not crisp, adults heading to the Labor Assignment Center at the avenue's end, where the day's work was posted on a board and you went where the board said. My mother always checked the board at six-thirty, though the board never changed her assignment, and I think she checked it the way Bisi's father oiled the pump handle, which is to say never quite, which is to say the checking was the routine, and the routine was the life, and the life was what we had.

## Nneka and the Library

Nneka Obi read books the way some people eat — not for pleasure exactly, but out of a need so basic that the question of whether she enjoyed it never came up. She read everything in the Youth Development Facility library, which wasn't large, and then she read it again, and then she started reading the reference books, the ones nobody touched, thick with tables and charts and language so dense it seemed designed to discourage reading.

She read the District Resident Handbook, all 240 pages. She read the Healthcare Protocol Manual. She read the Labor Assignment Guidelines and the Youth Development Outcomes Framework and a ring-bound document she found in the back of a supply closet titled *Resource Allocation Model: Managed Districts, Year 12 Onward*.

None of us asked what she found in those documents, and she didn't volunteer it. She kept reading. She kept sitting in the library's one comfortable chair, the one by the window that got the good light, and she kept turning pages with the steady rhythm of someone walking a familiar route, and if the route led somewhere she didn't describe, we didn't push.

I remember once she looked up from whatever she was reading and stared at me for a long moment. I was sitting across the table, working on the assignment they'd given us about community nutrition planning. Her face had an expression I've gone back to many times since. Not fear. Not anger. Something closer to what your face does when you've stepped in cold water and you're waiting for your body to decide how to feel about it.

"Do you want to talk about it?" I said, because it seemed like the thing to say.

"No," she said. And then: "Not yet." And then she looked back down at the page and I looked back at community nutrition and the afternoon light moved across the table between us and neither of us said anything more.

## Love, or Something Near It

Fatimah Bello was in love with Yusuf, and Yusuf was in love with his maps, and I was in love with watching both of them pretend these things weren't true.

Fatimah was the one who'd painted the mural on Building Three — the tree with impossible birds. She painted everything. She painted on walls with house paint she mixed herself, on scraps of cardboard, on the backs of used assignment sheets from school. She painted in colors that shouldn't have worked together — an orange that was almost red next to a blue that was almost green — and somehow they did work, they held, the way a chord holds even when you think the notes should clash.

She found reasons to be wherever Yusuf was. She'd bring a sketchbook to the courtyard when he was drawing maps and sit close enough to talk but not so close that it was obvious, which made it more obvious, which everyone knew except Yusuf, who was mapping the drainage pattern of the courtyard and couldn't be expected to notice human weather when he was tracking actual water flow.

"He's an idiot," Bisi said, doing Fatimah's hair one afternoon. It was said with love. Most things on the block were said with love, even the cruel ones.

"He's focused," Fatimah said.

"Focused on drains."

"Focused is focused."

It went on like this for weeks, the courtship that was not a courtship, the looking and not-looking, until one evening during the long slow dusk that was the block's best hour — when the light hit the buildings at the angle that made even the brown brick look warm, and the courtyard emptied of kids and filled with older people sitting on overturned buckets, talking about nothing — Fatimah showed Yusuf a painting she'd done of one of his maps. She'd taken Block Nine's layout and turned it into something else. The buildings became mountains. The courtyard became a valley. The perimeter wall became a river, and beyond the river were places he hadn't mapped yet, green and detailed, as though she'd been to them and come back.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he looked at her.

"You put things past the wall," he said.

"Someone had to."

I wasn't there for this. Bisi told me. Bisi told me the way she told me everything — midway through doing my hair, without looking up, as though the story were just another section to part and braid, something her hands could carry while her eyes stayed on the work.

## What We Knew

There were things we knew without being told, the way you know weather from the color of the sky.

We knew that no one from the block had ever gone past the perimeter wall and come back. This wasn't a rule anyone stated. It was an absence — a negative space in the shape of a person. Chiamaka's older brother had gone through the Transfer process three years ago, and when I asked her about him, she said "he's placed" and changed the subject, and the subject stayed changed the way a switched-off light stays off.

We knew that the Wellness Center tests, which happened twice a year and which we lined up for in our uniforms with our sleeves rolled up, measured something beyond our health. The nurses were kind. They gave us juice after and told us everything looked fine. But the machines they used were more complicated than health required, and the data went somewhere that wasn't the clinic's file room, because the file room had six cabinets and the data was enough for sixty.

We knew that the phrase "Youth Development Outcomes" meant something different from what the words said. We'd all heard the phrase. It was in the handbook, on the posters, in the speeches the district liaison gave at quarterly assemblies. *Your development outcomes are our highest priority.* It sounded like a promise. It had the shape of a promise. But a promise tells you what you're getting, and this one never did.

We knew these things the way you know your own heartbeat — constantly, unconsciously, without acknowledgment. The knowing was not something you carried. It was something you were.

## Fracture

One afternoon in September, during the quiet hour when school had ended and the labor assignments hadn't changed shifts yet and the block had the feeling of a held breath, Nneka came to the courtyard and sat on the bench next to me and said, very quietly, "They're going to move us in March."

I didn't ask who told her. I didn't ask where. I didn't ask what move meant, though the word had a specific weight on the block, a weight different from its ordinary meaning, the way *outcome* had a weight and *development* had a weight and *placement* had a weight — words that meant one thing in the dictionary and something else entirely in the district.

"All of us?" I said.

"Everyone from Cohort Year. That's what the framework calls us. Cohort Year."

I wanted to say something. I wanted to say: *I know. I've always known. We've all always known, haven't we?* But I didn't know. I'd suspected, the way you suspect morning will come when it's still dark — not with evidence, but with the body's sense of time. And suspecting is different from knowing. Suspecting lets you live. Knowing makes you sit on a bench in the courtyard on a September afternoon and feel the sun on your face and think: *this sun, this specific sun, the one that hits the courtyard at this angle at this hour, the one I have sat in a thousand times and never thought to count* — and the counting is what breaks you, the sudden urge to count the things you didn't think needed counting because you thought they'd always be there.

Nneka was looking at her hands. I was looking at the courtyard. Bisi was across the way doing someone's hair, and Fatimah was painting on the wall of Building Three, adding something to her mural — more birds, birds that didn't exist, birds that could fly anywhere — and Yusuf was at his usual spot with his notebook open, and Chiamaka's mother was singing *ndo, ndo, ndo* from an upstairs window, and the tomatoes were growing, and the pump handle was quiet for once, and the afternoon light was doing the thing it did, the thing where it turned the brown brick to gold, and I was so angry I couldn't move.

I was angry the way you are angry when you have loved something without realizing you were loving it and someone shows you the price tag. Not the cost — the cost I'd always felt. The price. The specific transaction. That all of this — the hair-braiding, the maps, the inside jokes about Mrs. Adaeze's tomatoes, the way Fatimah's orange met Yusuf's blue on the wall of Building Three — all of it was happening inside a container, and the container had an expiration date, and the people who'd built the container had never once considered that what grew inside it might be worth more than whatever they were growing it for.

I sat with the anger. It sat in me like a stone in a shoe, sharp and present and mine. Then Bisi called across the courtyard: "Ogechi, come here, I need your opinion on something," and the something was whether Fatimah's hair should be twisted left or right, and the answer mattered and didn't matter and I went.

## Mrs. Adaeze's Tomatoes

Mrs. Adaeze had been in the block longer than anyone else. She was old enough that the kids called her grandmother and she answered to it without correction, and she grew tomatoes in plastic buckets in the courtyard with a seriousness that suggested the tomatoes owed her something.

She talked about a place she'd lived before. Not every day. Not to everyone. But sometimes, when the evening was long and the courtyard was quiet and she'd found someone willing to sit, she'd describe a harbor. Hills behind it. A market that spread for kilometers. She never named the place. She described it from within the memory, circling, adding detail each time, so the picture accumulated like sediment, layer over layer, until you could almost see it.

"The fish stalls were closest to the water," she told me once. "You could hear the fishermen arguing about prices before you could see them. And behind the fish was the fabric, bolts of it, more colors than you'd think existed, piled on tables under tin roofs, and the women who sold them could hold a conversation, make change, and fold an ankara in the same breath."

"When did you leave?" I asked.

"When I came here," she said, which was not an answer. She went back to her tomatoes, pinching off a yellow leaf with the efficiency of someone who had learned that the things you tend are the things that live, and the things you don't, don't.

I never asked again. The question didn't belong to me. It belonged to whatever process had brought her to Block Nine, and that process — whatever it was, however it worked, whatever name it had in the documents Nneka read in the library's back corner — was not a thing you asked about. You lived beside it. You grew tomatoes beside it. You loved people inside it. And if you were lucky, you did these things long enough that the doing became its own kind of meaning, even if the meaning had a boundary, even if the boundary had a wall.

## Night

At night the block was different. The courtyard emptied and the buildings filled and the light in the windows made a pattern that was never the same twice, because people went to bed at different times, and the pattern was the sum of all their separate evenings — who was reading, who was arguing softly, who was watching the one channel the district provided, who was sitting in the dark because sitting in the dark was sometimes what you needed.

I lay on my bed and listened to the sounds. My mother washing dishes in the kitchen. The family upstairs walking — their footsteps had become so familiar I could tell who was moving by the rhythm: the father heavy and even, the mother quick, the baby a scatter of small impacts like rain on cardboard.

Outside, the perimeter wall. Beyond it, the facility whose purpose we'd settled into not knowing. Or not asking. The distinction matters less than you'd think.

I thought about Yusuf's maps. The way he drew the wall as a thick black line and left the other side blank. Not white. Blank. I used to think the blankness was fear — that he didn't draw what was there because he didn't want to know. But I think now it was something else. I think he drew the block with such care, such attention, such love for every crack and contour, because the block was the part that was his. The blank space belonged to the system. The detailed space belonged to us.

From somewhere in the building, the smell of someone's mother cooking — onions in palm oil, that particular smell that means someone is starting something that will take hours and will feed everyone who stops by, because that was how food worked on the block. You cooked what you had and you shared what you cooked and the sharing was not generosity, it was architecture. It was how the place held together.

## Quarterly Assembly

Four times a year the District Liaison came to the Youth Development Facility and spoke to us in the auditorium, which was a room with folding chairs and a podium and a banner that read YOUR DEVELOPMENT IS OUR PRIORITY in letters so large they must have been designed by someone who believed size was sincerity.

The Liaison was a woman named Dr. Holt. She wore gray and she smiled and she used words like *outcomes* and *benchmarks* and *transition readiness* and she meant them, I think, the way a machine means the sound it makes — not out of feeling but because that was the function, and the function was the point.

She never said what the outcomes were. She never said what we were transitioning toward, or what readiness looked like when you got there. She said these words the way weather reports say *partly cloudy* — as though the information were complete, as though partly and cloudy together added up to something you could hold.

We sat in the folding chairs and listened. Some of us took notes because we'd been told to. Some of us whispered to each other about other things — who had said what to whom, whether the courtyard pump was going to be fixed this week, whether Bisi was going to charge Fatimah for her next set of twists or whether love had changed the economy.

After the assembly we filed out and went back to the block and the block absorbed us the way it always did, warm and noisy and indifferent to whatever the Liaison had said, and we ate whatever someone's mother had cooked and did whatever homework the Facility had assigned and the evening came down over Block Nine like a hand closing gently over something small, and we were inside it, and it was ours, and we did not ask what it was for.

## February

The notice came on a Tuesday. Not a dramatic Tuesday — a Tuesday like every other Tuesday, cold enough for a jacket, bright enough to squint. The notices were posted at the Wellness Center and at the Youth Development Facility and on the courtyard bulletin board that usually held labor schedules and nutrition program updates. They were printed on district letterhead in the district's font, and they used the district's language, which was the language of process and procedure, of outcomes and transitions and development milestones.

Cohort Year would begin Transfer Preparation on March first.

Transfer Preparation was four weeks. After Transfer Preparation, Cohort would be Placed.

The notice did not say where. The notice did not say what Placement involved. The notice said that personal belongings should be limited to one standard bag per individual, and that the Wellness Center would conduct final assessments during the first week of March, and that any questions should be directed to the District Liaison during posted office hours.

I read the notice. Everyone read the notice. Then Bisi said, "Fatimah, come sit, I want to try something with your hair before it gets too cold," and Fatimah sat, and Bisi's fingers moved through the sections with the same quick certainty they always had, and Yusuf opened his notebook and started drawing, and I stood in the courtyard in the February light and watched my block being itself, completely and without apology, the way it had always been, the way it would be for twenty-seven more days, and I did not write this down because I didn't have to. I was already remembering.

## Ogechi, Again

My name means God's Gift. My mother chose it. Her mother chose hers.

On the final Transfer form, the one I signed on the morning of March first with a pen the District Liaison provided, it said Ogech Nwanko.

I didn't correct it.

There was nothing to correct. They had the name they wanted. I had the name I had. The two were not the same, and the difference between them was the size of a life — mine, specifically — which was larger than any form could hold, and which I carried with me when I walked through the perimeter gate into the transit line's morning noise, my one standard bag on my shoulder, the courtyard behind me, the blank space ahead.

The birds were singing. They sang like they sang anywhere.
