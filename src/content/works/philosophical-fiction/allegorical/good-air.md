> *The cut worm forgives the plow.*
> — William Blake, *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell*

I should explain the maps first, because they are the only thing I have left that proves what I knew, and knowing something is not the same as being right about it, though for most of my life I could not tell the difference.

The first map is dated the eleventh of March and shows the valley from above, as if drawn by a bird with an engineering degree. Kolna sits at the bottom like a coin in a bowl — the Skałka ridge to the north, the Grzbiet to the south, the river Tesa threading east through the gap where the rail line used to run before they closed the station in 2004. I drew the wind patterns in blue pencil. The prevailing current enters from the west, hits the Grzbiet, splits, curls back on itself. In winter, when the inversion layer settles, the air in the valley does not move for days. You can see it — a visible haze at the treeline, as if someone has drawn a border between the breathable world and the unbreathable one. I have marked this border on every map. I called it the Tidemark.

I was a hydraulic engineer for thirty-one years. I designed water-treatment systems for municipalities across the Opole and Lower Silesia voivodeships. When I retired, I came back to Kolna because my mother's house was empty and because I believed, in the way that people who have spent their careers in cities believe, that the countryside was a place where the systems worked without supervision. That the water ran clean. That the air was what air was supposed to be.

The bees died first. Not mine — I have never kept bees — but old Wójcik's, three hives on the slope above the cemetery. In October they were producing. By December the hives were empty. Not dead bees — no bodies on the landing boards, no corpses heaped at the base. The bees had simply left. Wójcik said it was the mobile phone tower they erected on the Grzbiet in September, and I wrote this down in my first notebook because at the time I still wrote down other people's theories alongside my own, before I had a theory that was strong enough to stand alone.

The birds came next. Starlings, mostly. You would find them on the road in the mornings, their bodies so light they could have been made of paper. The vet in Prudnik said it was avian flu, but the tests came back negative, and after the third round of negative tests he stopped returning calls. I collected fourteen starlings over the course of January and February, sealed them in plastic bags, and stored them in my mother's cellar, which was cold enough to preserve them. I labeled each bag with the date, location, and compass bearing from the nearest feature. If I had opened them and examined the organs, I might have noticed something. But I was not looking for what was inside the birds. I was looking for what had been around them.

I should say something about Kolna itself, because a place shapes the kind of wrongness that can grow there. Kolna has a population of eleven hundred, though this number is an estimate, because the young leave and the old do not report their deaths with the urgency the statistical office requires. There is a school that runs to grade eight, a church with a zinc roof that catches the light in ways that made my mother believe it was a sign, a post office that is also a tobacconist, and a cooperative store where the same woman has worked the register since I was a child. Her name is Jadwiga, and she knows what everyone buys, which means she knows what everyone eats, which means she knows more about the health of this town than any doctor. It was Jadwiga who first said that people were buying more antacids than usual. She told me this in November, before the bees left, before the starlings died, when the only symptom was a general unease — a sourness in the stomach, a difficulty sleeping, a feeling that the air in the house was too thick even with the windows open.

I did not listen to Jadwiga. Or rather, I listened and filed the information in the wrong cabinet. Antacids. Stomach complaints. I was thinking about respiratory exposure, about the lungs, about what enters the body through breath. The stomach did not interest me. The stomach was Baran's territory.

This is how errors compound. Not through ignorance but through specialization. I knew about air and water and the movement of fluids through systems. I knew about pressure differentials and dispersion models and the behavior of particulates in laminar versus turbulent flow.

---

In March, the first child was hospitalized. Natalia Kowal, age six, vomiting and listless, with a bluish tint to her lips that the doctor in Prudnik attributed to methemoglobinemia but could not explain. Two weeks later, her brother. Then the Pawlak boy. Then a baby whose name I never learned because the family left Kolna before I could introduce myself, packing their car at four in the morning like refugees, which in a sense they were.

I knocked on doors. I am not a sociable person — my husband, before he left, said that I had the interpersonal warmth of a pressure gauge — but I brought my first map and a notebook, and I asked each household: When did you first feel unwell? Was it the stomach or the head? Do you sleep with the windows open? Can you describe the smell?

The smell was the key. More than half the households reported an unusual odor — metallic, some said, or sulfurous, or like the inside of an old pipe. It came and went. It was worse at night. It was worse when the wind was from the west. It was worse in the houses closest to the valley floor.

I plotted these reports on the map. The pattern was unmistakable.

The smell followed the wind. The illness followed the smell. The wind was trapped by the valley. The valley was a bowl, and the air sat in it like water in a basin, and something in that air — something volatile, something that the instruments at the Prudnik monitoring station were not calibrated to detect — was making people sick.

> *The roaring of lions, the howling of wolves, the raging of the stormy sea, and the destructive sword are portions of eternity too great for the eye of man.*

I pinned Blake's words above my kitchen table, which had become my operations center. I am not a mystical person. I do not read horoscopes or consult the stars. But Blake understood something that modern environmental science has lost: that the natural world is not a collection of discrete measurable phenomena but a system of correspondences, each element connected to every other, each degradation rippling outward through chains of relation that no instrument has been built to trace. The birds died because the air was wrong. The bees left because they could smell what we could not. The children sickened because they breathed what we breathed but were too small to filter it.

---

Dr. Kamil Baran was the only physician in Kolna — not a general practitioner but a semi-retired dermatologist who had taken over the clinic when the previous doctor moved to Wroclaw. He was a careful, slow-speaking man with the particular authority of someone who has spent forty years looking closely at skin and has concluded that most problems are superficial.

"Daria," he said, when I brought him my third map — the one with the overlay showing wind direction, reported symptoms, and proximity to the valley floor — "I think it may be the water."

"It is not the water."

"The nitrate levels — "

"The nitrate levels in the municipal supply have been within acceptable limits for the past eight years. I checked. I have the reports." I had photocopied them from the voivodeship office and filed them in the green binder, which was the binder for data that contradicted my theory and therefore required careful scrutiny.

"Within acceptable limits" does not mean "zero," he said. "And the private wells — "

"The private wells are irrelevant. The Kowal family is on municipal water. The Pawlaks are on municipal water. If it were the water, the pattern would follow the pipe network, not the wind."

He looked at my map for a long time. I could see him trying to find the flaw, and I could see him failing, because the flaw was not in the map. The flaw was in the territory.

But I did not know this yet.

What I knew was that the town needed someone to be certain. Baran was not certain — he hedged, he qualified, he said things like "it could be" and "we should consider" and "one possibility among several." I did not hedge. I had spent my career designing systems where hedging killed people — where a miscalculated flow rate meant a flooded basement or a failed treatment plant. In engineering, you calculate and you commit. You do not present the client with three possible pipe diameters and ask which one they feel best about.

I calculated. I committed. I was wrong. But the commitment was real, and in a town where children were getting sick and no one could say why, commitment was the scarcest resource.

Old Wójcik came to see me in late March, after his second visit to the Prudnik hospital for stomach pains. He was seventy-four and had the build of a man who had spent fifty years lifting things — feed sacks, timber, bee boxes — and who was now discovering that the body remembers every weight. He sat in my kitchen and looked at the maps on the wall and said, "You are the only person in this town who is doing anything."

This was not entirely true. Dr. Baran was doing things. Father Józef was praying, which he considered doing something. The headmistress had sent a letter to the county. But Wójcik's statement had the force of a verdict because he was a man who did not speak often and therefore assumed that when he did speak, what he said was important, and because other people had never corrected this assumption, it had become true.

"I am trying," I said.

"The bees knew before we did," he said. "They always know first."

"Yes."

"And the air — you can smell it, on certain mornings. Something wrong."

"Yes."

"My grandfather called it *złe powietrze*." Bad air. "He said you could tell when the valley was sick because the air tasted of iron. He said his grandfather said the same thing."

I wrote this down. I wrote it in red pen, which was the pen I used for information that confirmed my theory, and the fact that I had separate pens for confirming and disconfirming evidence is something I prefer not to examine too closely, because it suggests a method I would have failed a student for.

---

The journalist's name was Ryszard Tomczyk, and he wrote for the regional supplement of *Gazeta Wyborcza* with the determined competence of a man who had once wanted to write novels and had settled for facts. He arrived in April, two days after the fourth child was hospitalized, and he found me because I was the person in Kolna who had data. Everyone else had fear or anger or resignation. I had spreadsheets.

"You believe the air is poisoned," he said. We were standing in my kitchen, and he was photographing my maps with his phone.

"I believe the air in this valley carries a contaminant that current monitoring infrastructure is not designed to detect. The geography of the valley creates an inversion layer that traps volatile compounds at ground level. These compounds are absorbed through respiration, and their effects are cumulative and disproportionately severe in children and the elderly."

"What compounds?"

"I don't know yet."

He lowered his phone. "You don't know?"

"I know the mechanism. I know the distribution pattern. I know the timing correlates with meteorological conditions. Identifying the specific compound is a matter of finding the right test, which requires the right laboratory, which requires funding, which requires attention, which requires" — I gestured at him — "journalism."

He wrote about me. The article appeared under the headline AIR OF SUSPICION IN SILESIAN VALLEY, which was the kind of headline that told you the editor had spent thirty seconds on it. But the content was fair. Tomczyk reproduced my maps. He quoted my Blake. He included Dr. Baran's objection about nitrates and my rebuttal, and to his credit he did not adjudicate between us. He presented two theories and let the reader decide, which is the most dangerous thing a journalist can do, because readers will always choose the theory with the better story, and my story was better than Baran's. Baran's theory was plumbing. Mine was poetry.

The calls started the next week. The voivodeship environmental office. A professor at the Wroclaw University of Environmental and Life Sciences. A woman from Greenpeace Poland who wanted to know if there was fracking in the area. There was not fracking in the area. There was an old coal seam, unworked since the 1960s, that I had marked on my second map as a possible source of sulfur dioxide off-gassing — the kind of slow geochemical process that, in a trapped valley with poor air circulation, could produce exactly the symptom profile I was seeing.

I told each caller what I knew. I was careful, precise, and — I see this now, though I could not see it then — persuasive in the specific way that people who have organized their delusion into a filing system are persuasive. My data was real. My maps were accurate. My correlations were genuine. The only thing wrong was the direction I was looking.

The Greenpeace woman, whose name was Marta, stayed for three days. She walked the valley with me. She photographed the Tidemark and said it was the most clearly documented case of thermal inversion trapping she had seen outside of industrial zones. I showed her the geological survey from 1978 — the coal seam running northeast to southwest at sixty to ninety meters depth, passing directly beneath the eastern quarter of Kolna, where three of the four sickest families lived. I explained the off-gassing mechanism. I showed her the stratified contamination model. She left with a folder of my data and a promise to escalate. She stopped answering my emails in June.

---

In May, two things happened. The first was that the county health authority dispatched a team to test the air quality in the valley. They set up monitors at six locations — I suggested the locations, based on my maps — and collected samples over a fourteen-day period. The results showed elevated particulate matter but nothing outside regulatory limits. No unusual volatile organic compounds. No sulfur dioxide. No anomalies.

"The monitors are too high," I told the team leader, a young woman named Agnieszka who wore her competence like a uniform. "You placed them at the standard height — two meters. But the contaminant pool in an inversion layer concentrates below one meter. The children are affected first because they are closer to the ground."

Agnieszka looked at me the way I must have looked at first-year students who challenged my lectures with confidence and no evidence. "We followed the standard monitoring protocol, Pani Holub."

"The standard protocol was designed for urban environments with vertical air mixing. This valley does not have vertical air mixing. This valley has stratified air. You are measuring the wrong layer."

She did not lower the monitors. She did not raise them either. She filed her report. The report said the air quality in Kolna was within acceptable parameters. I filed her report in the green binder.

The second thing that happened in May was that Father Józef gave a sermon about suffering. He did not mention Kolna specifically, but everyone understood. He spoke about the mystery of innocent suffering, about the trials God sends to test the faithful, about the importance of patience and prayer. Two women in the congregation walked out. Three others wept. Dr. Baran, who attended Mass out of habit rather than conviction, sat in the back row and studied his hands.

I was not in the church. I was on the ridge above the cemetery, at the site of Wójcik's empty hives, holding a glass jar with its lid removed, trying to smell what the bees had smelled. The air tasted of pine resin and cold stone and something underneath, something I could almost name, something that sat at the back of the throat like a word in a language I used to speak.

The hives were still intact — white wooden boxes with green roofs, arranged along the slope in a row that tracked the morning sun. Inside, the frames were heavy with uncapped honey that no bee had returned to finish. The wax was dark and fragrant. If you pressed your ear to the entrance hole you could hear nothing, which was itself a sound — the sound of a system that had stopped. I knew this sound. I had heard it in treatment plants when the aerators failed and the bacterial cultures died and the water went still and dark. It is the sound of a process that should be continuous discovering that it is not.

I took air samples at three heights — ground level, one meter, two meters — using mason jars that I sealed with paraffin. I would later send these to a laboratory in Wroclaw that a former colleague ran, a woman named Ewa who owed me a favor from a project in Nysa where I had signed off on her flow calculations despite reservations about her margin of error. Ewa's lab found nothing unusual in the samples. Particulate matter consistent with rural agricultural environments. No volatile organic compounds above background levels.

I filed her report in the green binder. The green binder was getting thick.

There is a particular loneliness in having evidence that no laboratory can confirm. You begin to suspect either the laboratories or yourself, and because suspecting yourself is intolerable — not out of vanity but because if you are wrong, then there is no one in Kolna who is looking — you suspect the laboratories. Their methods. Their calibrations. Their threshold levels. The entire apparatus of environmental monitoring, which was built to detect the problems of the last century and is blind to the problems of this one.

> *If the doors of perception were cleansed every thing would appear to man as it is, Infinite.*

The air in the valley carried something that lived below the threshold of detection, and I was the only person in Kolna who was trying to perceive it, and this is what I told myself every morning when I rose and marked the weather and checked my instruments and went out into the town to continue the work.

---

June. The Kowal girl had been released from the hospital but was not improving. She lay in her parents' living room on a sofa they had moved to the center of the room — away from the windows, because I had told them the air near the windows was worst — and she watched cartoons with the sound off while her mother stood in the doorway and looked at me with an expression I had seen on the faces of people in waiting rooms: not hope, exactly, but the memory of hope, the place where hope used to be.

"Have you tested the water?" Kowal's wife asked me.

"The water is not the problem."

"My sister says nitrates — "

"Your sister is not an engineer."

I heard myself say this, and I heard the cruelty in it, and I did not retract it because I believed, in that moment, that being right was more important than being kind.

The town organized. This is what towns do — not all towns, not immediately, but certain towns in certain valleys where the mountains close out the wider world and the people inside have no one to rely on but each other. They formed a committee. I was on the committee. Dr. Baran was on the committee. Wójcik was on the committee, although he attended only the first meeting and spent it talking about the phone tower. Father Józef sent his housekeeper as an observer, which was his way of being present without being accountable.

The committee's purpose was to coordinate the response. My purpose on the committee was to direct the response toward the air. Dr. Baran's purpose was to direct it toward the water. We had this argument at every meeting, politely, with data, and each time the committee voted to pursue both avenues, which meant pursuing neither with sufficient force.

Jadwiga, the woman from the cooperative store, was also on the committee. She did not speak at the first meeting, or the second. At the third meeting, in late June, she said: "I have been keeping a ledger. Since November. Every household that buys antacids, every household that buys rehydration salts, every household that has stopped buying fresh produce — because they are not eating, because they are too sick to cook. I can tell you which houses are affected. It is not the ones closest to the valley floor. It is the ones with the oldest plumbing."

I looked at her ledger. She had kept it in a school exercise book with a blue cover, and her handwriting was small and certain, and the data was organized by household and date, and it was the most useful piece of epidemiological evidence anyone in Kolna had produced, including me.

I did not use it. The ledger pointed toward the water. The ledger's geography followed pipe routes, not wind patterns. The ledger contradicted my maps, and I had spent four months on my maps, and the maps were beautiful, and the ledger was written in a school exercise book by a woman who sold cigarettes for a living.

This is the confession I am least able to make: that I dismissed Jadwiga's data because it was inconvenient, and that I justified the dismissal on grounds of methodology — she was not trained, her sample was biased, her variables were not controlled — when the actual reason was that her data threatened the architecture of my understanding, and I had built that architecture with too much care to let a shopkeeper's exercise book knock it down.

I drew a fourth map. This one showed the temporal distribution of cases — when each person fell ill, plotted against meteorological data. The correlation was clear. Illness clustered after periods of atmospheric inversion. The lag was three to five days, consistent with a cumulative respiratory exposure. I presented this at the July meeting with the satisfaction of someone laying down a winning hand.

"Or consistent with a waterborne pathogen with a three-to-five-day incubation period that happens to survive better in cold, still conditions," Dr. Baran said.

"That is a coincidence."

"Your entire theory is a coincidence, Daria. You have found a pattern. The pattern is real. But a pattern is not a mechanism."

"And your theory has a mechanism but no pattern."

He conceded this. I think he conceded it because he was tired. We were all tired. By July, eleven people in Kolna had been hospitalized, two had died — old Staszek, whose lungs were already damaged from forty years of smoking, and a woman named Henryka who was ninety-one and might have died anyway, though her daughter insisted the timing was not coincidental — and the voivodeship office had sent three more teams, tested three more times, and found nothing in the air.

Nothing in the air. I have written this sentence many times and each time it feels like a door closing. But doors that close also protect. Each negative result became a wall in the fortress of my certainty. If the standard tests found nothing, then the contaminant was non-standard. If the monitors detected nothing, the monitors were insufficient. If three rounds of testing showed clean air, then the contaminant existed below the detection threshold, in the interstice between what science could measure and what the body could feel.

In July I began sleeping badly. I would wake at three in the morning and go to the window and smell the air — that same metallic undertone, that same iron tang that Wójcik's grandfather had called *złe powietrze*. I would stand there in my nightgown and breathe deeply and try to isolate the note, the way a musician isolates a frequency in a chord, and I would think: I am the only person in this valley who is paying attention. I am the only one who has not given up.

This thought sustained me. There is a word in Polish — *zaślepienie* — that means something between blindness and infatuation. You are blinded not by darkness but by the intensity of what you are looking at. My maps were that intensity. Everything else was afterimage.

---

In August, the county health inspector — not the environmental team, not Agnieszka with her monitors, but a different office entirely, one I had not contacted because they dealt with water, and I was not interested in water — sent a technician to Kolna as part of a routine survey. Routine. He tested the municipal wells and the three private wells still in use, and he found nitrate levels four times the legal limit. He also found coliform bacteria and traces of agricultural herbicide that had been banned in the EU since 2011 but was still present in the soil from decades of use, leaching slowly through fractured limestone into the aquifer that fed the town.

The aquifer. The water. The ground.

I learned this not from the inspector but from Dr. Baran, who called me at seven in the morning with a voice that held no triumph, only exhaustion. "They found it, Daria. In the water. Nitrates, coliforms, and atrazine. The whole aquifer is compromised. It has been compromised for years."

I listened. I held the phone against my ear and looked at my maps on the kitchen wall — the wind patterns, the Tidemark, the inversion layers, the temporal correlations — and I felt my hand tighten on the receiver. There was a ringing in my left ear that I attributed to the phone and not to what he was saying.

Then the maps reasserted themselves. The patterns were too clean to be coincidental. The wind data was real. The correlation with atmospheric inversions was real. And as I stood in my kitchen, still holding the phone, I understood — I *understood* — that the water contamination was the vector, yes, but the distribution was governed by the air. The inversion layer trapped not only atmospheric pollutants but also the volatile organic compounds that off-gassed from the contaminated water as it surfaced in springs and seeps along the valley floor. The air carried the water's poison. The air was the medium. The mechanism ran through the ground, but the dispersal ran through the air.

I explained this to Dr. Baran. He was silent for a long time. I could hear, through the phone, the sound of his clinic — a door closing, someone coughing in the waiting room, the held silence of a building where people come to be told what is wrong with them.

"Daria," he said. "The children were drinking the water."

"The children were also breathing the air."

"The children were drinking the water and the water had poison in it and that is why they were sick."

"That is an oversimplification."

"It is the simplification that will save them."

I heard this and I heard it as an accusation, though I do not believe he meant it as one. Baran was not a cruel man. He was a pragmatist, which is a different kind of cruelty — the cruelty of the sufficient, of the answer that works even if it is not the whole answer. He wanted to stop the poisoning. I wanted to understand the mechanism. These are different ambitions, and in a crisis, I am still not certain which one was the right one to have.

---

The boil-water advisory went out in September. The voivodeship authorized an emergency connection to the Prudnik water system. The private wells were sealed. Within six weeks, the hospitalizations stopped. The Kowal girl improved. The committee dissolved. Father Józef gave a sermon about gratitude. Jadwiga told me, when I came into the cooperative store for tape to mount a new map, that antacid sales had returned to normal levels. She said this without accusation, without emphasis. She said it the way a shopkeeper reports inventory.

Dr. Baran came to my house in late September. He sat in my kitchen — in the same chair Wójcik had sat in months before — and looked at the maps that covered the walls, and he did not say what I expected him to say, which was some variation of *I was right and you were wrong.* He said nothing about right or wrong. He talked about the remediation schedule and the new pipe connection to Prudnik and the Kowal girl's blood work, and when he left he paused at the door and said, "You should take the maps down, Daria. Not because they are wrong. Because people will talk."

People were already talking. I knew this because Jadwiga had told me, in the way Jadwiga tells people things — not as gossip but as inventory, a factual accounting of what was circulating. They said I had delayed the investigation. They said the voivodeship spent resources on air testing that should have gone to water testing. They said the Kowal girl was sick for two extra months because of my maps.

I do not know if this is true. The timeline does not support it cleanly — the water inspector came on a routine survey, not redirected from the air teams — but timelines are not how people think. People think in stories, and the story of Kolna had a villain's role that needed filling, and I had auditioned for it with my Blake and my spreadsheets.

I did not take the maps down. I was working. I continued to monitor the air quality with my personal instruments — a particulate counter I had ordered from Germany, an anemometer, a barometric pressure logger. I continued to draw maps. I wrote a letter to the voivodeship environmental office arguing that the remediation plan failed to account for atmospheric dispersal of volatile contaminants from the compromised aquifer, and that monitoring the air in the valley should be a condition of any remediation contract. I quoted Blake. I cited my data.

The letter was not answered. The remediation proceeded without air monitoring. The town returned to its ordinary rhythms — the school reopened, the market resumed, the bells rang on Sunday morning — and I continued to rise, to record, to map, to walk the valley with my instruments and my notebooks, because the air in Kolna was still wrong. I could smell it. I had always been able to smell it. The difference between me and the rest of the town was that I had not stopped smelling it when they told me the water was fixed.

Tomczyk came back in October. He was writing a follow-up piece about the remediation — a success story, he said, which was the kind of story that editors liked because it had a beginning, a middle, and an end, and the end was clean.

"It is not clean," I told him. "The aquifer is one source. The air is another. They have addressed the aquifer and declared victory, but the air — "

"The air tests were normal, Pani Holub. Three rounds of testing."

"The tests measure the wrong things at the wrong height in the wrong season."

He wrote this down. I think he wrote it down out of politeness, or out of the journalist's instinct to record everything and decide later what matters. I do not think he intended to use it. I do not think he believed me. But he wrote it down, and there is a kind of faith in that — the faith of the scribe, who believes that the record itself has value even when the record is wrong.

His article appeared in November, and it mentioned me in the third paragraph as "a retired engineer who first drew attention to the crisis" and in the eleventh paragraph as "Holub, whose air-quality theory was not supported by subsequent testing." Both sentences were accurate. Both sentences described the same person. I clipped the article and pinned it to the wall between the second and third maps, where it covered a section of wind-pattern analysis that I had drawn in April and that was, I still maintain, the most precise wind mapping ever done of the Kolna basin.

Baran did not call about the article. I do not know if he read it.

---

> *In seed time learn, in harvest teach, in winter enjoy.*

It is February now. The valley is white and the air is still and the Tidemark is visible again, that faint line of haze between the breathable and the unbreathable. The remediation is ongoing — they are flushing the aquifer, replacing pipes, testing quarterly. The nitrate levels are dropping. The children are well. The bees have not returned to Wójcik's hives, but Wójcik is not looking for them. He has moved to Prudnik to live with his daughter.

My maps cover three walls of the kitchen now. They overlap and annotate each other, a palimpsest of wind and illness and time. Visitors — and there are fewer visitors now — look at them the way people look at the illuminated manuscripts in a museum: with admiration for the craft and uncertainty about the content. What is she charting? What does she see? The answer is: everything. Everything the instruments cannot detect and the committees will not investigate and the journalists will not publish. Everything that lives in the air between the Tidemark and the valley floor, in the breath between one season and the next.

The water was contaminated. This is a fact. The air carries the products of that contamination. This is also a fact. The question is not which fact is the cause — the question is which fact the town was willing to see.

Jadwiga asked me last week, when I went in for paraffin and masking tape, whether I was still making maps. I said yes. She nodded, not in agreement but in the way people nod when they have decided to stop arguing. "The children are well," she said. "The water is clean."

I did not tell her about the air. I have stopped telling people about the air. Not because I have stopped believing — I believe more firmly now than I did in March, because the evidence against my theory has forced me to refine it, and a refined theory is a stronger theory, the way a bone that has broken and healed is stronger at the fracture line — but because I have learned that certainty, when it is not shared, becomes a form of solitude, and I have enough solitude already, in this house, in this valley, with my maps and my instruments and my Blake.

I continue to map. I continue to measure. I walk the valley in the early morning when the air is heaviest and the Tidemark is lowest and the world smells of sulfur and stone and something I have spent a year trying to name. Blake says the cut worm forgives the plow. I have pinned this above the kitchen table and I read it every morning and I do not know what it means. I used to think it meant that suffering is part of a larger design. Now I am not sure it means anything at all, and I read it anyway, the way I read my maps — not because they have given me an answer, but because the reading itself is the only work I know how to do.

Last week I ordered a new particulate counter from a laboratory supply company in Dresden. It measures at ten-centimeter intervals from the ground. It cost more than my pension for three months. It arrives on Thursday.
