I have been given an envelope. The envelope is white, or off-white, or the white it was when it was new has been modified by my hands, which are not clean — they have not been clean since Tuesday, or the day I am calling Tuesday, the day the hot water stopped, which may not have been a Tuesday at all, but I have assigned it Tuesday because the week needs structure and I am the only one providing it.

The envelope contains something. I have not opened it. I was told not to open it, which is the same as being told nothing because I would not have opened it anyway — I am not the kind of person who opens things. I am the kind of person who carries things from one place to another, and this is not a vocation but a description, the way "heavy" is not a vocation for a stone.

The address is written on the front of the envelope. The handwriting is not mine. The address is eleven blocks south and two blocks east, or eleven south and three east — the three was written over a two, or the two was written over a three, and the ink has merged into a numeral that could be either or neither, a digit that has refused to commit. I will try two first. If two is wrong I will try three. If three is wrong I will stand on the corner and wait for the street to tell me which it is, though streets are not in the habit of telling.

I left the apartment at nine. I know this because the clock in the kitchen said nine. The clock in the kitchen has been wrong before — specifically, it was wrong for the entire month of November, running seven minutes fast, which I discovered when I arrived early to an appointment that did not exist, or existed but not for me, or existed for a me that was seven minutes ahead of the me I am, and I have not trusted the clock since, but I have also not adjusted it, because an unreliable clock is still a clock, and adjusting it would imply I had access to the correct time, which I do not.

Nine o'clock. I walked south. The first three blocks were familiar — the dry cleaner with the neon sign that says OPEN in blue and CLOSED in red and currently says both, the pharmacy, the bakery that smells of anise at seven and of nothing by noon, as though the smell has a schedule. My left knee was functional. I note this because it is not always functional, and the functionality of the left knee is the variable on which the journey's duration depends. On good days the knee permits a stride of approximately twenty-eight inches. On bad days the knee permits something closer to a shuffle, a gait that a physical therapist once called "compensatory" and that I call walking, because it is walking, because what else would you call the forward movement of a body over ground using legs, however abbreviated.

Today the knee was good. Twenty-eight inches. I counted. I stopped counting at block four because the counting had begun to replace the walking, the mind so occupied with arithmetic that the legs had become secondary, mere instruments of the count, and I could feel them rebelling, the left knee in particular tightening as though to say: I am not a unit of measurement.

---

At block five the city changed. I do not mean this metaphorically. The street I was walking — Kossuth, or Kosciuszko, or a street whose name began with K and contained more consonants than any mouth should be asked to negotiate — gave way to a street I had not seen before. Not a side street, not an alley, but a full boulevard, wide, tree-lined, with a median planted in something purple that I could not identify, a flower or a weed or a decorative cabbage, the kind of planting that municipal governments commission when they want to suggest vitality without committing to maintenance.

I stopped. I consulted the envelope. Eleven blocks south. I had walked five blocks south. I should have six remaining. But the boulevard was not south. The boulevard ran at an angle — roughly south-southeast, or south-southwest, depending on which direction I was facing, and I was facing a direction that I will call south for the purposes of continuity though I was no longer certain that south was where it had been when I started.

This is the problem with walking: the body has opinions about direction that the compass does not share. My right hip, which has been compensating for my left knee for approximately eleven years, pulls me eastward. Not dramatically — a few degrees per block, an error so small that on a short walk it is invisible but on a long walk accumulates into a curve, a slow drift from the intended line, the way a ship in a current arrives at a port twenty miles from the one it aimed for and the captain says: the bearing was correct; the ocean disagreed.

I corrected. I turned left, which I believed to be south, and walked two blocks, and found myself on a street I recognized — not from today but from a previous day, a day I cannot place, a day on which I had walked this same block in the opposite direction, or a block that resembled it so closely that the resemblance functioned as identity, the same yellow apartment building with the iron balconies, the same newsstand with its awning furled against rain that was not falling.

Fragment: On bearing.

A bearing is a direction measured clockwise from north. North is zero. East is ninety. South is one hundred and eighty. West is two hundred and seventy. These numbers are absolute — they do not care where you are standing or which way your body prefers to lean. A bearing of one hundred and eighty degrees is south from any point on the earth's surface, including the South Pole, where south ceases to have meaning, where every direction is north, and the compass needle spins freely, pointing at everything and therefore nothing, which is the condition I am approaching on foot.

A deviation is the difference between the bearing you intend and the bearing you achieve. In navigation, deviation is caused by magnetic interference — iron in the hull, electrical systems, the slow drift of the magnetic poles themselves. In walking, deviation is caused by the body. A shorter left leg. A hip that has compensated. A knee that favors extension over flexion and therefore pulls the stride into a curve. Over sufficient distance, deviation will bring you back to where you started. This is not a metaphor. A person walking in a straight line through featureless terrain, with no landmarks and no compass, will walk in a circle. The circle's diameter is determined by the asymmetry of the body. My circle, I estimate, has a diameter of approximately three kilometers, which means that if I walk for nine and a half kilometers in what I believe to be a straight line, I will be standing where I began, facing the direction I started from, with no memory of having turned.

---

I sat down. There was a bench, or a ledge, or a low wall belonging to a building whose function I could not determine — it had the look of a place where something official happened, or had happened, or was expected to happen eventually: a government annex, an archive, a bureau of something that required a building but not a sign. I sat on the wall and took the envelope from my coat and held it in both hands and looked at the address.

The address had not changed. The two-or-three was still ambiguous. The street name was legible. The recipient's name was legible. Everything about the envelope was legible except the distance between me and its destination, which was not written on the envelope because distance is not a feature of addresses — an address is a place, not a journey, and the envelope's job is to know where it is going, not how far it has to go to get there.

I had been walking for two hours. Two hours for six remaining blocks. This was not alarming. This was, if I am honest, expected. I have never been efficient. Efficiency implies a ratio between effort and result that flatters the result, and my results have never warranted flattery. I once spent four hours looking for a post office that turned out to be next door to my apartment. I had walked past it eight times. The post office was there, on the ground floor, with a sign and a flag and a slot for letters, and I had walked past it because I was looking for a post office and the post office I was looking at did not match the post office in my head, which was larger, or brighter, or located on a street that sloped upward, and the real post office was on a flat street and was small and dim and was, in every way, a post office, but not the one I was looking for, which was a post office that did not exist.

---

The city, I should say, is not a large city. It is the kind of city that could be crossed on foot in an afternoon by a person whose knees cooperated and whose sense of direction had not been modified by eleven years of compensatory drift. The river divides it roughly in half — the old city to the north, the new city to the south, the bridge connecting them, the bridge I was not supposed to need because the address was on my side of the river, the north side, the old side, the side where the streets were narrow and named after patriots whose contributions I could not remember.

I was on the south side of the river.

I do not know when I crossed. There is a bridge and I must have crossed it, but I have no memory of crossing. The bridge is not a subtle structure — it is concrete and steel and wide enough for four lanes of traffic, and crossing it would have involved walking for approximately seven minutes with the river visible on both sides, the water brown and slow, carrying whatever rivers carry, which is everything that has fallen into them, which is more than you would think. I must have crossed it. The alternative — that the city rearranged itself around me, that the south bank migrated northward, that the river jumped — is less plausible than the hypothesis that I was not paying attention, which is the most plausible hypothesis about me at any given moment.

I turned around. I walked north, which I determined by the position of the sun, which was behind clouds, which meant I was determining north by the position of a light source I could not see behind a cover I could not penetrate, which is how all navigation works when you think about it — you are always orienting yourself relative to something that is not fully available to you. The North Star is a point of light four hundred light-years away. The magnetic pole has drifted eleven hundred kilometers since it was first measured. I was navigating by an accumulation of guesses, each one slightly wrong, and the accumulation was my direction, and the direction was north, probably.

I found the bridge. It was where it should be. The knee had tightened during the detour — twenty-five inches now, maybe twenty-four, the stride contracting like a sentence losing its subordinate clauses. I crossed the bridge. I paid attention this time. The water was brown. A barge was moving upriver, or the barge was still and the river was moving around it — from the bridge, without a fixed reference point, the two cases looked identical. The barge had a name painted on its hull. I could not read it from the bridge. I leaned over the railing and squinted and the name was in a script I did not recognize, or recognized but could not read, or could read but could not pronounce, which in the world of names is the same as not reading, because a name you cannot say is a name that has not been given to you.

Fragment: On Chopin's heart.

In 1849, in Paris, Frédéric Chopin died of tuberculosis, or of pericarditis, or of cystic fibrosis, or of one of several conditions that the medical knowledge of 1849 could not distinguish and that modern medicine can only retrospectively guess at, the body having been buried and therefore unavailable for the kind of investigation that would settle the question, except for the heart, which was not buried.

The heart was removed by Chopin's sister, Ludwika, at his request. It was preserved in a jar — cognac, reportedly, though the specific spirit has been debated. Ludwika carried the jar from Paris to Warsaw, hidden under her cloak, across borders that were not merely administrative but political, the territory between France and Russian-occupied Poland being a landscape of checkpoints and suspicion and the particular bureaucratic paranoia that attaches to the movement of objects whose significance is not immediately legible. A jar of liquid containing a muscle. What is it? It is a heart. Whose? A composer's. Why are you carrying it? Because he asked me to bring it home. Where is home? Warsaw. Why is Warsaw home? Because that is where the heart wants to be.

The heart arrived. It was sealed in a pillar of the Church of the Holy Cross. During the Warsaw Uprising of 1944, the Germans removed it. After the war, it was returned. In 2014, Polish scientists were permitted to examine the jar, briefly, without opening it. They concluded the heart was well-preserved. They photographed it. The photographs show an organ the color of old wood, suspended in amber liquid, smaller than you would expect, because expectation adds size to the things we value, and the real thing is always smaller than the legend, always denser, always less photogenic, always more itself than we are prepared for.

The heart has traveled more than most hearts. It has crossed the borders of at least three political entities that no longer exist. It has been carried by hand, by horse, by rail. It has been hidden, displayed, hidden again. It has accumulated a provenance — a chain of custody — longer than many living people's biographies. And through all of this it has remained a muscle. A pump. An organ whose only function was to move blood in a circle, to push and pull, to contract and release, to maintain a rhythm that lasted thirty-nine years and then stopped, and whose stopping was the occasion for all this motion, all this displacement — the heart going everywhere the body could not, carried by hands that were not its own, toward an address it could not verify.

---

I was on the north side again. The streets were narrow. The patriots on the signs stared past me with the expressions of men who had more important things to do than name streets. I counted blocks. I had counted six from the apartment. The address was eleven south. I should have five remaining. But counting blocks requires knowing which direction you are counting in, and my counting had been interrupted by the detour to the south side and back, and I was no longer certain which block I was on. The blocks had the quality of blocks — they were rectangular, or approximately rectangular, with buildings on both sides and pavement in the middle and a certain distance between one intersection and the next — but they did not have the quality of *these* blocks, the blocks between my apartment and the address, the blocks I needed.

My knee was worse. The left one. The good one, which was good this morning, which was functional at twenty-eight inches, which was now functional at something closer to twenty-two inches, which meant the stride had shortened, which meant the deviation had changed, which meant the circle I was walking, if I was walking a circle, had tightened, and the address, if it was on the circle, was either closer or further away depending on where on the circumference I currently stood.

I sat down again. This time on a step, the front step of a building that had the look of an apartment building, five stories, stone, with a wooden door and a row of buzzers beside it. I read the buzzers. The names were arranged vertically: Kowalski, Wiśniewska, Jabłoński, Nowak, and one buzzer with no name, just a strip of blank paper behind a plastic window. I pressed the blank buzzer. Nothing happened. I pressed it again. A sound came from the speaker — not a voice, not a buzz, but a click, the kind of click that a lock makes when it is released, and the door opened slightly, not because anyone had opened it but because the latch had been released and the door, which was heavy but not balanced, swung inward under its own weight, offering me a hallway I had not asked for.

I did not go in. I am not the kind of person who enters buildings without purpose, and my purpose was the envelope and the address, and the address was not this building, or I did not think it was, though I had not checked, and now I checked, and the number on the building was — I looked — there was no number. The building had no number. It had buzzers and names and a door and five stories but it had no number, which meant it was either a building that existed outside the numbering system or a building whose number had been removed, or a building whose number I could not see, which in the taxonomy of buildings amounts to the same thing: a building that could be any address, including the one I needed.

I stood up. I walked south. My knee registered the decision before my mind did — the knee tightened, as though bracing for a distance it had already calculated and found excessive. I walked. The envelope was in my left hand. My right hand was in my pocket, where it found, as it always finds, the smooth, flat stone I have been carrying for years, a stone from no particular place, a river stone or a road stone or a stone from somewhere that was once a river and is now a road, the geological history of the stone being no more legible to me than the two-or-three on the envelope, but the stone being, in my pocket, the one object whose location I am always certain of, the fixed point in the coordinate system of my coat.

I rubbed the stone. I walked.

Fragment: On the route of the Neva.

A river is a deviation. It begins with rain, which falls where gravity directs it, and the gravity is local, and the local gravity is shaped by the rock, and the rock is shaped by events that occurred before the river existed, and so the river's course is determined by the history of something that predates it by millions of years, and the river follows this history without knowing it, the way I follow the deviation in my stride without choosing it.

The Neva, in Saint Petersburg, is four hundred and seventy-four meters wide at its widest point. It runs for seventy-four kilometers from Lake Ladoga to the Gulf of Finland. It is one of the shortest major rivers in Europe. Peter the Great built a city on its delta because the delta was the only access to the Baltic, and the Baltic was where Europe happened, and Peter wanted to be where Europe happened, and so the city was built on swamp and bones and timber driven into mud, and the river ran through it the way a bearing runs through a life: not because the river chose the city but because the city chose the river and then the river had no choice, the banks narrowed by granite, the current regulated by bridges, the water that had been finding its own way for ten thousand years suddenly required to find the city's way.

I have never been to Saint Petersburg. I have never been to Lake Ladoga. I have walked across a bridge today and that is as close to a river as I have come in months. The river under the bridge was brown and carried a barge whose name I could not read, and that is all I know about rivers: they are brown, they carry things, and they arrive somewhere without having intended to.

---

A woman was sitting on a fire hydrant. Not on the top of it — she had fashioned a kind of perch against the side, one hip braced against the metal cap, her legs extended onto the pavement, and she was eating an apple. The apple was green. She ate it with a concentration that suggested the apple was the only thing that had gone right for her today, or the only thing that had gone right for her in general, and she was determined to honor it with her full attention.

"Excuse me," I said. "Is this Sobieski Street?"

She looked at me. She looked at the envelope. She looked at my shoes, which I have not described and will not describe except to say that the left shoe is more worn than the right, which is a consequence of the knee, the knee wearing the shoe the way the shoe wears the pavement, everything wearing everything else in a cascade of friction that begins at the hip and ends at the ground.

"This is Zielona," she said.

"I need Sobieski."

"Sobieski is south." She gestured with the apple. The gesture was vague. South could have been any direction the apple pointed, and the apple was pointing at a building across the street, which was east or north or wherever buildings are when they are across the street from where you are standing.

"How far south?"

"That depends on where you are starting from."

"I am starting from here."

"Then it depends on which way you walk."

This seemed circular, and I said so, and she took another bite of the apple and chewed it slowly and said, "There used to be a shortcut through the courtyard behind the pharmacy, but they put a fence up. Or a gate. Something with a lock." She thought about this. "It might have been a different pharmacy."

I thanked her. She was still eating the apple when I left. I walked in the direction the apple had indicated, which I was calling south because the alternative was to have no direction at all, and no direction is worse than a wrong direction, because a wrong direction is still a direction and can be corrected, whereas no direction is a condition, and a condition cannot be walked out of.

---

By the time I found the street — the street on the envelope, the street I had been walking toward for what the light suggested was most of the day, the shadows having rotated from west to east, which meant the sun had crossed the sky, which meant time had passed in the direction time passes, which is the only direction anyone agrees on — my knee had shortened my stride to nineteen inches and the envelope had softened in my grip, the paper absorbing moisture from my palm, the address blurring at the edges, the two-or-three now indistinguishable from a five or a seven or a mark that might not be a number at all but a water stain that preceded the ink and that the ink had merely contoured.

The street was short. Seven buildings on one side, eight on the other. I walked the odd side. Numbers: 1, 3, 5, 7, 9. I walked the even side. Numbers: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. The address on the envelope was either 12 or 13, and the street had neither. The street ended at a wall — a brick wall, not old, not interesting, a wall that existed for the purpose of ending things, a wall that said: this is where the street stops, this is where the numbers stop, this is where you stop.

I stood at the wall. I held the envelope. The envelope was damp and the address was a smear and the person whose name was on the front — I could still read the name, though just barely, the letters holding their shape the way a person holds their shape at the end of a long walk, which is to say: approximately, and with effort — the person whose name was on the front was not here, had perhaps never been here, had perhaps moved, or died, or been misnamed, or been the invention of whoever had given me the envelope, which was — I tried to remember — someone in the apartment, or someone at the door of the apartment, or someone on the street outside the apartment, a person whose face I cannot now reconstruct because I was looking at the envelope and not at the person, and the envelope was the only face the errand had, and the face was dissolving.

I put the envelope in my pocket, next to the stone. The two objects occupied the pocket differently: the stone was heavy and still; the envelope was light and damp and seemed to be moving, shifting, settling into the pocket's folds, finding the lowest point, the angle of repose — the angle at which a material stops sliding and rests, the angle determined by friction and gravity and the specific character of the surface, the angle at which the thing being held and the thing holding it reach an agreement.

I walked back. South, which should have been north, because I was returning, but the city had done what it does, which is rearrange itself while you are not looking, and south felt like the right direction, and the body — my body, the body that had deviated me across a river and back, the body whose left knee had opinions about distance and whose right hip had opinions about direction and whose hands had opinions about stones — the body was walking south, and the body, at this point in the day, was the only navigation I had.

I would try again tomorrow. Or I would not try again tomorrow. The envelope was in my pocket, next to the stone, and the pocket was large enough for both, and both were patient, and patience, which is the virtue of objects that have nowhere to be, is also the virtue of errands that have failed to arrive, and of knees that have failed to maintain the stride they were given and have instead invented their own, shorter, slower, more curved, a stride that is writing something on the pavement that I cannot read.

My right hip pulled me east. I let it. The street was not the street I needed but it was the street I was on and the two are never the same and I have stopped expecting them to be.
