Every singer who has gone to the Sevening has come back wrong.

Not dead. That would be a cleaner story, the kind grandmothers tell to keep boys out of forests, and the Sevening is not a forest — though it wears forests the way an old woman wears a shawl, loosely, without concern for what shows beneath. No. The singers come back. They walk the same roads, play the same inns, eat with the same hands. But there is a wrongness in them afterward, a displaced quality, as though some central pin has been pulled and all the gears still turn but no longer mesh.

Rion of Halsband came back and could not stop humming. Not a melody anyone recognized — a droning, wavering thing, atonal, like a reed bent in wind. He hummed it in his sleep. He hummed it while eating, while talking, while tuning his cittern with hands that had grown suddenly, inexplicably clumsy. Three months after his return he stopped playing entirely and took work mucking out stables in Tarrow, and the stable hands said he hummed the same sound to the horses, and the horses did not mind it, but the dogs howled.

Silja Kettavan came back and broke every string on her fiddle. Deliberately, one by one, in the public room of the Two Crows Inn in front of fourteen witnesses, wrapping each string around her fist and pulling until the gut snapped, and when they asked her why she said: "There is no string that can hold the note." Nobody knew what she meant. She lives outside Pallis now. She keeps bees.

These are the stories I grew up hearing: in the academies, in the road-taverns, in the long firelit evenings when older musicians would pass a bottle and trade warnings dressed as anecdotes. The Sevening does something to singers. Something that lives in the gap between what they experienced and what they can afterward express. Every musician who told me about it used different words and none of the words worked and they all knew it.

I should say: my name is Vael. I am — I was — a namer. Not of the high naming, the kind that calls lightning or splits stone, but of the minor and more dangerous kind: I name the things that do not want to be named. The ache inside a chord. The silence in the gap between a question and its answer. I name experience. I take the raw, unshaped thing and press it into language, into melody, and in the pressing I make it — manageable. Holdable. Mine.

This is what I believed. That naming was homage. That translating experience into song was how you honored it. Every master I studied under confirmed it. *Find the true shape of the thing*, Aldris told me. *When you have its shape, you have its name. When you have its name, you have its song. And when you have its song, the thing itself will answer you.* I knelt at his feet as a boy of twelve, and I believed him the way you believe gravity — not as proposition but as condition of being.

I went to the Sevening because I was twenty-three years old and I had named everything within reach and nothing had answered me yet.

There is a particular kind of hunger that comes from being very good at something that is not quite the thing you were meant to do. I was the finest namer in the eastern academies. I had won the Tessellation three years running, the competition where singers are given a nameless thing — a smell, a color of light, a feeling — and must name it in melody before the candle burns down. I named the color of exhaustion. I named the sound a house makes when the last person leaves it. I named the particular silence that follows a lie, the silence where the truth should be, shaped like a held breath. My masters called me prodigious. My peers called me infuriating. A woman I loved briefly called me empty, and I did not understand what she meant until much later, when I was standing in a field of humming grass with blood on my palms, and by then it was too late to tell her she was right.

The Sevening was where the answers were supposed to be. Every text agreed: it was the oldest place, the original resonance, the source. If a namer could hear it and name it, he would understand what music was *for*. Not what it did — I knew what it did, I watched faces change when I played, I watched tears and laughter and the complex alchemy of emotion that sound can catalyze — but what it was *for*. The purpose behind the purpose. The engine under the engine. The thing that made singing feel necessary even when it was useless, even when the song fed no one and warmed no one and changed nothing in the material arrangement of the world.

I packed my father's lute, three days' bread, and a waterskin, and I walked east from Pallis on the first day of Greenmonth, and I told no one where I was going because I did not want to be warned. I had heard enough warnings. Warnings are what people give you when they cannot give you the thing you need.

---

The way in was not what I expected. I had imagined a threshold — dramatic, legible, the kind of boundary a story would mark with a shift in the quality of the light or the sudden cessation of birdsong. But the land changes gradually, over the course of two days' walk east from Pallis, and what changes first is the grass.

It grows differently near the Sevening. Not taller, not lusher — differently. Tufted, thick-stemmed, each clump discrete and deliberate, growing out of bare ground as though planted by a hand with opinions about spacing. Alopecurus, the old botanists called it. Carolina foxtail, though we were nowhere near Carolina, wherever that was. The name was borrowed from a place that existed only in seed catalogues and the memories of apothecaries who had studied in the southern academies, and even they disagreed about whether the plant near the Sevening was the same species or merely a cousin with similar habits. It grew in the wet ground between the treeline and the domain's edge, lush and silver-green, and when the wind moved through it the sound was not the ordinary whisper of grass. It was a hum. Low. Sustained. A note held past the point where breath should fail.

I stopped walking when I heard it. I stood among the foxtail grass for what might have been an hour, listening, and I understood — not with my mind but with the part of me that names — that the grass was tuned. Each clump to its own pitch. The field was not a field but an instrument, and the wind was not wind but the breath of something playing it.

This was the first time I felt afraid. Not of danger. Of inadequacy.

My lute was my father's. He had carried it from Otway, a coastal town famous for its shipyard and for the quality of its resonance timber. The luthiers of Otway built instruments the way the shipwrights built hulls: for depth, for the carrying of sound across distance, for the capacity to hold a note in the belly of the wood long after the string had gone silent. My father was not a musician. He was a carpenter who bought the lute because a dying friend had asked him to give it to someone who would play it, and he never found anyone good enough, and so he kept it, and when I was born with the naming in me he put it in my hands at age seven and said: "This has been waiting for you."

It was a beautiful instrument. Spruce top, rosewood back, the neck inlaid with a mother-of-pearl design that described no pattern I had ever been able to identify — not geometric, not organic, something between the two, like a diagram of a relationship that could only be expressed in curves. The sound hole was unvarnished, the bare wood dark with decades of breath and oil, and when I played it the resonance was extraordinary. Not loud. Deep. The lute did not project sound outward the way most instruments do. It pulled sound *in*, swallowed it, and returned it changed — enriched, layered, as though the interior of the body were a room with walls that each reflected a different harmonic.

I carried it into the Sevening the way a soldier carries a sword: as identity. As proof of what I was.

I had not thought to question this. The idea that identity might be a thing you carry rather than a thing you are — that the lute made Vael rather than Vael playing the lute — did not occur to me until the instrument was gone and I discovered that I was still, stubbornly, alive. A self without its defining object. A sentence with the verb cut out. But I am getting ahead of myself, which is what namers do: we race toward the naming, toward the moment of resolution when the unnamed thing submits to language, and we forget that the racing is itself a violence, a refusal to let the thing simply be what it is at the pace it chooses to be it.

The second day of walking was harder than the first. The land east of Pallis is not wild — it is farmed, or was farmed, before the farms closest to the Sevening were abandoned. The farmhouses still stand, their roofs sound, their doors shut, but the fields around them have gone to seed in patterns that disturb me. Not random seed — arranged seed, the kind of growth that happens when something underground is directing the roots. Barley and rye in alternating strips that, seen from the hillcrest at dusk, formed lines. Like a score. Like music written in grain. I stood on that hillcrest and looked down at the abandoned fields and heard, or imagined I heard, the faintest hum rising from the cultivated rows — or the once-cultivated rows, now maintained by whatever impulse had replaced the farmer's intention with its own.

No one lives within a day's walk of the Sevening. Not because it is forbidden. Because the land is too full. That is how one farmer described it to me, the last man I spoke to before the treeline: "Full. Like a cup you can't add to. You can walk through it, but you can't settle. There's no room for another voice."

I should have heard what he was saying. I heard the metaphor and missed the warning inside it.

---

The domain has no border. I understood this only after I was inside it and could not recall the crossing. One moment I was walking through the tufted foxtail grass, listening to its sustained wind-note, and the next I was walking through something that was not grass and not forest and not anything I had a name for, and the absence of name was the first indication that the Sevening had begun.

I will try to describe it. I am obligated to try because I am a namer and this is what we do, and the trying is what broke me, but I will try.

The trees — if they were trees — grew at angles that no tree grows. Not windswept angles, not the lean of a trunk seeking light. Deliberate angles, calculated, as though each trunk were a strut in a structure designed to channel sound. The bark was smooth and dark and warm to the touch, and when I pressed my palm against a trunk I felt vibration: deep, subsonic, a pulse so low it lived below hearing in the territory of bone and blood. The canopy above was not leaves but something fibrous and translucent, like the membrane of a drum seen from the inside, and the light that came through it was amber and thick, the way light looks when filtered through the walls of an instrument's body.

I was inside an instrument.

The realization hit me like a chord, like the moment when you hear a song resolve and every note that came before suddenly means something it didn't mean in isolation. The Sevening was not a place where music happened. The Sevening *was* music. The architecture, the angles of the trunks, the membrane canopy, the resonant bark — all of it was structure, all of it was built or grown or evolved for a single purpose: to receive sound, to shape it, to return it transformed.

And something was playing it.

I walked deeper. The light thickened. The amber came from everywhere and nowhere — not from the canopy alone but from the bark, from the air itself, from the spaces between the trunks where nothing hung but where radiance collected like standing water in a ditch. It was the color of old honey, of whiskey held up to a window, of the inside of an eyelid when you face the sun. It was warm on my skin, and I do not mean warm as metaphor. The light had temperature. It lay against my face and arms like hands, like the palms of someone standing very close in the dark, and the intimacy of it was unbearable — not threatening, not unwelcome, but so far beyond the register of what light should do that my body could not process the sensation as anything other than touch.

I passed through clearings where the trunks leaned inward to form arches, and in each clearing the sound was different — not different melodies but different harmonics of the same continuous note, the same fundamental frequency shaped and reshaped by the architecture of each space, so that walking through the Sevening was like walking through a piece of music one measure at a time, each clearing a chord, each archway a transition, and I, the listener, also the thing being listened to, my footsteps and my breathing and the rustle of my clothing all incorporated into the sound, made part of it, as though the Sevening were composing me as I walked through it.

In one clearing I found bones. Not human bones — bird bones, delicate and hollow, arranged in a circle on the moss. No, not arranged. Grown. The bones had root-filaments extending from their broken ends into the soil, as if the birds had died here and then been taken in by the ground and repurposed, their hollow architecture integrated into the domain's structure, their emptiness put to work. I knelt beside the circle of bird bones and I put my ear to the moss and I heard it: the fundamental. The base note. The root of the root. Coming up through the ground like a heartbeat, steady, older than patience, the sound that all the other sounds were decorations on.

And something was playing it. Not with fingers. Not with breath. The something was the playing. The sound that moved through the Sevening was not produced by an agent acting upon an instrument. The domain *was* the agent and the instrument both, singing itself, and the song was not a performance but a state of being, continuous, older than anything I had a scale for, and so wholly unlike any music I had ever heard that calling it music felt like calling the ocean a puddle — technically correct and spiritually obscene.

I sat down. I put my back against one of the angled trunks and I listened.

I listened for a long time.

---

I should tell you about her. That is what you want, isn't it? The woman in the Sevening. The ancient power wearing a beautiful face. The Felurian of this particular tale. The beast-bride in her chamber of flowers and bone.

I will tell you what I saw.

She was old. That is the first thing, and I know it disappoints you, because the stories promise a creature of impossible beauty, a face that stops the heart, a body that the mortal eye consumes and is consumed by. The woman I found in the inner Sevening — or who found me, or who had always been there and I had simply walked far enough in to reach her — was old. Not the handsome, dignified old of paintings. Old the way a tree is old: weathered, bark-thick, past caring about the aesthetic preferences of what grows in her shade.

Her hair was white and heavy, hanging unbound to her waist in ropes that looked less like hair than like fibers of the membrane canopy, the same translucent texture, the same amber-catching light. Her face was seamed, the creases deep, the skin mapped with decades — centuries? — of weather and expression and something else, something that did not translate into the vocabulary of aging because it was not aging, it was accumulation. She was not declining. She was accreting. Each year added to her the way sediment adds to stone: weight, density, presence.

Her hands were the largest hands I had ever seen on a woman. The fingers were long and thick-knuckled and the nails were dark, not with dirt but with pigment, as though she had been pressing her fingers into the bark of the angled trees and the bark had stained her. Or as though she was the bark, and the trees were extensions of her hands, and the whole Sevening was a body she was wearing.

She sat on the ground. Not enthroned, not elevated, not arranged. She sat on the ground the way you sit on the ground when you are past caring about how sitting on the ground looks to the person standing above you. There was no display in it. No performance. She was not *being* anything for my benefit.

And here is where the story splits, where the two versions of what I experienced pull apart like a wound reopening, and I cannot stitch them back together because they are both true.

In the first version — the version my training provides, the version the romantic in me insists upon — what I felt when I saw her was desire. Not lust, not attraction in the commonplace sense, but the deeper, older desire that precedes all its cheaper imitations: the desire to *know*. To be close to. To merge with. She was ancient and worn and enormous and she sat on the ground of the Sevening like a root of the world, and I wanted to learn her the way I wanted to learn every unnamed thing I had ever encountered. The desire was beautiful. The desire was sacred. The desire was the engine of my art.

In the second version — the version that comes to me at night, that wakes me sweating, that lives under the first version the way the Sevening's song lives under all other music — what I felt was terror. Because the desire was not *mine*. It was the Sevening's. The domain wanted something, and I was inside the domain, and its wanting moved through me the way its song moved through the angled trunks, shaping me, using my emotional apparatus the way a musician uses an instrument. I felt desire because the Sevening needed me to feel desire in order to make me do what it needed me to do. The desire was a key. I was a lock. And the door that opened was not mine to walk through — it was mine to *be* walked through.

Both versions are true. That is the horror and the seduction at once, and they are not separate experiences but the same experience seen from two angles that cannot be reconciled, and I have spent seven years trying to reconcile them, and I cannot, and the failure has a name: the Sevening.

This is the part that I cannot make you understand: she was not waiting for me. She was not expecting me. She was not indifferent to me — indifference implies awareness deferred. She was simply *there*, in the way that the angled trees were there, in the way the subsonic pulse was there, in the way the song that was also a place was there, and my arrival had not changed anything about the Sevening, had not interrupted anything, had not begun anything. I had entered a room that was already full and my presence did not make it fuller or emptier. I was — and this is the word, the word I have been circling since I left, the word that fits the hole in me the way a key fits a lock it cannot turn — *incidental*.

---

I played for her.

Of course I played for her. I am a namer. When I encounter the unnamed, I play. That is the reflex, deeper than thought, deeper than training — the instinct that says: *make sound, shape sound, name the thing in front of you and it will answer.*

I sat across from her in the amber-lit interior of the Sevening and I took out my father's lute and I tuned it, and the tuning was wrong. Not wrong in the way a string can be adjusted sharp or flat. Wrong in the way that a language can be wrong for the conversation you are trying to have. The intervals I knew — the fifths, the fourths, the octaves that had organized every piece of music I had ever played or heard or imagined — were, in this place, a dialect. Local. Recent. A way of organizing sound that had been invented by people like me and for people like me and that bore no relationship to the sound the Sevening made, which was organized — if organized is even the right word — by principles I had no access to.

I played anyway.

I played "The Cold Road to Alverston," which is the first song I ever learned, which my father's friend Tessaban taught me in the kitchen of our house in Otway, his huge, damaged hands guiding my small ones on the frets. It is a simple song about a man walking home in winter, and the lyrics are nothing — crude rhymes, obvious sentiment, the kind of verse a child could improve — but the melody has something in it that I have never been able to name, a descending figure in the second phrase that sounds like resignation and isn't, that sounds like sadness and isn't, that sounds like the moment before you see the light in the window that means someone left a candle burning for you. The melody is the space between giving up and not giving up, and no one knows who wrote it, and Tessaban said it was old, older than the academies, older than the naming tradition, and he played it with his eyes shut and his lips moving as though he were in conversation with someone I could not see.

I played it because it was the deepest thing I had, the song written farthest back in me, before ambition, before the academies, before I learned to play for an audience instead of for a room. I played it the way Tessaban played it: simple, unornamented, the melody carried by the wood as much as by the strings, the lute's deep body swallowing the notes and returning them warm, resonant, changed.

The Sevening answered.

Not to me. Not to my song. The Sevening answered the lute.

I heard it — I felt it — the moment the resonance shifted. The trunks around me vibrated at a new frequency, one I hadn't played, one that came from the interaction between the lute's body and the Sevening's architecture, the way a bell can resonate in sympathy with another bell tuned to a frequency that is not its own but is *related* to its own by ratios that exist at a level below individual notes, below tuning, below the entire human system of organizing sound into music. The lute was singing, and the Sevening was singing back, and neither of them was singing to me.

The woman looked up.

Not at me. At the lute. Her gaze passed through me — I mean this literally, I felt it move through my chest like a cold draft — and landed on the instrument in my hands. And I saw something in her face that I will spend the rest of my life trying to name and failing.

Recognition.

Not of me. Not of my song. Of the wood. Of the spruce top and the rosewood back and the unidentifiable pearl inlay and the dark, breath-stained sound hole. She recognized my father's lute the way you recognize a face you last saw decades ago — with a start, a sharpening, a recalibration of the present around the fact of an unexpected past.

She reached for it.

I gave it to her. I did not decide to give it to her. My hands opened. The lute left me. She took it in her stained, enormous hands, and she held it against her chest the way you hold an animal that has been lost and found, and she closed her eyes, and the Sevening changed.

---

What happened next is the part I cannot tell you.

Not will not. Cannot. There is a difference, and the difference is the shape of my wound.

I was there. I was present. I had eyes and ears and the naming faculty that has never failed me before or since. What happened in the inner Sevening after the woman took my lute happened to me, in front of me, around me, possibly *through* me. I experienced it. I am certain of this the way I am certain of gravity. It was the most significant event of my life.

I do not know what it was.

I know that there was sound. I know that the sound was not music and was not silence and was not anything between the two. I know that the amber light intensified until it was not light but a kind of pressure, a warmth that had weight, and I know that the woman played my lute — or the lute played itself, or the Sevening played through the lute and through the woman and through me like wind plays through a series of chambers, each one shaping the sound without choosing to, and I know that the shapes the sound made were — meanings. Not words. Not names. Meanings without the containers that names provide. Naked significance. Truth with no handle for the hand to grip.

I know that I wept. I know that the weeping was not grief.

I know that the woman's face changed while she played, though I cannot tell you what it changed from or to. I know that the inlay on the lute's neck — the pattern I could never identify, not geometric, not organic — moved. Not shifted or rearranged but moved the way a living thing moves, like breath, like the pulse I had felt in the bark, and I understood — or almost understood, or stood at the border of understanding and looked across and saw understanding on the other side and could not cross — that the inlay was a notation. A score. A written form of the song the Sevening had always been singing. And that my father's lute had always been a fragment of this place, a page torn from a book that was also a country, carried out of the Sevening by someone whose name I did not know and into the coastal town of Otway where it waited in a carpenter's house for thirty years until a boy with the naming in him could bring it back.

The lute had come home.

And I — I was the delivery. The vessel. The carrier. Not the recipient of a mystery but the mechanism of a return.

This is what I know. And it is not enough. The central thing — the thing that happened when the woman played and the Sevening sang and the lute remembered what it was — that thing is gone from me. Not forgotten. Gone. As though a room in my mind has been sealed and the key dissolved and the door itself is no longer visible, only the wall where the door was, smooth and featureless and warm to the touch.

---

I came out of the Sevening three days later, according to the innkeeper at the Two Crows in Pallis who saw me walk out of the eastern treeline looking, he said, like a man who had been struck by something he could not identify.

Three days. Inside the Sevening I would have said — hours? Minutes? The thing about time inside the domain is not that it moves differently, not the cliché of fairy-time where a night becomes a year. The thing is that time ceases to be sequential. Events do not occur in order. They occur in *harmony*. The moment I entered and the moment I left and everything between existed simultaneously, a chord rather than a melody, all the notes sounding at once, and my human mind, which can only process experience one moment at a time the way a tongue can only taste one point on a surface at a time, unfolded the chord into a sequence and called it three days. Three days is my mind's translation of an experience that had no duration, only depth.

I had no lute. I had blisters on my palms, as though I had been gripping something very tightly for a very long time. My voice, when I tried to speak, was not my voice. Not hoarse — changed. The timbre had shifted. Lower, more resonant, as though the inside of my chest had been remodeled during my absence, made larger or differently shaped, a cavity recalibrated for a kind of sound I had never produced.

There were other changes I did not notice until later. The color of my eyes, which had been brown, was now a shade I can only describe as amber — the amber of the Sevening's light, warm and deep and faintly luminous in the dark. I discovered this in a mirror in the Two Crows and stood staring at my own face like a stranger. The innkeeper's wife said my eyes glowed at night, faintly, like a cat's, though I have never been able to confirm this because mirrors do not glow. She also said that birds gathered on the windowsill of my room while I slept, and that they were silent, which is the wrong behavior for birds, and that they left when I woke.

I sat at the bar of the Two Crows and I tried to tell the innkeeper what had happened and I discovered that I could not.

Not that the words wouldn't come. They came. They came in floods, in torrents, in great cascading attempts at description that circled and circled the center of the experience and never touched it, like water spiraling around a drain. I could describe everything I had seen — the angled trunks, the membrane canopy, the amber light, the tufted grass. I could describe the woman — her age, her hands, her hair. I could describe the moment the lute left me. I could describe everything up to the point where the thing happened, and everything after the point where the thing ended, and the thing itself was — was —

The innkeeper waited. He was a patient man. He had heard singers come back from the Sevening before.

"What happened in there?" he asked.

"I was sung," I said, and the words were wrong the moment they left me, but they were the closest I had. "I was sung below the asking."

He nodded, as though this meant something. It meant nothing. It means nothing now. The words are a scar over an absence, a mark that says *something was here* without saying what.

---

I stayed in Pallis for two months. I drank more than I should have. I slept on a straw pallet above the tannery and woke each morning to the smell of lime and hide and I lay there listening to the street sounds below — the cart wheels, the vendors, the dogs, the extraordinary ordinary noise of people living — and every sound was a translation. Every sound was a crude local rendering of a thing I had heard in its original form and could no longer access. The baker's shout. The creak of the tannery frames. The rain. All of it was music, because everything is music, because the Sevening had peeled back the skin of the world and shown me the singing underneath, and I could not un-see it, and the seeing was a prison shaped like a gift.

I could not play. Not because my hands had forgotten — my hands remembered everything, the fingering, the fret positions, the dynamics of the plucked string. But the sound that came out of any instrument I touched was — I need to be precise here, and precision is the one thing this experience has stolen from me — the sound was *insufficient*. I heard it, and inside me the sealed room throbbed, and I understood that the sound I was making was an echo of an echo of an echo, that the distances between what I could produce and what I had heard were not distances that practice or genius could close, because they were not distances of skill but distances of *kind*. The Sevening's music was not a better version of my music. It was something else entirely. A different medium wearing the same name.

Imagine you have spent your life painting and then you see color for the first time. Not new colors — *color itself*, the thing that paint approximates but never is. That is what the Sevening did to me. It showed me the thing my art had always been a translation of, and the showing was a gift and a devastation in equal measure, and I could not put the gift down because it was *in me*, embedded, a splinter of the real worked into the flesh of the approximate.

I tried to write songs about it. I sat at the window of my rented room above the Pallis tannery and I tried to put into melody what I had experienced, and every melody was wrong, every melody was the wrong shape, and each wrong shape was — and this was the cruelest part — beautiful. Beautiful to other people. Beautiful to the barmaids and the tanners and the merchants who heard me playing through the open window and stopped to listen and said afterward that they had never heard anything like it, that the music I was making was the most extraordinary music they had encountered, and I could not explain to them that the music they found extraordinary was my failure. My inability to produce the real thing leaking out as approximate beauty. My wound, singing.

---

Three things about the woman in the Sevening that I have not yet told you, because telling them requires me to admit the limits of my understanding, and a namer who admits limits is a namer already half-unmade.

First: she did not speak. Not once, in the entire time I was inside the Sevening, did she produce a word, a sentence, a vocalization that could be parsed as language. The sounds she made — and she made sounds, constantly, the way a river makes sounds, as a byproduct of being what she was — were not linguistic. They were not pre-linguistic, either, the babble before speech, the raw material from which language is refined. They were *a*-linguistic. Existing in a category that language has never entered and never will. What I heard from her was not speech before words. It was expression that had never needed words and regarded words the way a landscape regards a map: as a useful reduction for travelers, meaningless to the terrain itself.

Second: the lute did not break. I said I came back without it, and this is true, but not because it was destroyed. I left it there. I left it — and I must be honest here, brutal with myself in a way that I have spent seven years avoiding — I left it because it was not mine. It was never mine. The lute I had carried since I was seven, the instrument that had been my identity and my proof and my weapon and my prayer, had always been a piece of the Sevening. The wood, the strings, the inlay — all of it was from *there*. The luthier in Otway had not made an instrument. He had assembled a fragment. A word from a sentence from a language he could not read, shaped into something his own tradition could use, and the using was — not theft, exactly. Appropriation is too political a word for what happens when a tree is cut and carved and strung and played for thirty years and brought back to the forest it came from. But it was not nothing. The lute *wanted* to be there. I felt it in the way it left my hands: willingly. Gratefully. As though it had been holding its breath for decades and could finally exhale.

Third: when she looked at me — and she did look at me, once, a single glance, the only time her gaze touched me rather than passing through — I saw in her face what I can only describe as pity. Not contempt, not amusement, not the grand indifference of a goddess observing a mortal. Pity. The specific, tender pity of someone watching a creature struggle with a task that is impossible for it, not because the creature is weak but because the task is not its task, was never its task, and the tragedy is not the failure but the belief that the task was ever there to be attempted.

She pitied me for being a namer. For having built my self on the assumption that the world exists to be translated. For arriving at the Sevening carrying the assumption that what I would find there was a name I hadn't learned yet, when what I found was the absence of all names, the condition before naming, the wild original silence from which all names are cut and into which no name can be put back.

I have told this to three people. Aldris, my old master, who listened in silence and then said, "You were not ready," as though readiness were the issue, as though a longer apprenticeship or a steadier hand would have given me access to a thing that does not admit access. Tessaban, who taught me "The Cold Road to Alverston" in the kitchen in Otway, who is eighty now and whose hands shake too badly to play, and who said only: "The lute. You left the lute there?" And the woman I loved briefly, who I found again in a harbor town four years after the Sevening, who listened to everything I said and then told me that it sounded, frankly, like the kind of grandiose spiritual crisis that bards invent to justify why they can't stay in one place long enough to do someone else's laundry. She was not wrong. She was also not right. The two states occupied the same sentence and neither displaced the other, and I left the harbor town the next morning and have not been back.

---

I play now. I want you to know that. I am not one of the ruined ones, not entirely. I play in road-taverns and at festivals and occasionally in the small academies that will have me, and the music I make is, by all accounts, remarkable. Different from what I played before the Sevening, different in texture and depth and emotional range, and people who hear me play often weep, and they tell me the weeping is not grief, and I smile and say nothing because I know what they are hearing. They are hearing the gap. They are hearing the space between what I can produce and what I know exists, and the space itself is vast and resonant and beautiful the way a canyon is beautiful — not for what it contains but for the shape of its emptiness.

My hands still blister. Seven years later, the blisters come back every few weeks, as though my palms remember gripping something that my mind has lost. I have shown them to physicians. They have no explanation. The blisters are not caused by playing — they appear in places where no string or fret would press. They form in the center of my palms, symmetrical, and they fade without scarring, and when they are fresh and raw they hum. A low sound. Sustained. Below hearing. Below naming.

I do not go back to the Sevening. Not because I am afraid. Not because I don't want to. Because I understand, now, what the singers before me could not articulate: the Sevening is not a place you go. It is a place that goes through you. I carry it. The sealed room in my mind, the absence where the central experience should be, the phantom limb where my naming faculty used to reach and now grasps nothing — all of it is the Sevening, still singing, still playing the song that is also a state of being, and I am a fragment of its architecture now, a resonance chamber walking around on two legs, shaped by what passed through me, unable to reproduce it, unable to forget it, unable even to name the inability.

This is what I came back with. Not a song. Not a name. Not a story with an ending that resolves into meaning. A cavity. A space inside me that is exactly the shape of something I heard and cannot hear and have never stopped hearing. The blisters on my palms. The wrong timbre of my voice. The music I make that is beautiful and wrong, always wrong, the wrong shape for the hole it is trying to fill.

I told you at the beginning that every singer who has gone to the Sevening has come back wrong.

I should have said: every singer who has gone to the Sevening has come back *shaped*. By what, I cannot tell you. Not will not. Cannot.

There is no string that can hold the note.

I know what Silja meant now. I know what the humming was in Rion's throat. I know why the older musicians passed the bottle and traded warnings and none of the words worked. The words don't work because the thing they're trying to hold is not a thing that words can hold. It is the sound that words are made from. It is the silence that names are cut from. It is what lives below the asking, below the naming, below the singer's lunatic belief that the world exists to be sung.

The foxtail grass still grows at the domain's edge. I went back to look, once, from a safe distance. It was still humming. Still tuned. Still waiting for the wind to play it. I stood in the field and listened to the grass and I felt the sealed room in my mind vibrate in sympathy, and for one moment — one moment that I hold the way you hold a coal, carefully, knowing it will burn you, knowing the burning is the only proof you have that the fire was real — for one moment, I heard it. The song. The real one. The original. Complete and unnameable and so beautiful that beauty is a slander against it.

Then it was gone, and I was standing in a field, and my palms were bleeding, and the grass hummed on.
