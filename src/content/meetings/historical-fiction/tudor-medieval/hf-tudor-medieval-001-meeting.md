Jones was already seated when I arrived at the pub in Bermondsey, which was not the pub I'd suggested. I'd suggested somewhere near the British Library. Jones had texted back a single line — "I don't want to be near an archive today" — and sent me an address south of the river, a place with low ceilings and carpet that had survived multiple decades without anyone deciding to replace it. He was drinking something dark, reading a folded newspaper he'd found on the bar.

Follett came in ten minutes later, stooping through the doorway. He's a tall man and the doorway was built for people who lived in centuries when nobody was. He surveyed the room the way I imagine he surveys buildings in the novels — structurally, assessing what bore weight and what was decorative. He seemed to conclude that very little in this pub was decorative.

"Edward," he said.

"Ken."

They shook hands. It was a brief handshake, almost formal, between two men who knew each other's work well enough that any pretense of casual discovery would have been insulting.

"Medieval," I said, because someone had to start.

"Tudor-medieval," Follett corrected. "Which is a period, not a mood, though people treat it as a mood. Rushes on the floor. Plague carts. Henry's wives. People think they know it because they've watched something on television, and what they've watched is costumes and betrayal and someone getting beheaded, and they believe the period is about power at the top and suffering at the bottom and nothing in between."

"There's always something in between," Jones said. He hadn't looked up from the newspaper, or rather he'd looked up and then looked back down, a gesture I'd come to understand was not rudeness but the way he listened — by appearing not to. "The in-between is where most people live. In my experience, the people at the top and the people at the bottom are equally convinced that nobody occupies the middle, because the middle is inconvenient to both their stories."

"What story does the middle tell?"

Jones folded the newspaper. "The middle tells the story of getting by. Not rising, not falling, not heroic, not tragic. Getting by. And getting by in a hierarchical society means participating in the hierarchy without thinking of it as participation. You're not oppressing anyone. You're paying your tithes. You're not collaborating with the system. You're selling your grain at the market price. The violence of ordinariness — that's what interests me. The people who aren't making decisions but whose daily actions make the decisions operational."

Follett ordered a pint. The barman pulled it slowly, with the inattentive expertise of someone who'd pulled that same lever several thousand times. The foam was good. Follett noticed the foam. I think Follett notices physical processes in the way most writers notice emotional cues.

"I've spent thirty years writing about medieval towns," Follett said. "Kingsbridge. Caris. Merthin. Gwenda. These are people in the middle — the wool merchant, the builder, the nun with medical knowledge, the serf who runs away and comes back. And the engine of the story, the thing that makes it work across nine hundred pages, is ambition. They want something. A bridge. A hospital. A cathedral. And the wanting propels them through the system, through the obstacles the system creates. I'm not embarrassed by ambition as a narrative engine. I think it's honest. People in medieval towns wanted things with ferocious clarity."

"White people in medieval towns," Jones said.

The pub went quiet, or seemed to. In reality it was already quiet — three men at a table, a barman, a dog asleep under a stool near the window. But Jones's sentence created a different kind of quiet.

"Free people in medieval towns," Follett said. "White, yes. English, yes. But also: free. My characters are constrained, they fight within constraints, they sometimes lose. But they are never property. That's a boundary my novels don't cross because it's not my material. The people I write about can appeal to the law, even when the law is unjust. The people you write about exist in a place where the law is the instrument of their subjection."

"And also the instrument of their own power," Jones said. "That's the part nobody wants to talk about. The Known World is about Black slaveholders. Free Black men who owned other Black people. Not as a curiosity. Not as an exception. As a system. A system that operated by the same logic as the white system — property, personhood, ledger, deed. The oppressed didn't simply suffer the hierarchy. They replicated it. They found their place in it, and their place required someone beneath them."

I'd been waiting for this. The Known World was the center of gravity for what Jones brought to this meeting — the refusal of clean moral categories, the insistence that power corrupts across every line of identity, that complicity is not a failure of character but a condition of participation. And the question I'd been turning over for days was whether that insight could survive transplantation to a medieval English town.

"Can it?" I asked. "Can that idea — the oppressed also oppress, no clean categories — work in a Tudor-medieval setting? Or does it belong specifically to the American context, to the racial architecture of slavery?"

"Every hierarchy produces that structure," Jones said. "Every one. The serf who has authority over his wife. The wife who has authority over her servant. The servant who has authority over the vagabond begging at the kitchen door. The question isn't whether medieval England had people who were simultaneously oppressed and oppressing. The question is whether you can write it without turning it into a lesson. The danger is the moral diagram — here is the power, here is the powerlessness, here is the complicity. As soon as the reader can draw the diagram, the story is dead."

"So how do you avoid the diagram?"

"You make it personal. You make the complicity look like love, or duty, or habit, or appetite. In The Known World, the slaveholder loves his property. Not metaphorically. He loves them the way you love a family member you also control. That's not a contradiction for him. It's Monday. And the reader sits with that love and feels its reality and also feels its monstrousness and cannot separate the two, because the character cannot separate the two."

Follett had been listening with his chin on his fist, the posture of someone absorbing rather than waiting to respond. Now he straightened.

"I think about the Black Death," he said. "Not the dying, though the dying matters. What I think about is what happens after. Who rises. In World Without End, the plague kills a third of England and the survivors — the ones who'd been invisible, the laborers, the women, the marginally skilled — suddenly have leverage. Labor is scarce. Wages go up. The old order tries to reassert itself through the Statute of Laborers, which is essentially legislation designed to make people stay in the social position they were born to, and it doesn't work. The plague is a revolution conducted by a microbe. Nobody planned it. Nobody led it. The hierarchy simply developed a crack, and people climbed through."

"And what did they build on the other side?" Jones asked.

"A different hierarchy."

"Right. That's what I'd expect. The crack in the wall doesn't abolish walls. It lets certain people through to the other side, where they immediately start building their own wall. The new order that replaces the old order carries the old order's DNA. The woman who rises to become a wool merchant after the plague doesn't abolish the system that kept her down. She joins it. She benefits from it. She hires servants and pays them poorly and explains this to herself as the way things work."

"You're describing Caris," Follett said. There was something in his voice — not defensiveness, but recognition. A writer hearing his character described from an angle he hadn't written.

"I haven't read World Without End," Jones said.

"You've just described one of its central characters without reading it, which either means the character is archetypal or you and I are thinking along the same lines, and I'm not sure which possibility disturbs me more."

Jones almost smiled. "The archetype exists because the pattern exists. The person who escapes a system and replicates it is not a failure of imagination. It's the most common story in the world. The American version is just louder."

I brought up the risk card. "We've been given genre subversion," I said. "The story has to set up the conventions of medieval historical fiction and then break them. I've been thinking about what those conventions are — what readers expect when they open a story set in a medieval town."

"They expect stone," Follett said immediately. "Stone walls, stone churches, cobblestone streets. They expect mud. They expect hierarchy made physical — the lord in the manor, the merchant in the high street, the beggars outside the gates. They expect a character who is either rising through the hierarchy or being crushed by it. They expect a plot driven by inheritance, or plague, or war, or the church. They expect the smell of piss and woodsmoke. They expect an ending where something has changed — the cathedral is built, the siege is broken, the rightful heir is restored."

"And they expect the medieval world to be foreign," Jones said. "That's the genre's foundational assumption. The past is another country. The reader visits it like a tourist — marveling at the cruelty, the ignorance, the dirt, the faith. The reader is positioned above the period, looking down from the modern vantage. That's the convention I'd want to break. The sense that the past is over."

"How do you break that?"

"You make the reader recognize themselves. Not through anachronism. Not by having a medieval character express modern sentiments. But by showing a social dynamic so precisely — the way a person rationalizes their place in a system, the way someone explains away the suffering their comfort requires — that the reader cannot maintain the tourist position. The reader is implicated. The past isn't over because the reader is still living in it."

Follett turned his pint glass. "That's the opposite of what historical fiction usually does. Historical fiction usually says: look how different the past was. You're saying: look how it isn't."

"I'm saying both. The past is different in its surfaces and identical in its structures. The clothes change. The architecture of complicity doesn't."

"If we break the convention," I said, "we need to know what we're replacing it with. If the story doesn't deliver the expected medieval arc — the rise, the fall, the siege, the cathedral — what does it deliver instead?"

"Stasis," Jones said. "The most honest medieval story might be one where nothing changes. Where the hierarchy at the end is the hierarchy at the beginning, and the reader has spent five thousand words coming to understand its interior logic — how it reproduces itself through the daily actions of people who believe they're simply living their lives. The drama isn't in the disruption. The drama is in the maintenance. How does a system sustain itself? Through what daily cruelties, what small compromises, what habitual blindnesses?"

"That's not a story," Follett said. "That's a condition. A story requires motion. Something has to shift, even if it shifts by an inch. A character has to want something and move toward it or away from it, and the movement, however small, is the narrative."

"A character can want things inside a system that doesn't change."

"Then the character is the story and the system is the setting, and you haven't actually broken the convention at all. You've just shrunk it. Instead of a town transforming, you've got a person struggling. The medieval pageant becomes a domestic drama in period costume."

This was a real disagreement, and I could feel it pulling the conversation toward something productive. Follett's insistence on motion — narrative as a character moving through space and time toward or away from a goal. Jones's insistence that the most truthful story might be one about the impossibility of motion — the system that holds everyone in place, not through chains but through the ordinary texture of daily life.

"What if the subversion is the absence of the thing the reader waits for?" I said. "The plague doesn't come. The siege doesn't happen. The king doesn't intervene. The reader keeps expecting the external event that will crack the hierarchy open, and it never arrives, and what they get instead is the hierarchy's quiet persistence. The medieval story without its catalyzing disaster."

"You'd need a substitute engine," Follett said. "Something local. A property dispute. A marriage negotiation. A craft guild protecting its monopoly. Something small enough that it doesn't register as historical event but large enough that it structures every relationship in the town."

"A deed," Jones said. "A piece of land. Who owns it. Who works it. Who claims it. Who the law says it belongs to and who actually sleeps on it. In The Known World, everything comes back to property — who holds the deed, whose name is on the paper, and how the paper erases the person. A medieval land dispute would carry the same weight. The deed says one thing. The ground says another. And the people living on it are caught between the document and the dirt."

"The document and the dirt," I repeated. "That's the story."

"Maybe," Jones said. He didn't commit. He never commits in these conversations, which I've learned is not uncertainty but respect for what hasn't been written yet. He won't pretend to know what the story is before the story exists.

Follett had opened his notebook — leather-bound, the pages unlined, the handwriting slanted and decisive. He wrote something I couldn't see.

"The town needs to be complete," he said. "Market, church, river, fields, walls. Not described like a museum — inhabited. The reader should smell the tannery before they see it. The buildings should have weight. I want the reader's feet on the cobblestones, and I want the cobblestones uneven, because medieval cobblestones were always uneven and the unevenness is how you know the world is real."

"And inside every building," Jones said, "someone is making a calculation they'd never describe as a calculation. The tanner decides which hides to sell cheaply and which to hold back. The decision is economic. It's also moral, because the cheap hides go to the saddler who works for the lord, and the lord protects the tanner's position, and the protection costs the tanner's neighbor, who can't sell hides at all because the tanner has the lord's favor. None of this is visible. All of it is structural. And the tanner goes home and eats his supper and does not think of himself as someone who has harmed his neighbor. He thinks of himself as someone who has provided for his family."

"The violence of ordinariness," I said, echoing what Jones had said earlier.

"Don't echo me. Find your own language for it."

I deserved that. I'd been taking notes inside my head, which is a way of cataloguing someone else's thinking rather than doing your own. Jones's corrections always land this way — not harsh, but precise, a cut that leaves you with less dead wood.

Follett drained his pint. The dog under the stool shifted, sighed, resettled. Outside, a bus passed, and the windows of the pub trembled the way old glass does — a wobble in the surface, a momentary distortion of the street. Medieval glass would have been worse. Medieval glass was full of impurities, bubbles, color variations. You saw the world through its flaws.

"I keep thinking about maps," Jones said. "The Known World ends with a map. A wall-hanging, sewn by a former slave, depicting the county — every plantation, every road, every house. But the map is also a map of power. Where the lines are drawn. Whose land touches whose. Who has a road to market and who doesn't. The map is beautiful and it's a diagram of suffering, and the beauty and the suffering are not two layers. They're one layer."

"And in a medieval town, the map is the town itself," Follett said. "The placement of buildings. Who lives near the market, who lives near the ditch. The church in the center, the lepers beyond the wall. The map is written in stone and timber and mud, and everyone can read it because everyone walks it every day. But nobody talks about it, because naming the map would mean admitting the system, and admitting the system would mean

"Would mean what?" Jones asked.

Follett didn't finish the sentence. He turned the empty glass on the table. The dog sighed again. In the silence I could hear the barman wiping down a surface that was already clean, the repetitive motion of someone maintaining order in a space that didn't require it.
