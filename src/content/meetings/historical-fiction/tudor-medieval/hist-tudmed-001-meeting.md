The room was cold in the way that only English rooms are cold — damp in the mortar, the kind of chill that doesn't respond to the fire someone had lit in the grate an hour ago. We were in a rented function room above a pub in Salisbury, because Follett had suggested meeting within sight of a cathedral and Nguyen had said he didn't care where we met as long as the coffee was drinkable. The coffee was not drinkable. It came in a carafe that had last been washed during a previous administration and tasted of that specific institutional bitterness that transcends geography. Nguyen drank it anyway.

Follett sat with his back to the window. Behind him, the spire of Salisbury Cathedral cut into a low grey sky. He'd arrived with a leather notebook and a working pen and the air of someone who expected the meeting to produce results. Nguyen had arrived with nothing, his coat still on, looking at the room the way a man might look at a crime scene — noting exits, assessing the weight-bearing capacity of what was on offer.

"Tudor," Follett said. "We should probably start with what we mean by Tudor, because it doesn't mean the same thing to both of us."

"It means a family that killed its way to power and then built churches to apologize for it," Nguyen said. "That's a story I recognize."

"It's more complicated than that."

"It's always more complicated than that. Complexity is how power justifies itself after the fact. The Tudors were complicated. The Nguyens were complicated. Every dynasty that bathed in blood is, in retrospect, complicated."

Follett opened his notebook but didn't write anything. He held the pen like an instrument he might need at any moment — a tool ready for deployment. "I've written about medieval power for thirty years. Cathedrals, priories, the architecture of faith and commerce intertwined. The thing I've learned is that complication is the story, not a distraction from it. The prior who genuinely believes he's building for God and also wants the biggest nave in England — that's not hypocrisy. That's how people actually work. They contain contradictions and don't experience them as contradictions."

"Unless they're the ones being crushed by the cathedral," Nguyen said. "The stonemason who falls from the scaffold doesn't experience the prior's contradictions as nuance. He experiences them as gravity."

I wanted to say something about the multiple narrators — the risk card we'd been dealt, the structural constraint of two or three voices whose accounts couldn't be reconciled. But the conversation was already moving faster than my instinct to manage it, and I'd learned from other meetings that the worst thing I could do was steer.

"I've been thinking about a monastery," I said instead. "Or something adjacent to a monastery. A place where knowledge is hoarded and controlled, where the architecture is a form of argument."

"The Name of the Rose," Follett said.

"Yes."

"Eco's monastery works because the mystery is intellectual. The deaths are caused by a book — a banned Aristotle, a text the institution fears because laughter threatens authority. That's elegant. That's European. That's a story where ideas are the weapon and the battlefield is a library."

"And outside the library?" Nguyen leaned forward. "Outside the monastery walls? In Eco's novel, the peasants are a backdrop. They riot at the end, they burn things, they serve as a kind of elemental force — chaos versus order. But they don't think. They don't have interiority. The novel isn't interested in what the tanner's boy believes about God. It's interested in what the monks believe about Aristotle."

Follett nodded — a quick, decisive motion. "That's true. And it's the limitation of that kind of novel. The intellectual mystery happens in the upper rooms and the rest of the world is scenery. I've always tried to write the other way: the stonemason, the wool merchant, the woman running an inn. The people who build the cathedral, not the people who commission it."

"But you make them sympathetic," Nguyen said.

The sentence hung in the room. It didn't sound like a compliment. Follett registered this.

"I make them human."

"You make them legible. Which is different. Your characters — and I respect this, I've read your work — your characters want things the reader can track. Build the cathedral. Save the child. Marry the woman. Defeat the villain. The wanting is clear. The reader can follow it, root for it, feel satisfied when it arrives. And that clarity is a form of politics."

"How so?"

"Because real power is illegible. The Tudor court wasn't a story about people who wanted clear things. It was a story about people who couldn't afford to want clear things. Thomas Cromwell doesn't want to build a cathedral. He wants to survive. And survival in that court means becoming whatever the room requires — pious with the bishop, brutal with the dissenter, invisible with the king until the king needs you to be visible. Hilary Mantel understood this. Cromwell's interiority is a fog. The reader can't quite locate him, and that's the point. He's hidden inside his own usefulness."

I felt the argument sharpening. This was the productive collision I'd been waiting for: Follett's commitment to visible human wanting against Nguyen's insistence that visibility is itself a form of power — that some people can't afford to be legible.

"Wolf Hall," I said. "That's the other gravitational pull here. The court as a killing floor, and the man who navigates it by erasing himself."

Follett set his pen down. "I admire Mantel enormously. But I'll tell you where she and I part company. Her Cromwell is a consciousness — the reader lives inside his head, inside his memories, inside his dreams. And the prose mirrors that interiority: dense, recursive, the past bleeding into the present. It's brilliant. But it's also exclusive. One consciousness at a time. The reader sees what Cromwell sees and nothing else. The world outside his perception doesn't exist."

"That's what makes it honest," Nguyen said. "We don't have access to the world outside our perception. The claim that we do — the omniscient narrator who can enter any room, see any character's private thought — that's the colonial position. The view from above. The map-maker's view. Mantel refuses it. She stays inside one skull and admits that the skull is a prison."

"The colonial position," Follett repeated, and I could hear him deciding whether to engage with the term or reject it. He chose to engage. "You're saying the omniscient narrator is an act of empire."

"I'm saying it pretends to be neutral. It pretends to see everything equally. But it always has a position — it's always standing somewhere, looking down. And the question is whose bodies it's standing on. When you write a sweeping multi-plot novel with twelve characters across three continents, you're exercising a kind of narrative sovereignty. Every character exists because you've granted them permission to exist. That's not the same as them speaking for themselves."

The fire popped. A log shifted. I got up and pushed it back with the poker, and the small domestic act felt absurd against the weight of what was being discussed.

"All right," Follett said. "Then how does this story work? Three narrators, you've said. Whose accounts don't agree. One of them has to be inside the court — a Cromwell figure, someone navigating the lethal machinery. And one of them has to be outside it — your stonemason, your tanner's boy, the person the machinery chews up. Who's the third?"

"The institution," I said.

They both looked at me.

"Not a person. A document. A chronicle, a ledger, an official record. The monastery's account of itself, written in the third person by no one in particular, claiming the authority of objectivity. The institutional voice. And it lies — not through commission but through omission. It records the execution but not the scream. It records the acquisition of land but not the eviction. And the reader holds this document alongside the two human accounts and has to triangulate the truth from three unreliable sources."

Nguyen put his coffee down. The carafe was empty. He'd drunk the entire thing, which I took as either an extraordinary act of will or evidence that he'd stopped tasting it three cups ago.

"That's interesting," he said. "The institutional narrator. Because that's what history is — the institutional narrator. The chronicle. The official record. And every postcolonial writer knows that the official record is a weapon. It doesn't describe reality. It produces reality. The monastery says the land was granted by the king, and therefore it was. Never mind that someone was farming it first. The document replaces the fact."

"But the document is also beautiful," Follett said, and his voice had shifted into something warmer, more personal. "I've spent years in archives. I've held charters written on vellum in the twelfth century, and they are gorgeous objects — the script, the seals, the weight of the skin in your hand. The Domesday Book is an act of total surveillance, William the Conqueror cataloguing every pig and plough in England, and it is also one of the most remarkable documents in human history. I can hold both of those things without one canceling the other."

"You can hold them because you're the inheritor," Nguyen said. "You're the one whose ancestors did the cataloguing, not the ones who were catalogued. When I look at the French colonial archives in Saigon — beautiful penmanship, beautiful classification, every village indexed and assessed — I don't see a remarkable document. I see the bureaucratic face of my family's displacement. The beauty doesn't cancel the violence. The beauty is the violence in its Sunday clothes."

Follett didn't argue. He nodded in a way that wasn't agreement but wasn't refusal either — the nod of a man adding something to a column he's been running in his head.

"Then the institutional narrator in this story can't be neutral," I said. "It can't be a transparent window onto the facts. It has to be — what? Self-serving? Delusional? Carefully edited?"

"Confident," Nguyen said. "That's worse than any of those. Make it confident. The chronicle that doesn't know it's lying because it has never considered the possibility that its categories might be wrong. The monastery records that Brother Anselm died peacefully when in fact he was strangled. But the chronicle doesn't say he was strangled — not because it's covering up the murder, but because within its taxonomy, a monk who dies in his cell during the night died peacefully. The violence isn't visible to the system. The system has no category for it."

"The system has no category for it," I repeated. That was the line. That was the thing I'd come to this room to find, and I hadn't known it until Nguyen said it.

Follett was writing in his notebook now — quick, angled marks, a shorthand I couldn't read. "The reader needs an entry point. One narrator who wants something they can track. Call it my weakness, call it populist instinct, I don't care — the reader needs a body moving through space toward or away from something. A goal that's legible even if the character isn't. Otherwise you've written a thought experiment, and thought experiments don't survive bedside tables."

"The outsider," I said. "Someone from outside the monastery, outside the court. Someone who arrives. Not English — or not quite English. Someone who carries a different set of assumptions into the stone corridors and sees what the insiders have stopped seeing."

"A refugee," Nguyen said.

"Can you have a refugee in fifteenth-century England?"

"You can have a refugee in any century. People have been fleeing since people existed. A Flemish weaver displaced by trade wars. A Breton monk transferred after his house was dissolved. A converted Jew from Spain who has anglicized his name and hopes no one asks questions. The medieval world was not a closed system. It was full of displaced people, and the displaced person sees the architecture differently — not as tradition but as theater. As a performance of permanence by a power that could collapse tomorrow."

Follett tapped his pen against the page. "A Flemish weaver. I like that. Someone who works with their hands, who understands material, who sees the monastery not as a house of God but as a building — stone and mortar and labor, someone's labor, probably coerced. And this weaver keeps a different kind of record. Not a chronicle. A pattern. A textile record. Information woven into cloth the way — actually, this is historically attested. Weavers did encode information in patterns. Trade secrets, certainly. But also —"

"Also the things they couldn't say aloud," Nguyen finished. "The refugee's archive. Not written in the colonizer's script but woven into the colonizer's fabric. You sell the cloth to the court, and the court wraps itself in a language it can't read."

I felt something click — not a resolution, not a plan, but a structural possibility. Three narrators: the court insider who hides within his own usefulness, the institutional chronicle that lies through confidence, and the outsider who weaves a counter-record into the material the powerful wear on their backs. Three accounts of the same events. None of them complete. None of them honest. The reader holding all three and finding the truth in the gaps between them.

"The insider," Follett said. "The Cromwell figure. What does this person want?"

"To not be killed," Nguyen said. "That's sufficient. You don't need a grander motive. In a court where people lose their heads for miscalculating a conversation, the desire to keep your head attached is enough to sustain seven thousand words."

"It's not enough," Follett said. "Survival alone is passive. There has to be something they're protecting beyond themselves. A person, a secret, a debt. Something that makes survival costly — something that the act of self-preservation endangers."

This was the tension I couldn't resolve, and I didn't try. Nguyen's insistence that survival was sufficient — that in conditions of extreme power, the desire to continue existing was a story complete in itself. Follett's counter-insistence that survival needed something to push against, a competing loyalty that gave the survival its dramatic weight. Both of them were drawing from their own work: Nguyen from the refugees who survived by becoming invisible, Follett from the builders and merchants who survived by building something larger than themselves.

"I think the three narrators will answer this differently," I said. "The insider believes in something beyond survival. The chronicle can't imagine anything beyond the institution. And the weaver — the outsider — the weaver doesn't believe in survival at all. The weaver believes in the record. In getting the pattern right. In leaving something encoded in the fabric that will outlast the monastery, the court, the whole bloody dynasty."

"And will it?" Nguyen asked.

"Will what?"

"Will it outlast?"

I didn't know. Outside, the cathedral spire still cut into the sky, nine hundred years of stone standing over everything we'd said. The fire had burned down to ash. Follett closed his notebook. Nguyen looked at the empty carafe as though considering whether to attempt the dregs.

"The stones know whose blood they're mixed with," Nguyen said. "That's something my grandmother used to say about temples. The French built their administrative buildings over Vietnamese graves, and the graves remember even when the building doesn't. The stones know. The question is whether anyone alive still reads them."

"In my experience," Follett said, "someone always reads them. Maybe not in the right century. But someone always comes back to the wall and sees the mark and asks what it means. That's why I write novels. To be the person who comes back to the wall."

"And I write novels," Nguyen said, "to be the mark."

The fire was out. Neither of them moved to relight it.
