We met in a model home. That was Carter's idea — she'd read about a new development on the edge of a county that had once been all orchards, and she wanted us to sit in one of the showpieces, the kind staged with furniture that no one has ever sat on, books that no one has ever read, fruit in a bowl that has never ripened because it was never alive. Du Maurier had not wanted to come. She had written back to say she'd meet us at a restaurant, or in a proper house, someone's house, a place with history in its floorboards. Carter said that was precisely the problem.

"You want a house that's already done the work for you," Carter said. She was standing in the kitchen of the model home, running a painted fingernail along the edge of the granite countertop. The granite was called Venetian Gold. There was a brochure on the breakfast bar that told you this. "You want damp rising through the walls and an attic with a locked door and three generations of someone else's grief soaked into the carpets. And that's fine, Daphne, that works, it works extraordinarily well. But I want to know what happens when there's no history at all. When the horror has to come from somewhere else entirely."

Du Maurier was sitting on a chair in the open-plan living room, very straight, the way she sat in every room, as if posture could protect her from whatever the room intended. She'd taken off her gloves and folded them in her lap. "I don't write about houses with history. I write about houses with appetites. There's a difference."

"Explain the difference."

"A house with history stores what's happened in it. Manderley doesn't do that. Manderley wants things. It acts on the people inside it. The house at the end of 'Don't Look Now' — Venice itself — Venice wants something from John and Laura. The building is not a container. It's a mouth."

I said I'd been thinking about that distinction — the passive space versus the active one — and whether suburban architecture could have that same quality of hunger. My concern was that these houses were too new, too planned, too exposed. Gothic dread needs shadows and there are no shadows in a cul-de-sac at two in the afternoon.

Carter laughed. It was not a reassuring sound. "That's the most boring possible objection. Shadows. Gloom. You're talking about set dressing. The wolf doesn't need a forest."

"The wolf does need the forest," du Maurier said quietly. "The forest is what makes the wolf a wolf. An animal in the open is just an animal. The containment is what transforms it."

"Absolutely not. The wolf is the wolf regardless of where you put it. The forest is the girl's idea about what danger looks like. When the wolf shows up in grandmother's bed, wearing grandmother's nightgown, the entire point is that the domestic space has failed to exclude what was supposed to stay in the forest. The girl was told the world had boundaries. It does not."

They were both right, which was the problem. I said so, and Carter looked at me with what I can only describe as amused contempt.

"Don't do that. Don't try to synthesize us into something digestible. If we agreed, there'd be no reason to write the story."

I backed off. I asked instead about the specific texture of suburban dread — what it smells like, sounds like, what it does to the body.

Du Maurier spoke first. "It smells like other people's cooking when you can't see the kitchen. It sounds like a lawnmower running on a day when no one is cutting the grass. It is the feeling of being watched from a window you can't identify."

"It smells like air freshener," Carter said. "It smells like something designed to replace a smell. That's the suburban uncanny — everything is a substitute for something else. The lawn is a substitute for the meadow. The cul-de-sac is a substitute for the village. The neighbor is a substitute for the community. And the marriage — the domestic arrangement at the center of it — is a substitute for whatever the people inside it actually feel."

"That's reductive."

"It's precise. I'm not saying every marriage is a fraud. I'm saying the suburban arrangement puts enormous pressure on the marriage to be the thing it represents. The couple must perform the couple. The family must perform the family. And when the performance cracks, what comes through is not the failure of love — it's the exposure of the performance itself. The revelation that there was always something underneath, organizing everything, that had nothing to do with love."

I asked what that something was.

"Appetite," Carter said. "Hunger. The body. Everything the development was built to contain."

Du Maurier made a small sound. Agreement or dissent — I couldn't tell. "In 'Don't Look Now,' John sees the small figure in the red coat and he believes it's his dead daughter. His grief has made him permeable. He's rational, he insists on being rational, he explains away everything Laura and the sisters perceive — and yet he keeps seeing the red coat. His resistance is the story. The moment he stops resisting is the moment the story kills him."

"Yes," Carter said, and for once there was no edge. "Because the rational explanation is the costume the wolf is wearing. John's rationality is the grandmother's nightgown. He's telling himself a story about what he's seeing and the story is exactly wrong, but it fits so well that he keeps wearing it right up to the knife."

I said: what about withholding? If the story is going to operate on information the reader doesn't have — a structural absence, something the narrative can't or won't say —

"Won't," du Maurier said immediately. "The narrator must choose not to say it. Not because they're lying but because the thing itself has become invisible to them. The way you stop seeing your own furniture. The way you stop hearing the hum of the refrigerator. The most terrifying withholding is not deception. It's habituation."

"Or complicity," Carter said. "The narrator withholds because saying the thing aloud would require them to stand outside the arrangement and look at it, and looking at it from outside would destroy it. The withholding is structural because the narrator's survival depends on the structure remaining intact."

"Those are the same thing," I said.

"They are not remotely the same thing," said Carter. "Habituation is unconscious. Complicity is a choice that has been made so many times it no longer feels like one. The difference is enormous. In one version the narrator is a victim. In the other the narrator is a participant. I insist on the latter."

"You would," du Maurier said, and something passed between them that I wasn't fully part of.

I brought up the domestic body — Carter's work on flesh, transformation, the physical fact of being an animal in a human house. How the suburban home is designed to manage the body: where it sleeps, where it eats, where it eliminates, where it is permitted to desire. The floor plan as a diagram of acceptable embodiment.

Carter sat down for the first time. She sat on the countertop, which would have horrified the real estate agent. "The floor plan. Yes. The master bedroom and the children's bedrooms on separate floors. The bathroom with a lock. The kitchen with its implied labor — who cooks, who serves, who cleans. The garage where the man goes to be alone with his machines. Every room is an instruction. And the garden — the garden is the last perimeter. Beyond it, the world."

"In my experience," du Maurier said, "the garden is where things go wrong first. Before the house is compromised, the garden is. Something growing that shouldn't be. Something not growing that always has. The change begins at the edge and moves inward."

"My stories work the other direction. The change begins in the body and moves outward until the house can no longer contain it. The girl in 'The Company of Wolves' doesn't become a wolf because the forest encroaches — she becomes a wolf because she was always a wolf, and the cottage, the grandmother, the red cloak were the containment that finally failed."

I was starting to see the shape of something. A house, new, without history. A couple. Something withheld — not a secret so much as a condition, something so thoroughly integrated into the domestic arrangement that the narrative can present it without ever identifying it. The reader senses something is wrong but can't locate it, because the narrator has organized their world around the wrongness so completely that it has become the foundation.

"And children," I said. "What about children in this story?"

The silence lasted long enough that I regretted saying it. Du Maurier set her gloves on the arm of the chair and folded her hands over them. "Children change the architecture of a story the way they change the architecture of a house. They make the stakes absolute. In 'Don't Look Now,' the dead child is the engine — everything flows from Christine's drowning, everything John sees or refuses to see is organized around that loss. The child doesn't need to be present. The child needs to have been present."

"I don't agree," Carter said. "Children in gothic fiction are too often used as moral weight. Put a child in the story and you've told the reader what to feel. The dread becomes about protection rather than about knowledge. I'm more interested in a story where the domestic arrangement itself is the thing at risk — not any person within it, but the shape of it. The pattern."

"Can you have a domestic story without children?"

"You can have a domestic story about the absence of children. Or about the space where children would go. An empty room with a specific intended purpose — that's more frightening than a child in danger."

I thought about that. A room in a new house. A room that has never been used for anything. A room that the couple has agreed, without quite saying so, is for something that has not arrived. Or will not arrive. Or arrived once and —

"Don't finish that thought out loud," du Maurier said. "Keep it. That's the thing the story won't say."

"Don't tell me the shape," Carter said. "I'll smell the outline and that's worse than surprise. Write it so that I'm uncomfortable on page one and only understand why on the last page. And then make the understanding incomplete. I want to finish the story and realize I still don't know what I don't know."

"That's asking a great deal of the reader."

"It's asking exactly what a reader comes to fiction for. The experience of being disturbed in a way they can't immediately process. If they can process it while they're reading it, you've given them a problem. If they process it three days later while they're washing dishes, you've given them a haunting."

Du Maurier stood. She picked up her gloves. "The couple must love each other. That's my condition. Whatever else is true, whatever is being withheld, whatever is growing in the garden or failing to grow, the love must be real. Not performed, not a delusion — actual love, operating within and around the thing that makes the story terrible. Because love does that. Love operates within terrible arrangements without being diminished by them, and that is the most frightening thing I know."

Carter didn't respond to this directly. She hopped off the counter and walked to the window above the kitchen sink, which looked out onto a yard that had been landscaped but not yet lived in. Fresh sod, a patio with no furniture on it, a fence with no weather on it.

"Write the fence," she said. "Write what it's keeping out. And then make the reader realize it isn't keeping anything out at all. The fence is facing the wrong direction."

I asked what she meant.

"You'll figure it out," she said, and we left the model home. Du Maurier went first. She did not look back at it, which I understood later was its own kind of answer — the refusal to give the house the satisfaction of a second glance.

The development had a name. Orchard Glen. There were no orchards.
