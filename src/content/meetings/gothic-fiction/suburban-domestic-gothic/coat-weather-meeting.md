We met in a house that didn't exist yet — or rather, in the idea of a house, a floor plan pinned to corkboard, walls suggested by tape on subflooring. Angela had arrived first. She was sitting cross-legged on the plywood where the living room would be, smoking, flicking ash into a coffee can she'd found among the construction debris. Daphne stood at what would eventually be the kitchen window, looking out at the tree line forty yards away, where the bulldozers had stopped.

"They always stop," Daphne said, without turning. "You can see it in the stumps. They cut to a line someone drew on a survey map and then they simply — stopped. As though the forest had negotiated a border."

"The forest didn't negotiate anything." Angela tapped her cigarette. "Someone's insurance policy drew that line. But you're right that it looks like a treaty. The interesting question is what the forest thinks the terms are."

I said I wanted to write about a woman who moves into one of these developments. Recently widowed. A daughter. The tree line visible from every back window.

"Of course a widow," Angela said, not unkindly but with the particular emphasis she gave to words she intended to take apart. "The gothic loves its widows. It puts them in black and sends them to large houses and then haunts them for four hundred pages. But why must she be haunted? Why can't she be the one doing the haunting?"

"Because she's grieving," Daphne said. She turned from the window. In the construction light her face had the quality of old ivory — composed, a little worn at the edges. "Grief is a kind of haunting that begins in the body. You don't need a ghost. You need a woman who keeps seeing what isn't there and who cannot stop herself from looking. That's the engine. Not the supernatural. The eye that won't close."

Angela conceded the point but only partway. "Grief is one engine. But what you're describing, Daphne — the eye that won't close — that's also desire. The widow keeps looking because she wants something. You'll say she wants her husband back, and perhaps she does, but she also wants to be the kind of person who feels things at that pitch. Grief can become a performance of one's own depth. I don't mean she's faking. I mean grief has an architecture, and some people furnish it."

"That's cruel," Daphne said.

"It's honest. I'm not interested in being gentle with characters. Gentleness is the reader's job. Ours is to see clearly."

I asked about the red coat. The daughter's red raincoat, seen at the tree line when the daughter is elsewhere. Was this too obvious a reference?

"It's a signal, not a reference," Daphne said. She sat down on an overturned joint compound bucket, very straight-backed, the way she always sat — as though the chair might at any moment be pulled away. "In my story, the red was a misreading. The husband sees the color and his mind completes the picture: child, danger, rescue. The color is accurate. Everything his mind builds around it is wrong. That's how grief works. It gives you one true thing and wraps it in fabrication so thorough you can't find the border."

"The border between what's true and what the mind has built." I wrote that down. "That's the ecotone."

"The what?"

"Ecology term. The border where two habitats meet. Forest edge, shoreline, the line where prairie becomes woodland. It's not a clean division. It's a zone of overlap where species from both sides coexist, often in strange combinations. Edge species. Opportunists. Things that don't belong fully to either world."

Angela leaned forward. I could see I'd handed her something. "That's the suburban gothic right there. The development is one habitat. The forest is another. And the back yard — that strip of mown grass between the patio and the trees — is the ecotone. That's where your widow keeps seeing the figure. Not in the forest. Not in the house. In the zone between."

"Yes," Daphne said, "but the point isn't the ecology. The point is that she can't stop looking because the zone is where her grief lives. It's neither the life she had nor the life she's trying to build. It's the gap between them. Of course she sees a child there. The gap is child-shaped."

I said I wanted the story to withhold something fundamental. The identity of the figure. What it actually is. Whether it's a ghost, a delusion, a real child, something from the forest that has learned to wear a coat.

"Don't explain it," Daphne said immediately, with such force that Angela looked at her with new interest. "In my story the reveal at the end was — well, the reveal was the point, but it was also the least interesting moment. The most interesting moments were the ones where the husband was following the figure and didn't know what it was. The uncertainty was the horror. Not the answer. The answer was just a mechanism."

"I disagree," Angela said. "The answer in your story was important precisely because it was wrong — not wrong as in inaccurate, but wrong as in obscene. The child was not a child. The thing wearing the child's shape was something else entirely. That wrongness is what I want to feel in this story. But I don't think you need to name it."

"You want the wrongness without the reveal."

"I want the wrongness to be the whole story. Not a punchline. A condition."

Daphne considered this. "Then the story has to do the work of making the absence felt. Every scene has to lean against the hole where the explanation should be. The reader has to feel the shape of what's missing."

"The reader will fill it in themselves," Angela said. "They always do. And what they fill it in with will be worse than anything we could provide. Because it will come from their own particular darkness."

I raised the question of the daughter. Lily. Eight years old. What if Lily has seen the figure too? What if Lily has been playing with it?

The silence that followed had a specific weight. Daphne looked at her hands. Angela stubbed out her cigarette slowly, deliberately, and then said: "Now we're in the real story."

"Lily can't be a victim," Angela continued. "I refuse it. Every fairy tale puts the child at the mercy of the wolf. The child is eaten, rescued, warned, taught a lesson. But in my telling, the girl walks into the forest because she wants to. The girl chooses the wolf. She knows what it is and she chooses it anyway, and that choice is what makes her powerful."

"Lily is eight," Daphne said quietly.

"Lily is eight and she is already making choices her mother can't understand. That's what frightens the mother more than the figure. Not that something in the woods might take her daughter, but that her daughter might go willingly."

I felt the story crack open. The widowed mother's grief and the daughter's strange autonomy. The red coat appearing where it shouldn't. The neighbors who know something they won't say. And underneath it all, the withheld explanation — the figure's identity, the previous family's departure, what happened in the house before Nora moved in — never provided. The reader presses against the silence and the silence holds.

"What about the path?" I asked. "The children are told not to take the path into the woods."

"Every fairy tale has a path," Angela said. "The path is the story's spine. Red Riding Hood's path through the forest, the breadcrumb trail, the yellow brick road. The path exists to be followed despite the warning. And the warning exists to make the following meaningful."

"In my work," Daphne said, "the paths were Venice's streets. Labyrinthine. The husband kept seeing the figure ahead of him, always turning a corner, always about to be caught. He followed because grief had made him stupid and brave and wrong. He followed because the alternative was to stand still and feel everything."

"And what does your widow do?" Angela asked me directly.

"She follows."

"Good. And does she find what she's looking for?"

"She finds her daughter on the path. Wearing the red coat. Following the figure deeper into the trees."

Angela nodded slowly. "The mother follows the daughter who follows the thing in the woods. Three women on the path. Grandmother, mother, child. That's my story, stripped to the spine."

"Except we don't know what's at the end of the path," Daphne said.

"We never do. That's the whole point of paths."

I asked about the neighbor, Diane. The woman who knows the history of the house, the previous family, the development's relationship with the forest. She warns Nora but the warning is partial, oblique, maybe deliberately unhelpful.

"The psychic sisters in Venice," Daphne said. "The warning that was accurate but misunderstood. The information that comes too late or in the wrong shape. Diane is that figure. She tells Nora what she needs to know but Nora can't hear it because grief has stuffed her ears with cotton."

"Or," Angela said, "Diane doesn't tell Nora everything because Diane is also protecting herself. Diane knows what's in the woods and she's made her peace with it. She's the grandmother who survived the wolf by learning to live alongside it. She keeps her curtains drawn on the forest side. She never goes past the tree line. She has terms."

"Terms with what?"

"With whatever it is. And that's what you never explain."

This was the moment the story locked into place for me. The figure in the red coat. The path into the forest. The mother's grief, the daughter's fearlessness, the neighbor's knowing silence. And the vast, pressurized absence at the center — the explanation that never comes, that the reader can feel pressing against the walls of every paragraph.

"One more thing," Angela said. She had lit another cigarette, though I hadn't seen her do it. "The grandmother's bones."

"What about them?"

"In my story, after the wolf eats the grandmother, the girl finds the bones under the bed. But she doesn't mourn. She doesn't avenge. She lies down with the wolf and the bones are still there, underneath, clattering. What I want to know is: what are the bones in this story? What has already been consumed that the protagonist must lie down next to?"

Daphne said, very quietly: "Her marriage. Not her husband — her marriage. The shape of the life she had. It's been eaten and the bones are still in the house. The furniture arrangement for two. The king bed. The second toothbrush holder. The bones are everywhere and she's lying down among them every night."

"Yes," Angela said. "And the figure in the red coat is not the thing that consumed them. The figure is what came after. What moves into the cleared space."

"Like the knotweed," I said. "Japanese knotweed. It grows into every cleared space. Through concrete, through foundations. You can't kill the roots."

"Don't make it a metaphor," Angela said sharply. "Make it a real thing growing through the fence posts at the tree line. Make it ugly and physical. The gothic isn't metaphor. The gothic is the metaphor come alive and standing in your back yard."

Daphne smiled at that — one of her rare smiles, more unsettling than comforting. "She's right. Don't be clever about it. Be accurate. The knotweed is real, the path is real, the figure is — well. The figure is the question. Keep it as a question."

I promised to keep it as a question. I promised to write the ecotone, the edge habitat where neither the domestic nor the wild fully holds. I promised to let the red coat be a signal without being a symbol. I promised that the story would press against its own silence until the silence became a presence, a shape, a figure at the tree line that the reader would see long after the last page.

"And the ending?" Daphne asked.

"She walks toward it."

"Toward what?"

"Toward the space where the answer should be."

Angela blew smoke at the exposed ceiling joists. "Good. Don't fill it in. Let the reader stand at the tree line forever."

We sat for a while in the house that wasn't finished yet, looking out the window that didn't have glass, at the tree line where the bulldozers had stopped and the forest began its slow, patient return.
