The cafe was in Wroclaw, on a street where the tram lines split and you could hear the cables humming when the wind was right. Tokarczuk had chosen it because the owner kept bees on the roof and sold their honey at the counter, and she said she wanted us to be in a place where something was being produced overhead while we talked. Camus had arrived twenty minutes early and was sitting near the window with a small black coffee that he had not yet touched. He was reading a Polish newspaper he could not read, studying the photographs with the attention of someone who believes images are more honest than captions.

I brought my notebook, which was a mistake. Every time I opened it, Tokarczuk glanced at it with the expression of a woman watching someone bring a fishing rod to a swimming pool.

"Put that away," she said, not unkindly, when I began to write down something Camus had said about quarantines. "If you cannot remember what matters, writing it down will not help."

"She's right," Camus said. "The notebook is a crutch against forgetting, but forgetting is where the interesting work happens. You remember what your body found important, not what your hand transcribed."

I put the notebook in my bag. I left it open, because closing it felt like a concession I was not ready to make.

"So," Tokarczuk said. She stirred her tea, which had something floating in it — dried chamomile, or possibly lavender, I could not tell. "We are writing about a woman who is wrong."

"A person who is wrong," Camus said. "Must it be a woman?"

"In my experience," Tokarczuk said, "when a woman is wrong, the wrongness is more interesting because people are less prepared for it. A wrong man is a tragedy or a comedy. A wrong woman is a disturbance. She disrupts the order of things because women are supposed to be — what is the word — attuned. Intuitive. When a woman is passionately, structurally wrong, the people around her do not know where to place their discomfort."

"That is why Janina works," I said, meaning the narrator of *Drive Your Plow*. "Because she is so confident. She reads the stars and quotes Blake and the reader is charmed, and then gradually you realize she may have killed people, and the charm does not go away, which is the uncomfortable part."

"Janina is not wrong," Tokarczuk said. "She is excessive. There is a difference."

"Isn't excess a form of wrongness?"

"No. Excess is a form of commitment. Wrongness implies that there is a correct position from which the person has deviated. Janina does not deviate. She is on her own path entirely. The question is whether the path leads somewhere livable."

"Every path leads somewhere livable if you are committed enough," Camus said. He said this without irony, which made it worse. "The question is whether the people around you survive the journey."

Tokarczuk turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "I think you and I disagree about what constitutes moral seriousness, Albert. You believe it lives in clarity — the clear-eyed assessment of an absurd world. Rieux's heroism is that he sees the plague for what it is and fights it anyway. I believe moral seriousness can also live in confusion. In the wrong answer arrived at with total sincerity."

"Sincerity is not a moral category."

"Of course it is. A sincere person who acts wrongly is a tragedy. An insincere person who acts correctly is a bureaucrat. The interior state matters."

"The dead do not care about the interior state of the person who failed to save them."

I thought this would provoke her, but instead she laughed — a short, percussive sound that turned a few heads at nearby tables. "Now you sound like yourself. Good. I was worried you were going to be agreeable."

Camus almost smiled. "I am never agreeable. I am sometimes patient, which people confuse for the same thing."

I had been thinking about form. "If we are writing a story about someone who is wrong, the story itself has to be wrong somehow. Or at least off-center. The narrator — whoever tells this — has to be inside the protagonist's logic. We cannot write from above, looking down at her mistakes."

"Obviously not," Tokarczuk said.

"But we also cannot write from entirely inside," Camus said. "If the reader is completely immersed in her worldview, with no friction, no counter-evidence, then the story is just — propaganda for a wrong idea. There must be a gap. Small gaps. Places where the reader can see what the protagonist cannot."

"Like windows," I said.

"Like windows, yes. But not picture windows. Narrow ones. The reader glimpses the truth the way you glimpse a courtyard through a slit in a wall — enough to know it is there, not enough to see it whole."

Camus set down his coffee cup, which was still full. "This is exactly the distinction I want to explore. In *The Plague*, Rieux is not excessive. He is stubborn. He does the same thing every day — treats the sick, records the dead, refuses to stop — and this stubbornness is presented as a kind of heroism. But suppose he were wrong. Suppose his stubbornness were aimed at the wrong target. Suppose he were so committed to his diagnosis that he could not see the actual disease."

"Then he would be dangerous," I said.

"He would be *human*," Camus said. "The most dangerous thing about conviction is that it feels the same whether you are right or wrong. The interior experience — the heat, the urgency, the sense of moral clarity — is identical. You cannot distinguish between justified action and catastrophic error from the inside."

Tokarczuk was nodding, but in the way that meant she was about to disagree. "You are describing epistemology. I am interested in something different. I am interested in the wrongness that comes from loving something too much. From seeing the world through the lens of one obsession until the obsession becomes the world."

"Like astrology," I said.

"Like astrology, yes. Or like environmentalism. Or like any framework that explains everything. When a system explains everything, it explains nothing, but the person inside the system cannot see the borders because the system IS the borders. They think they are looking at the world when they are looking at the frame."

The beekeeper came to our table with a small dish of honey and three wooden tasting spoons. Tokarczuk thanked him in Polish, and they spoke for a moment about the bees — something about the winter, about losses. Camus watched this exchange with the focused patience of a man who has spent his life in countries where he did not always speak the language and had learned to read tone as text.

"What did he say?" Camus asked when the beekeeper left.

"He lost two hives this winter. He does not know why. They were healthy in autumn and then in spring they were empty. Not dead — empty. The bees simply left."

"Colony collapse," I said.

"Yes. But he does not like that term. He says it describes the result, not the cause. He says the bees did not collapse. They departed. Something in the hive or in the air told them to go, and they went. He thinks it is the electromagnetic radiation from the tram lines."

Camus smiled, which for him was a small rearrangement of the mouth that could mean amusement, recognition, or sadness, and in this case seemed to mean all three. "There is our protagonist."

"The beekeeper?"

"No. The beekeeper's certainty. A person in a town where something is going wrong — something invisible, something that empties houses and sickens people — and this person has a theory. A beautiful theory. An elegant theory that connects everything: the tram lines, the electromagnetic fields, the patterns of illness, the timing of deaths. A theory that is almost certainly wrong. And because the theory is beautiful, and because the person is passionate, and because passion is indistinguishable from authority, people begin to believe it. Or at least they stop arguing."

"Yes," Tokarczuk said, and her voice had changed — it had the quality of someone recognizing something she had been thinking about for a long time but had not yet heard spoken. "And the wrongness is not stupidity. The wrongness is a kind of poetry. The connections are real — they are just not causal. The tram lines do hum. The bees did leave. People are getting sick. Everything the protagonist says is true except the thing that connects it all."

I could see the story now, or the shape of it. "So the town is under some kind of siege — not a plague exactly, but something. An illness, a contamination, a collapse. And our protagonist — "

"Our protagonist does what Rieux does," Camus said. "She works. She goes door to door. She takes notes, she makes maps, she records patterns. She is doing exactly what a hero does. The form of her response is heroic. But the content is wrong."

"And she reads Blake," Tokarczuk said.

"Why Blake?" Camus asked.

"Because Blake saw everything as connected. The grain of sand and the universe. It is the most beautiful and the most dangerous idea — that everything is one system, that every detail matters, that nothing is random. Blake made it poetry. Our protagonist makes it a theory of contamination."

"She maps the town," I said. "She correlates illness with — what? With proximity to something. With the direction the wind blows. With the alignment of certain buildings or roads or — "

"With the air," Tokarczuk said. "She believes the air is poisoned. Not in the ordinary way — not carbon monoxide, not industrial emissions. Something subtler. She believes the air carries something that cannot be measured with instruments, that can only be detected by paying attention. By being attuned to the patterns."

"Miasma," Camus said quietly.

The word sat between us. I could feel Tokarczuk's excitement, the way she leaned forward, and Camus's stillness, which was his form of excitement.

"A modern miasma theory," I said. "She reinvents the oldest wrong idea in medicine — that disease comes from bad air — and she reinvents it beautifully. With data. With correlations. With Blake."

"And the town believes her because the alternative is to believe nothing," Camus said. "This is the logic of the absurd. The human need for explanation is so powerful that a wrong explanation is preferable to no explanation. Rieux fights the plague because the alternative is to do nothing, and doing nothing is unacceptable. Our protagonist fights the bad air because the alternative is to admit that no one knows what is happening, and no one can know, and the dying will continue regardless."

"But the dying continues anyway," Tokarczuk said. "Even while she works. Even while she maps and correlates and quotes Blake. The children still get sick. The wells are still contaminated. And she cannot see that her beautiful theory is the thing standing between the town and the actual answer, because her theory HAS become the town's way of understanding itself."

"What is the actual answer?" I asked.

Camus shrugged. "It does not matter."

"It has to matter," I said. "The reader needs to know — "

"The reader needs to feel the gap between the protagonist's certainty and the reality. Whether the reality is groundwater contamination or industrial waste or something banal — the point is not what the answer is. The point is that the protagonist's heroism prevents the question from being asked correctly."

"I disagree," Tokarczuk said. "The reader should know. The answer should be specific, and it should be ugly, and it should be the kind of answer that a person who reads Blake and watches the stars would never think to look for. Something in the ground. Something in the water. Something that has been there for years, leaching slowly, that nobody tested for because nobody thought to test."

"An aquifer," Camus said. "Contaminated. Agricultural runoff, perhaps. Or an old factory. Something with history."

"Yes. Something with decades of history. Something that accumulated in the soil while everyone was looking at the sky." Tokarczuk took one of the honey spoons and held it up to the light. "And our protagonist — she tested the air. She mapped the winds. She did everything right except look down."

I tried to push on the structure. "What does the town look like? Where is this?"

"Central Europe," Tokarczuk said immediately. "A valley. A place where the mountains hold the air in. This is important — the geography has to support her theory. If she believes the air is trapped, stagnant, carrying poisons, the landscape has to make that plausible. A valley where the fog sits for days. Where you can smell the river in every room."

"Oran was a place no one would choose to die in," Camus said. "That was the point. The ugliness of the city was part of the story — it was not a place that deserved tragedy, it was a place that deserved only its own ordinariness. I think our town should be similar. Not picturesque. Not a place with a tragic history. A place where people live because their parents lived there, and where the only remarkable thing is that something remarkable is now happening."

"And she is a retired teacher," Tokarczuk said. "Or no — a retired engineer. Something precise. A woman who worked in a field that trained her to see systems, and who, in retirement, has turned that systems-thinking toward the town itself. She knows how pipes work. She knows about pressure and flow. She applies this knowledge to the air."

"She is dangerous because she is competent," Camus said.

"She is dangerous because she is *almost* competent. She knows enough to build a convincing theory. She does not know enough to see the theory's limits."

I thought about the bees upstairs, and about the beekeeper's certainty. "Are there animals in this story? There should be animals."

"There must be animals," Tokarczuk said. "Dead animals. Animals that die before the people get sick. The canaries in the mine, except she misreads what killed them."

"Bees," I said. "Or birds. Something that falls out of the air."

"Both," Tokarczuk said. "Both."

There was a long silence. The tram passed outside and the cables hummed. Camus finally drank his coffee, which must have been cold by then.

"I want to be careful," he said, "that this is not a story about someone being stupid. The wrongness must be — "

"Noble," I said.

"No. Not noble. *Coherent*. It must make sense within her worldview. If you lived in her mind, you would reach the same conclusions. The wrongness is not a failure of intelligence. It is a failure of — what would you call it? Frame."

"Paradigm," Tokarczuk offered.

"Frame," Camus said again, rejecting the synonym. "A paradigm is intellectual. A frame is perceptual. She does not perceive the ground because she is looking at the air. It is not that she is wrong about the air — the air is real, the air carries things. It is that the air is not where the answer lives."

"And she never realizes this," Tokarczuk said. "I want to be clear about that. She does not have an epiphany. She does not look at the soil samples and think, my God, I have been wrong. Someone else finds the answer — the water authority, a county health inspector, someone banal. And our protagonist incorporates it into her theory. She says: yes, the water was contaminated, but the contamination was carried by the air, was distributed by the wind patterns, was predicted by the charts. She is not lying. She genuinely believes this. The wrongness is not correctable because it is not experienced as wrongness."

"That is the cruelest ending," I said.

"That is the honest ending," Camus said. "In Oran, the plague ends. Not because the doctors cure it, but because plagues end. And Rieux goes on being a doctor. And the rats are still in the city, waiting. The question my book asks is: what do you do when you know the plague will return? Our story asks the adjacent question: what do you do when the person fighting hardest is fighting the wrong thing, and they will never stop, and they will never know?"

"And you cannot tell them," Tokarczuk said, "because their fighting is what holds the town together. Without her theory, the town has nothing. No explanation. No structure. No hero. The wrongness is load-bearing."

I wanted to write that down — *the wrongness is load-bearing* — but my notebook was in my bag and Tokarczuk was watching me, so I pressed the words into memory and hoped my body would find them important enough to keep.

"There is something else," Camus said. He had been quiet for several minutes, which with him always preceded the thing he most wanted to say. "The chronicle. In *The Plague*, the entire novel is structured as a chronicle — a document of witness. Rieux writes it because someone must record what happened, even if the recording changes nothing. I think our story should have this quality. Not literally a chronicle, but the sense that someone is keeping a record. Perhaps the protagonist herself — her maps, her air-quality logs, her Blake quotations in the margins. The record is the artifact of her wrongness. It is beautiful and it is useless and it is the only thing that will survive the crisis."

"A museum of wrong conclusions," Tokarczuk said.

"A chronicle of sincere effort. Which may be the same thing."

I asked: "Who tells the story? If not her, then who?"

Camus and Tokarczuk looked at each other across the table. It was the first time during the meeting that they seemed to be having a conversation I was not part of.

"Someone who sees her clearly and loves her anyway," Tokarczuk said.

"Someone who sees her clearly and cannot help her," Camus said.

"Those might be the same person," I said.

Neither of them confirmed this. The beekeeper returned to collect the honey dish, and Tokarczuk asked him something else in Polish — a longer question this time. He answered at length, gesturing toward the ceiling, and then toward the street. When he left, she did not translate. Camus did not ask her to.

"I think," Tokarczuk said, "that the hardest thing about this story will be the prose. The prose has to love her. The sentences have to be on her side even as the evidence turns against her. If the prose turns cold or ironic, the story becomes a satire. If the prose stays warm, it becomes something harder to name."

"Tragedy without recognition," Camus said.

"Exactly. The protagonist does not recognize her own tragedy. The reader does. And the prose is the space between those two recognitions — warm on her side, cold on the reader's."

The meeting should have ended there. That felt like an ending. But Tokarczuk ordered another tea, and Camus asked for water, and we sat for a while longer talking about valleys and fog and the way certain towns in certain landscapes develop a relationship with their own air that is almost superstitious, almost devotional, and I realized that the protagonist was already forming in the room, in the space between these two writers who agreed on almost nothing except that the story mattered and that the woman at its center deserved better than she would get.

Outside, a tram went past and the bees on the roof, if they were still there, hummed in frequencies we could not hear.
