We met in a railway station cafe that I had chosen precisely because it was wrong — too loud, too transient, full of people hauling luggage toward platforms for trains that were delayed or cancelled or possibly imaginary. The departures board kept rearranging itself. Tokarczuk sat near the window watching passengers with the detached intensity of someone cataloguing species. Kafka was already seated when I arrived, which should not have been possible because I had arrived first. He had a coffee he was not drinking and a newspaper folded to the crossword, which was blank.

"The problem with conspiracy," Tokarczuk said, before I had fully settled into my chair, "is that it assumes a center. Someone is pulling strings. The world has an author. But the interesting thing — the thing Pynchon understood, I think, though he drowned it in too much noise — is the moment when you realize the strings are pulling themselves. No one is in charge. The pattern is generating the pattern."

Kafka looked at her. He had a way of looking at people that made them feel they had just been entered into a ledger. "The pattern is generating the pattern," he repeated. "That is worse than a conspiracy. A conspiracy can be resisted. A pattern that generates itself can only be observed."

"Or joined," Tokarczuk said. "That is the third option, and it is the one no one wants to talk about. You can resist the pattern, you can observe the pattern, or you can become so much a part of the pattern that you can no longer distinguish between yourself and it. The wanderer who walks through a city documenting its strange connections — at what point does she become one of the connections? At what point is her map just another street?"

I opened my notebook. I had come with ideas — something about a person searching for a thing that might not exist, about waiting, about the comedy of sustained belief in the face of nothing — but Tokarczuk was already somewhere I had not anticipated, and I could feel my preparations becoming irrelevant, like a speech written for the wrong ceremony.

"I want to write about someone who is convinced they have discovered something," I said. "A system, a conspiracy, a hidden order — but the discovery is the delusion. The protagonist is wrong. Not partially wrong, not wrong in ways that lead to a deeper rightness. Fundamentally, architecturally wrong. And the question is whether the reader figures this out before the protagonist does, or after, or never."

Kafka set down his coffee cup with a precision that made the small sound seem like punctuation. "The protagonist is always wrong," he said. "That is what it means to be a protagonist. You believe you are the subject of the story. You believe the events are happening to you, for a reason, in a sequence that moves toward something. You believe in forward motion. This is the fundamental error. There is no forward motion. There is only the corridor, and the corridor does not lead anywhere, and the fact that you are walking down it does not mean it was built for walking."

"But people do walk down corridors," Tokarczuk said. "They walk and they arrive at places. Not always the right places. Not always the places they intended. But places. I reject the corridor that leads nowhere. My corridors open onto fields, and the fields have people in them, and the people are doing things that are connected to other things that are connected to other people in other fields. This is not optimism. This is geography."

"Your geography is comforting," Kafka said.

Tokarczuk's expression changed — not to anger, but to something harder, something closer to the look she got when critics called her work "gentle." "It is not comforting," she said. "It is overwhelming. The connections are not reassuring. The connections are terrifying, because they mean you are never alone, you are never free, every action ripples outward into a web you cannot see the edges of. That is not comfort. That is vertigo."

"Vertigo is not entrapment," Kafka said. "You can close your eyes during vertigo."

There was a silence. A train was announced overhead in three languages, none of which were the language of the country we appeared to be in. A woman dragging a suitcase with a broken wheel scraped past our table. The sound of the wheel on tile was the most concrete thing in the room.

"Let me try something," I said. "A woman — let me not give her a name yet — receives a letter. Or finds a symbol. Or overhears a phrase. Something small, ambiguous, that could mean nothing. But she decides it means something. She begins to follow the thread. She finds more evidence — not conclusive evidence, but the kind of evidence that accumulates, that builds a structure. She starts to see the pattern everywhere. In street addresses, in the timing of buses, in the way pigeons gather on certain ledges and not others. And the reader — at first the reader goes with her. The pattern seems plausible. The evidence mounts. But slowly the reader begins to suspect what the protagonist refuses to see: that she is building the pattern herself. That her attention is the loom. That the conspiracy is made of looking."

Kafka nodded. "This is Pynchon's question. Oedipa Maas. Is the Tristero real or is she inventing it? But Pynchon never answers. He leaves her — and the reader — suspended between the two possibilities. You are proposing something different. You are proposing that we answer. That we say: she is wrong."

"Yes."

"That is cruel," he said, and he sounded approving.

Tokarczuk was shaking her head slowly. "It is too simple. She is wrong — fine. But wrong about what? Wrong about the specific conspiracy? Wrong about the existence of hidden connections? Wrong about meaning itself? These are different levels of wrongness. A person can be wrong about the conspiracy and right about the connections. A person can be wrong about the connections and right that looking for them is the only sane response to a world that seems to have none. You cannot just say 'she is wrong' as if wrongness were one thing."

"Can't I?" I asked, and I meant it genuinely. "Can't the story work precisely because her wrongness is total? She is wrong about everything — the conspiracy, the connections, the meaning of her search. She is wrong about her own life. She is wrong about why she started looking. And the reader watches this wrongness accumulate like — "

"Like a corridor," Kafka said, and I could not tell whether he was helping me or trapping me.

"Like a constellation," Tokarczuk countered. "A constellation of errors. But here is the thing about constellations — and this is where I push back on both of you — a constellation is wrong. The stars are not connected. They are not even near each other. They are separated by distances that make the idea of a picture absurd. And yet. And yet we see the bear, the hunter, the crown. The wrongness of a constellation is also its beauty. If your protagonist builds a constellation of false connections, the question is not whether she is right. The question is whether what she builds is beautiful. Whether it sustains her. Whether the act of building it — wrongly, deludedly, absurdly — is itself a form of meaning that survives the exposure of its falseness."

I wrote this down. I wrote it all down. My hand was moving but my mind was stuck on something Tokarczuk had said — *the act of building it is itself a form of meaning* — and I was trying to square this with the risk card, the mandate that the protagonist's worldview be fundamentally flawed, revealed through consequences. If the building is itself meaningful, then is she really wrong? Or is she wrong in a way that is also right, which would be a cheat, a softening of the premise?

"There have to be consequences," I said. "She can't just be wrong and beautiful. The wrongness has to cost her something. Real things. Relationships, time, opportunities she missed because she was following a pattern that wasn't there."

Kafka straightened in his chair. "Good," he said. "This is where the waiting begins. Because a person who is wrong in the way you describe — who has built an entire life around a false pattern — this person is always waiting. She is waiting for the final piece of evidence, the one that will confirm everything. The one that will make the search worth it. And this final piece never comes. Not because it does not exist, but because no single piece of evidence could ever bear the weight she has placed on the idea of confirmation. She is Godot's audience. She is waiting for something that was never going to arrive, and the waiting has become her life, and the life has a shape, and the shape looks from the outside like a life being lived, but from the inside it is — "

"A holding pattern," I said.

"A queue," Kafka said. "A queue in which she is the only person, waiting for a window that will never open, in a building that exists only because she keeps showing up."

Tokarczuk poured herself water from a bottle she had apparently brought from somewhere else. The bottle had a label in a language I did not recognize. "You are making her pathetic," she said to Kafka. "That is the danger. If she is merely deluded, merely waiting, merely wrong — then she is a case study, not a character. The reader pities her or diagnoses her. Neither is interesting. She needs to be — " Tokarczuk paused, and the pause was not performative, she was genuinely reaching for something. "She needs to be competent. That is the word. She needs to be brilliant at the wrong thing. Her method of finding patterns needs to be rigorous, elegant, internally consistent. The architecture she builds needs to be genuinely impressive. An outsider watching her work should think: this woman is a genius. And then gradually, terribly, should realize: this woman is a genius who is building a cathedral on a foundation of nothing."

"The competence makes it worse," I said.

"The competence makes it everything," she said. "Because competence is seductive. You watch someone who is good at something and you want them to be right. You root for the pattern to be real. And when it turns out to be nothing — when the cathedral is built on air — the reader's loss mirrors the protagonist's. They were complicit. They wanted to believe."

Kafka was looking at the departures board. The board had changed again; several destinations now appeared to be cities that did not exist, or cities that existed only in certain administrative records from the 1930s. "There is a comedy here," he said, not looking at us. "You are both describing a tragedy. But the waiting — the Beckett waiting, the Godot waiting — is comedy. It is comedy because the waiting is the condition, and the condition is permanent, and the recognition that it is permanent is the joke. The joke that no one laughs at because to laugh at it would be to admit that you are also waiting. That you have always been waiting. That your life is the queue."

"Is this story funny?" I asked. I had not considered this.

"It must be funny," Kafka said, and he turned back to us with an expression I can only describe as the face of a man who has spent his life writing comedies that everyone mistakes for nightmares. "If it is not funny, it is only sad. And sadness is — sadness is the easy response. Sadness asks nothing of the reader. Comedy asks the reader to recognize themselves in the absurdity. To laugh at the woman building her cathedral of nothing and to feel the laugh curdle when they realize they are building one too."

"Funny in what register?" Tokarczuk asked. "Slapstick? Ironic? The humor of accumulation, where the pattern-finding becomes so elaborate that its own excess is the joke?"

"The humor of precision," Kafka said. "The humor of a form filled out in triplicate for an office that has been demolished. The humor of doing something correctly in a context that has made correctness meaningless. Your protagonist — if she is competent, as you say, then her competence in the service of nothing is itself funny. A brilliant investigation that uncovers nothing. A meticulous map of a country that does not exist. The care is the comedy. The rigor is the joke."

I looked at my notebook. I had pages of notes and none of them were a story, which felt right. What I had was a shape — a woman, competent, rigorous, wrong about everything, building something beautiful from nothing, waiting for confirmation that would never come, in a world that was either a corridor or a constellation depending on which of my companions you believed. And it had to be funny. Funny the way a perfectly formatted letter to a dead man is funny.

"One more thing," Tokarczuk said. She had been watching the passengers again, a man and a woman arguing near the platform gate, their gestures large and operatic against the grey light from the station's glass ceiling. "The wandering. She cannot be static. The woman who searches for patterns must move. She must cross borders, ride trains, walk through cities she has never been to and will never return to. The search must have a geography. Not because the geography matters — the patterns are in her head, after all — but because movement is how humans process obsession. You do not sit still with a fixation. You carry it through space. You inflict it on landscapes."

"Pynchon understood this," I said. "Oedipa drives across San Francisco, through neighborhoods that start to look like clues."

"Everyone drives in American novels," Tokarczuk said. "I want walking. I want trains. I want the kind of movement where you feel every kilometer in your legs, where the body's exhaustion becomes part of the argument. A person who has driven to their delusion is different from a person who has walked to it."

Kafka said nothing. He had returned to his crossword, which was still blank, and was holding his pen above the grid with the posture of a man about to write something he had known all along. The station announced another departure, another delay. Outside the window, a pigeon landed on the platform edge, stayed for precisely three seconds, and left. Tokarczuk watched it go. I could see her deciding whether it meant something.

It didn't. But she watched it anyway, and in the watching I saw the story — not its plot, not its structure, but its essential gesture: a woman watching something ordinary with the absolute conviction that it is a clue, and the ordinary thing staring back, indifferent, unaware that it has been recruited into someone's architecture of meaning.

"She should be wrong about the pigeons too," I said.

Kafka filled in one square of the crossword. I could not see what letter he had written. He did not fill in another.
