The place was a laundromat. I had not chosen it — the address on the email was wrong, or the building had been subdivided since the email was sent, or the coffee shop that was supposed to be at 14 Rue de Something had decided, at some point between my receiving the directions and arriving, to become a laundromat. In any case the machines were running. Three of them, front-loaders, tumbling what appeared to be the same load of grey towels at different stages of the cycle. Beckett was sitting on one of the folding tables with his legs dangling, not touching the floor, watching the machines with the expression of a man who has seen everything and finds laundry no less bewildering than the rest.

Tokarczuk was standing near the detergent dispenser, writing in a small notebook. She wrote standing the way other people write sitting — comfortably, with no apparent awareness that the posture was unusual.

"I wanted to talk about movement," I said, pulling a plastic chair from a stack and sitting down.

"You are sitting," Beckett observed.

"I'm sitting now. The story will have movement."

"Every story has movement. Even the ones where no one moves. Especially those." He shifted on the table. The dryer behind him changed pitch — a higher whine, a wobble in the drum. "Molloy moves. He crawls through a ditch for thirty pages. Is that movement? He arrives nowhere. He is further from his mother at the end than at the beginning, or he is closer, but closeness and farness have stopped meaning anything because the coordinate system itself has collapsed. He has a bearing but no destination. The bearing is what kills you."

"A bearing without a destination," Tokarczuk said, looking up from her notebook. "I know that condition. It's called flying."

"Flying is not what I mean."

"Of course it's what you mean. You sit a man in a ditch and point him at his mother's house and the house retreats, and he keeps crawling because crawling is the only verb he has left. That is exactly what it feels like to be in seat 34A watching the seatbelt sign and knowing that the city you are landing in is not the city you left for, because you have changed in the air, the air has done something to you, and the destination on your boarding pass is now a place you have never been to even though you've been there six times."

Beckett looked at her with an expression I would call interested if the word did not imply enthusiasm, which it did not. "You romanticize displacement."

"You fetishize stillness."

"I do not fetishize it. I describe it. Stillness is the condition. Movement is the hallucination." He pointed at the washing machine nearest to us, the one in the spin cycle, its drum a grey blur. "That machine is moving very fast and going nowhere. This is the model. Not travel, not wandering, not the exquisite loneliness of the airport lounge — spinning. Centrifugal force applied to wet cloth."

I was writing all of this down, which meant I was not thinking about it, which meant I would have to think about it later, alone, which is always worse. "The story I want to write," I said, "has someone moving through space. Actually moving. Buses, walking, maybe hitchhiking. But the movement doesn't accumulate into progress. It's motion without vector."

"That is a ditch," Beckett said. "A ditch that happens to cross municipal boundaries."

"No," Tokarczuk said. She closed the notebook and sat down on the floor, cross-legged, next to a basket of someone else's laundry. "A ditch is linear. A ditch has walls. What you are describing — motion without vector — is a different topology. It is the topology of the airport, or the highway, or the body of a person who cannot stop flying from city to city because every city fails to be the city they need, and the failure is not the city's fault, and the need is not a need for a place but a need for the sensation of approaching."

"The sensation of approaching," I repeated.

"Yes. That narrowing. That feeling that you are getting closer. You can sustain it only by never arriving. The moment you arrive, the narrowing becomes a widening — you are in the place, the place is all around you, and the distance you were closing has been replaced by a different kind of distance, the distance of being somewhere and knowing it is not enough."

Beckett slid off the table. He was taller standing than I had expected, and thinner, and he moved with a deliberation that made every step look like a decision that could have gone the other way. He walked to the window. Outside, a street I did not recognize — cobblestones, a pharmacy, a dog tied to a parking meter.

"Moran," he said, to the window. "Moran is sent to find Molloy. He sets out. He has a plan, a route, provisions. He is organized. He is the opposite of Molloy — purposeful, directed, equipped. And by the end he has become Molloy. The route has eaten his purpose. The provisions are gone. He is in a ditch, or near a ditch, and the man he was sent to find is either himself or no one, and the report he writes is the story itself, and the story is a report of failure that is also, somehow, the only success available. The bearing held. The destination did not."

"That's what I want," I said, too quickly.

"You cannot want it. You can only fail to avoid it."

Tokarczuk laughed. It was a genuine laugh, short and sharp, and Beckett turned from the window with something that might have been pleasure at having produced it.

"Here is where I disagree," she said. "In *Flights*, I wrote about Chopin's heart. You know the story — his heart was removed after death, preserved in cognac, smuggled out of Paris in a jar, carried to Warsaw, sealed in a church pillar. During the occupation the Germans took it. After the war it was returned. It has traveled more than most living hearts. And the point — no, not the point, the *fact* — is that the heart's journey was not a ditch. It was not spinning in place. It accumulated meaning at every border crossing, every hand that held the jar. The heart arrived in Warsaw as something more than it was when it left Paris. Travel does that. Not to everyone, not always, but it does. Displacement adds. It does not only subtract."

"Displacement adds to the story told about the object," Beckett said. "Not to the object. The heart is meat. The meaning is in the telling, and the telling is what we do in the ditch while we're not getting anywhere."

I felt the conversation forking, and I did not know which fork was mine. "Can we have both?" I asked. "A character who moves through space, who crosses actual distances — but for whom the movement functions like Beckett's ditch? Motion that looks like Tokarczuk's travel from the outside but feels like Beckett's stasis from the inside?"

"That is every commuter," Tokarczuk said.

"That is everyone," Beckett said.

They agreed, and the agreement was uncomfortable, and neither acknowledged it. The washing machine nearest us finished its cycle and beeped three times and then was silent. The grey towels settled into a wet, motionless heap. Nobody came to collect them.

"There has to be a body," Tokarczuk said, after a while. "A body that registers the movement even when the mind does not. In *Flights* I wrote about anatomical specimens — a body sliced into cross-sections, each slice a map of a single moment in the body's depth. You can lay them out and walk the length of a person. It is the most intimate journey possible: traversing someone's interior from skin to spine. And the body you traverse is still. The body is the ditch. The walking is yours."

"A body that is the ditch," I said.

"Careful," Beckett said. "The metaphor is getting comfortable."

He was right. I could feel it settling, growing symmetrical, and symmetry in this context was a lie. "What if the character has a task?" I said. "Something specific. Not find-my-mother, not carry-the-heart. Something smaller. Deliver an object. Meet a person at a specific place. The task is clear and achievable and should take a few hours, and instead it takes — "

"Forever," Beckett said.

"A week," I suggested.

"A week is forever if you are paying attention."

"A week," Tokarczuk said, "in which the character crosses the same city repeatedly, taking different routes, each route revealing something the previous route concealed, so that the city is not one city but a series of overlapping cities, a palimpsest of routes — "

"Too pretty," Beckett said.

"Too pretty, maybe. But true. Every city is a palimpsest. You walk down a street and the street you walked yesterday is underneath it, and the street from ten years ago is underneath that, and the street from before you were born is underneath that, and you are walking on all of them at once, and your feet know this even if your head does not."

"Molloy's feet know nothing. They have gone numb. Numbness is a kind of knowledge — the knowledge that knowing is finished, that the foot and the ground have reached an agreement that does not include the person attached to the foot."

I sat with this. The laundromat was warm and smelled of soap and something metallic — the machines, perhaps, or the pipes. "What about the comedy?" I said. "You both write comedy. Beckett's is the comedy of the body failing on schedule. Tokarczuk's is the comedy of systems that work perfectly and mean nothing. Where do those meet?"

"In the waiting room," Beckett said, immediately.

"In the departure lounge," Tokarczuk said, at the same time.

They looked at each other.

"What is the difference?" I asked.

"In a waiting room you are waiting for something to happen to you," Beckett said. "A name called. A door opened. In a departure lounge you are waiting to happen to somewhere else."

"Both involve chairs that are designed to prevent sleep," Tokarczuk added.

"Both involve the body in a state of managed anticipation. The muscles are half-clenched. The bladder is making calculations. The mind is performing the specific labor of passing time, which is the hardest labor there is, because time does not want to be passed, time wants to sit there with you, time is the heaviest thing in the room."

I realized I had not asked the question I had come to ask, which was whether meaninglessness is a prison or a freedom, and whether waiting ever becomes traveling, and I realized I had not asked it because they had been answering it for the last hour without anyone framing it as a question, and perhaps that was the answer — that the question dissolves when you stop asking it and start sitting in a laundromat watching machines spin.

"One more thing," Tokarczuk said. She had opened her notebook again. "The route. In navigation, a bearing is a direction measured from north. Zero degrees is north. Ninety is east. You can have a perfect bearing — know exactly which direction you need to go — and still deviate from it. Wind pushes you. Current pulls you. Your own body lists to one side because one leg is shorter, or one shoe is worn, or the hip has been grinding for years and the grind has a direction, and the direction is not the direction you chose. Deviation is the body's autobiography written over the map's instructions."

"The bearing is the intention," Beckett said. "The deviation is the life."

"Yes."

"And the story?"

"The story is the difference between them."

The dryer behind the folding table shuddered and stopped. In the silence that followed, I could hear the pharmacy across the street opening its metal shutters, and the dog tied to the parking meter shifting its weight from one hip to the other, and the sound of my own breathing, which was doing what breathing does, which is continuing, which is the most absurd and the most honest thing a body can do.

Beckett looked at the pile of wet towels in the machine that no one had come to collect. "Someone should move those to the dryer," he said.

No one moved.
