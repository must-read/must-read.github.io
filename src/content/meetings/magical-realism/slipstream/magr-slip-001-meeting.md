The building was wrong. I'd booked a conference room at the University of Buenos Aires — fourth floor, Faculty of Philosophy and Letters, the kind of room with a long table and insufficient lighting that smells permanently of chalk and someone else's lunch. But when I arrived the hallway was longer than it should have been, and the room numbers had rearranged themselves in a way that felt not malicious but indifferent, the way a river rearranges stones.

Borges was already seated at the far end of the table. He had a book open in front of him, though I knew he couldn't see it — this was the Borges of the later years, the one who navigated by memory and sound and the particular gravity of familiar rooms. He was running one finger along the page as if the text might rise up to meet him.

"The room has moved," he said, without looking up. "Yesterday it was 412. Today I counted the doors and it is the seventh from the staircase, which would make it 407. But the number on the door says 412. One of these facts is lying."

"Maybe they're both true," said Karen Russell, who was standing in the doorway behind me. She had a canvas tote bag over one shoulder that appeared to contain, among other things, a paperback, a tangerine, and what looked like a small taxidermied lizard. She saw me staring at it and said, "Gecko. Thrift store. Don't ask."

She took the chair across from Borges and set the gecko on the table between them like a centerpiece. Borges's finger stopped moving on the page.

"There is something on the table," he said.

"A gecko."

"Dead?"

"Taxidermied. Which is a different condition."

Borges considered this. "Preserved. An animal removed from time but kept in shape. That is more interesting than dead." He closed the book. "Shall we discuss this story?"

I sat down between them and opened my notebook to the page where I'd written my thoughts, which now seemed childish — a few scrawled images, a vague sense of architecture. I said I was thinking about a building. A protagonist who lives in a building where the rooms rearrange themselves, and she treats it as normal. The way you'd treat a creaky floorboard. An annoyance, a feature, not a crisis.

"Good," Borges said. "The crisis should not be the architecture. The architecture should be the condition."

"But what's the architecture *doing*?" Russell leaned forward. She had already peeled the tangerine and the smell of it was filling the room, sharp and citric, cutting through the chalk and dust. "Because Ishiguro does this thing in *The Unconsoled* where rooms connect to other rooms that shouldn't be there, and the protagonist just accepts it, and the acceptance is the horror. Or not horror — the dread. That low-grade spatial anxiety where you know something is wrong but your body keeps moving through it. And I love that. But I want to know what the rooms smell like. What's growing in the corners. Is there mold? Is there condensation on the windows? Because a building that rearranges itself is going to have humidity problems."

I laughed. Borges did not.

"You are asking me to consider humidity," he said.

"I am asking you to consider the body in the labyrinth. Your labyrinths are beautiful. They're philosophical instruments. A man enters a garden of forking paths and confronts the nature of time. A librarian wanders an infinite library and confronts the nature of knowledge. But does the librarian sweat? Do his feet hurt? Does the infinite library have a mildew problem?"

"The library of Babel," Borges said, with the patience of a man who has answered this question in different forms for forty years, "is not a physical space. It is an argument."

"But it's shaped like a physical space. Hexagonal galleries. Spiral staircases. You gave it architecture. The moment you gave it architecture, you gave it a body, and bodies have fluids."

"The architecture is a conceit."

"The architecture is a decision. You chose hexagons, not squares. You chose spiral staircases, not elevators. Those choices carry sensory weight whether you intended them to or not. A spiral staircase is a thing you feel in your knees."

The room was quiet for a moment. Somewhere in the hallway a door opened and closed, and the sound was wrong for the distance — too near, as though the hallway had shortened while we weren't paying attention. Borges tilted his head at the sound the way a dog tracks a frequency only it can hear.

"I chose hexagons," he said slowly, "because they tile without gaps. A hexagonal grid fills all available space. There are no corridors, no wasted areas. It is the geometry of total saturation. I was not thinking of the body."

"I know you weren't. That's the gap I want to work in."

I tried to say something about Machado — about *Her Body and Other Parties*, about the body as the site where genre bends, where the domestic becomes the uncanny. I said something about how the protagonist's body could be the thing that registers the impossibility of the building. Not her mind accepting it, but her skin, her joints, her inner ear.

"Yes," Russell said, pointing a tangerine segment at me. "The inner ear. That's where spatial disorientation lives. Vertigo isn't intellectual — it's the fluid in your semicircular canals telling you something your eyes disagree with. If the building rearranges itself, the body knows before the mind does. The body has already fallen."

"But the mind's acceptance is what makes it fiction and not a medical case study," Borges said. His tone had shifted. Not conceding exactly, but circling the idea the way he circled ideas in his stories — approaching from unexpected angles, letting the thing define itself through the space around it. "In *The Unconsoled*, the protagonist does not question the impossible connections. He walks through a door in a hotel and arrives at his childhood home. He does not say: this is impossible. He says: ah, I had forgotten it was so close. That is the most terrifying sentence Ishiguro has ever written. Not because the architecture is impossible, but because the consciousness has reorganized itself to accommodate the impossible architecture. The labyrinth is not in the building. The labyrinth is in the acceptance."

"And I'm saying the body can't accept what the mind accepts. The body keeps score." Russell had eaten most of the tangerine. A seed sat on the table next to the gecko. "You can walk through a door and accept that you've arrived at your childhood home. Your mind can do that. But your body was walking on tile and now it's walking on carpet and the temperature dropped four degrees and there's a smell of cedar that wasn't there before. The body notices the seams."

"The seams," I said, and I wrote it down. I was thinking about Machado's narrator in "The Husband Stitch" — the green ribbon around the throat, the body carrying a secret the narrative keeps almost but never quite revealing. The body as container for something the story won't name.

"What if the building's rearrangements are keyed to the protagonist's body?" I said. "Not to her emotions — not mood architecture, not the building reflecting her inner state, that's too neat — but to her physical state. When she's menstruating the hallways get longer. When she has a fever the walls sweat. Not metaphor. Not pathetic fallacy. Something stranger — a sympathy between her physiology and the building's geometry."

Borges was very still. I could not tell if the stillness was interest or distaste.

"That is dangerously close to allegory," he said.

"It's the opposite of allegory. Allegory says the building stands for the body. I'm saying the building responds to the body without standing for anything. A sympathy that has no meaning. Correspondence without metaphor."

"Correspondence without metaphor." He repeated it as though tasting something unfamiliar. "Swedenborg proposed correspondences between the physical and spiritual worlds. Every natural object was a symbol of a spiritual reality. You are proposing correspondences without the spiritual. Sympathies that are merely — sympathies."

"Merely sympathies is exactly right," Russell said. "That's the slipstream. It's not magic because magic implies a system, rules, a cosmology. It's not realism because the hallways are too long. It's the place in between where things correspond for no reason and the characters live there without explanation because explanation would collapse the thing into either fantasy or metaphor, and it's neither."

"I have spent my life," Borges said, "constructing precise metaphysical systems in miniature. Crystal labyrinths. Logical paradoxes. Infinite libraries with exact dimensions. What you are proposing is the opposite — an imprecise system. A labyrinth that sweats."

"A labyrinth that sweats," Russell repeated. "I want that on a t-shirt."

"I do not want it on a t-shirt. I want to understand what it is."

"It's a building whose corridors correspond to a woman's body for reasons no one in the story understands or attempts to explain. She walks through it. The reader walks through it. No one maps it. Mapping it would be your impulse — the Borgesian impulse, the impulse to taxonomize. I want to resist that. I want a labyrinth that can't be diagrammed."

Borges lifted one hand from the table and placed it back down, a gesture I had seen him make in interviews — a kind of physical punctuation, a full stop made with the body. "And yet the labyrinth must have rules, or it is merely chaos."

"Not rules. Consistencies. There's a difference. A rule says: if the protagonist enters room seven, she will always arrive in room twelve. A consistency says: the hallways are always longer when it rains. Not because rain causes the hallways to grow, but because that's how it is. The consistency doesn't explain itself. It simply recurs."

I was writing fast now. Something was forming — not a plot, nothing as solid as a plot, but a set of textures. A woman in a building. Corridors that respond to her body. A humid, physical space that has the properties of a Borges story — impossible, recursive, self-referential — but smells like mildew and feels like vertigo. A story where the intellectual precision of Borges's architecture collides with Russell's insistence on the sensory, on the body that sweats and bleeds and walks on aching knees through the hexagonal gallery.

"I want to tell you about alligators," Russell said.

Borges raised an eyebrow.

"When I was writing *Swamplandia!*, I spent time in the Everglades. The thing about the Everglades is that the land and the water aren't separate categories. There's no line where land stops and water starts. It's gradient. Sawgrass growing out of water, water seeping through what looks like solid ground. You think you're standing on earth and then you're ankle-deep. You think you're in a creek and then there's a ridge. The whole place is a refusal to commit to being one thing or the other. That's what I think slipstream is. Not a genre — a biome. A place where the categories leak."

"And the alligator?"

"The alligator is perfectly at home. The alligator does not care that the land and water are indistinguishable. The alligator is the protagonist who accepts impossible architecture."

Borges was silent for a long time. The hallway door opened and closed again, closer than before. The gecko on the table seemed to have shifted slightly — facing me now instead of the window. I did not mention this.

"In my story 'Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius,'" Borges said finally, "the objects from the imaginary planet begin appearing in the real world. A compass whose needle is inscribed with a letter from Tlön's alphabet. A cone of bright metal that is impossibly heavy. These objects do not explain themselves. They are simply present. They are — consistencies, as you say. They recur. But they are hard-edged. They have weight. They are precise in their impossibility."

"And I want our story to be imprecise in its impossibility. The corridor doesn't grow by exactly three meters. It grows by some amount. The protagonist's body registers it not through measurement but through exhaustion — her legs are tired by the time she reaches her door, and yesterday they weren't."

"You want me to give up precision."

"I want you to consider that precision is one way of being truthful and exhaustion is another."

I asked about eros. I was thinking about Machado — the way *Her Body and Other Parties* makes the erotic into a site where genre dissolves. The woman in "The Husband Stitch" whose body is a story she can't fully tell. The inventory in "Inventory" where lovers become the measure of a collapsing world. If the building responds to the protagonist's body, what happens when her body is involved with another body? What happens to the architecture during sex?

Russell picked up the tangerine seed and rolled it between her fingers. "That's the question I was hoping someone would ask."

"It is not the question I was hoping anyone would ask," Borges said.

"Because you don't write about sex."

"I write about desire. I write about the desire for knowledge, for order, for the infinite. These are desires of the mind."

"The mind is a body."

"The mind is — " He stopped. He touched the edge of the book on the table, tracing the spine as though reading by feel. "I will grant you that the mind is housed in a body. But the desires I write about transcend the housing."

"Nothing transcends the housing. That's the point." Russell set the seed down next to the gecko. "Machado's genius is that she never lets you forget the housing. Her narrators are women who bleed, who orgasm, who gain and lose weight, who get sick, and the genre of the story bends around those physical facts. When a woman in a Machado story takes a lover, the walls might start changing color. Not because the love is magical — because the body is the place where the real and the unreal share a border, and sex is when the border patrol goes home."

I wrote that down verbatim. It was better than anything I would have come up with.

"So the building responds to intimacy," I said. "The protagonist brings someone home and the apartment reconfigures. Not to accommodate them — not romance, not the building being welcoming. Something stranger. The building registers the second body as a new variable. The geometry changes because the building was calibrated for one body and now there are two."

"Calibrated," Borges repeated. "You see, even when you resist my precision you reach for it. Calibrated is my word."

He was right. I crossed it out. "The building was used to one body," I said instead.

"Better. Worse, but better."

Russell laughed — a sharp, surprised sound that broke something in the room's atmosphere. "Used to. I like that. The building is used to her. The way a mattress remembers a body, the way a shoe holds a foot's shape. Not calibrated — habituated. And habituation breaks when the variables change."

"But we must not make the second body the disruption," Borges said, and his voice had gained an edge I hadn't heard before — not defensive, but insistent, the tone of a man protecting something he considers essential. "If the second body is what breaks the building's consistency, then we are writing a domestic story. A story about a woman whose life is disrupted by a lover. That is a fine story. It is not our story."

"What is our story?"

"I do not know yet. But I know it is not a story about disruption. It is a story about accommodation. The building has already rearranged itself. The protagonist has already accepted this. The question is not *what breaks* but *what has always been broken and is only now being noticed*."

Russell was quiet for a few seconds. She pulled the tangerine peel apart into smaller pieces, and the citrus smell surged again, briefly overpowering the room's chalk-and-old-paper atmosphere. "Fine. You may be right about that." She said it the way you concede a point in chess — acknowledging the move while already thinking three moves ahead. "The building was always like this. The protagonist's body was always responding. The lover — if there is a lover — doesn't change anything. The lover just makes the protagonist aware of what was already happening."

"Awareness is not the same as understanding."

"No. Awareness without understanding. The body aware of something the mind cannot categorize."

Borges turned his face toward the window. Outside, the Buenos Aires afternoon had gone a strange amber, the light thickening with humidity as though the sky itself were sweating. He was quiet long enough that I wondered if he'd left the conversation — gone somewhere internal, one of his libraries or labyrinths.

"There was a house in Palermo," he said at last. "When I was a boy. My family's house. I knew every room, or I believed I did. But the house was different at night. Not in the way all houses are different at night — not simply darker, quieter. The proportions changed. The hallway from my bedroom to the bathroom was longer at three in the morning than at three in the afternoon. I could not prove this. I measured it once, or I believe I measured it — I was eight, perhaps nine — and the measurement was the same both times. But my body did not believe the measurement. My body knew the hallway grew."

Russell's expression changed. Something opened in it — not triumph at having won the argument, but recognition. The recognition of one writer hearing another tell the truth.

"That's the story," she said.

"That is a memory."

"Those are the same thing, for our purposes."

I wanted to ask more — about the house in Palermo, about the hallway, about what Borges's body knew that his measurements couldn't confirm. But the light outside the window shifted again, and I realized the room had rearranged itself around us while we talked. The door was no longer where I remembered it being. The gecko was facing a direction that didn't correspond to any wall. The tangerine peel on the table was giving off a smell that had nothing to do with citrus — something older, vegetal, like moss or standing water.

Neither of them seemed to notice. Or they noticed and did not remark on it, which was worse, and better, and closer to what the story needed to do.

"The protagonist should not be a librarian," Borges said. "That is too easy. That is too much mine."

"And not a swamp girl. Not mine either."

"Someone ordinary. Someone whose relationship to space is functional. A woman who works in the building. Who has memorized the route from the elevator to her desk and back. Whose body has automated the architecture."

"Until the architecture un-automates."

"Yes."

Russell picked up the gecko and put it back in her tote bag. "I think we're done. Or we're at the point where talking about it more will kill it."

She was right. There was a shape in the room now — not a plot, not an outline, but an atmosphere. A humid labyrinth. A woman whose body knows things her measurements cannot confirm. Architecture that corresponds to physiology without meaning to. The sensory precision of swamp water filling the hexagonal galleries of an impossible library.

I closed my notebook. We stood up to leave.

The door was three steps away, where it had always been, where it had never been.
