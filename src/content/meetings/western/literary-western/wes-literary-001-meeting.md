I had imagined somewhere outdoors — a porch, a rail fence, the kind of Western geography that arranges itself behind a conversation the way a set dresses a stage. Instead, Annie Proulx suggested a laundromat in Sheridan, Wyoming, and Larry McMurtry said he'd been to that laundromat and it had closed, and Proulx said the one next to it was still open, and McMurtry said that was a different laundromat entirely, and this argument consumed the first four minutes of our meeting before either of them had addressed me or the story or why we were all in the same room.

The laundromat was real. Two rows of front-loaders, a wall of dryers with porthole windows like a bank of dead televisions, a folding table scarred with cigarette burns from before the no-smoking sign went up. The fluorescent light gave everything the flat, accusatory brightness of a police interrogation. Proulx had brought her own laundry — a canvas bag of what appeared to be work clothes, the kind you wear to move hay or mend fence — and she fed quarters into a machine with the practiced efficiency of someone who has done exactly this in exactly these places for decades. McMurtry sat on a molded plastic chair near the window, looking at the parking lot, which had three cars in it and a dog sleeping under a truck.

"I don't want to write about a ranch," Proulx said, not looking at me, watching her clothes begin to turn in the machine's window.

I said that was fine. I said the story didn't have to involve a ranch.

"It'll end up involving a ranch. Everything in Wyoming involves a ranch eventually. But I don't want the ranch to be the center. I want the house to be the center. I want a house that is failing the people in it — not because the house is old or falling apart, although it may be both, but because the idea that a house can hold a life in place is the lie that Western settlement was built on. You put up walls and a roof and you call it home and you think the walls will keep the outside out. They won't. The wind comes through. The cold comes through. And eventually the person comes through — walks out the door and doesn't walk back."

McMurtry turned from the window. "You're talking about women leaving."

"I'm talking about women leaving."

"Whose leaving?"

"I don't know yet. I know she leaves. I know the house she leaves is important — that it represents something to the people who stay. And I know the story has to hold both things: the leaving and the staying. Not as opposites. As two responses to the same impossibility."

I said I'd been thinking about Marilynne Robinson — about *Housekeeping*, about Sylvie and Ruth and the house in Fingerbone that can't keep the water out, that can't keep anything in. About how Robinson makes the house into a character and then lets the character fail. The house doesn't burn down or collapse. It just stops being the thing it was supposed to be. It stops holding.

"Robinson understands houses," McMurtry said. "She understands that a house is a bet. You're betting that this place, this specific arrangement of rooms and walls, will be sufficient. That you can live a life in this shape. And most people win that bet, or they win it well enough. But some people lose it, and the losing is not dramatic. The losing is quiet. You wake up one morning and the rooms don't make sense anymore. The hallway is too long. The kitchen faces the wrong direction. Nothing has changed and everything has changed."

"That's a middle-class problem," Proulx said. "Robinson's house in Fingerbone is the house of people who have enough. The furniture's still there. The structure's sound. What fails is the spiritual contract. Out here, when a house fails, it fails materially. The roof goes. The well dries up. The foundation cracks because the ground shifts or the frost heaves or someone built on shale because shale was what was there. The spiritual contract fails too, but you don't notice the spiritual contract failing when you're trying to keep the pipes from freezing."

"So you want the material and the spiritual to fail at the same time."

"I want them to be the same failure. In the West, they are. When your house is cold, your marriage is cold. When the fence breaks, the family breaks. People treat that as metaphor. It's not metaphor. It's economics. The house and the life inside it are the same structure, and when one goes, the other goes with it."

I asked about the women. I said I kept seeing two women — not as characters yet, but as positions. One who stays in the house. One who leaves. Two lives running parallel, the way Haruf runs lives parallel in *Plainsong* — not intersecting, not commenting on each other, just existing side by side in the same landscape, the same weather, the same unforgiving economy of prairie survival.

McMurtry shook his head slowly, but it was the kind of headshake that meant he was thinking, not refusing. "Parallel lives are hard in short fiction. You need space for parallels. You need time. Haruf takes three hundred pages to let those lives breathe next to each other. You'll have, what, five thousand words? Six? You can't build parallels in six thousand words. You can suggest them."

"Suggest is enough," Proulx said. "You don't need full parallels. You need two women and one house and the reader needs to understand that the house means something different to each of them. One sees the house and sees a life. The other sees the house and sees a sentence."

"A prison sentence."

"A sentence. The kind you serve. The kind that has a term, except nobody told you the term, so you just keep serving it."

McMurtry moved to the folding table, pressed his palm flat against the Formica. "I knew a woman in Archer City. Nell Greer. She lived in a house her husband's father had built — a wood-frame house, nothing special, three rooms and a kitchen and a porch that faced west because west was the view, which was nothing, which was the whole point of the view. Her husband died in 1974. Her children moved to Dallas. She stayed in that house for twenty-six more years. When I asked her why, she said, 'Where would I go that the wind wouldn't find me?' I've never forgotten that. Where would I go that the wind wouldn't find me."

"That's resignation," Proulx said.

"That's belonging."

"What's the difference?"

"The difference is whether the staying costs you something. Resignation is free. You give up and you stop paying. Belonging is expensive. You stay and it keeps costing you — every day, every winter, every morning you wake up and the house is cold and the well is low and the road goes both directions and you choose the direction that keeps you where you are. That's a choice. You can call it resignation if you want, but Nell Greer made that choice every morning for twenty-six years, and each time she made it, it meant something."

I felt the argument bending into the territory where both of them were right and neither would admit it. I tried to pull us toward something concrete. I said I thought the story needed a specific house — not a symbolic house, not a house that stands for the West, but a house with a crooked doorframe and a stain on the ceiling and a drawer that sticks in August because the wood swells. A house you could draw a floor plan of.

"Shotgun house," Proulx said. "Three rooms in a line. Front door, back door, and you can see clean through from one to the other. No hallways. No privacy. Everything is visible to everything else."

"That's Southern."

"Shotgun houses exist in Wyoming. They exist everywhere people were too poor or too practical to build anything more complicated than a line of rooms. And the form is perfect for what we're doing. A house with no hallways is a house that doesn't hide anything. The woman who stays in it — she stays knowing there's nowhere in the house to be alone. The woman who leaves — she leaves because there's nowhere in the house to be alone."

"Same house, same absence, different conclusion," I said.

Proulx's dryer buzzed. She got up, opened the door, felt the clothes, closed the door, put in another quarter. She did this without interrupting the conversation, which is to say without acknowledging that the conversation was something separate from the rest of her life.

"I want to be careful about the woman who leaves," McMurtry said. "Because in Western fiction, the woman who leaves is always punished. She leaves the ranch, she leaves the town, she leaves the marriage, and the story punishes her — she comes to ruin, she regrets it, she dies in a city that doesn't know her name. The whole tradition says women who leave the West are traitors, and the West punishes traitors."

"Robinson doesn't punish Sylvie."

"Robinson is smarter than the tradition. But even Robinson makes the leaving strange. Sylvie is uncanny. She's beautiful and damaged and she drifts like weather. She's not a woman who makes a rational decision to leave a house that isn't working. She's a force of nature. And that's gorgeous, but it lets the reader off the hook. You can admire Sylvie without reckoning with her choice, because her choice feels inevitable rather than chosen."

"So our woman who leaves should be rational."

"She should be boring about it. She should be practical. She should leave the way people actually leave — not in a grand gesture, not in the middle of the night, not with a suitcase and a look of determination. She should leave on a Tuesday. She should leave after doing the dishes. She should do the dishes first because the dishes need doing and she's the kind of person who finishes what she's doing before she does the next thing, and the next thing happens to be leaving."

I said that was devastating. McMurtry accepted this without visible reaction, which I was beginning to understand was his version of agreement.

Proulx pulled her clothes from the dryer and began folding them on the scarred table, making precise squares of the work shirts, the jeans, the heavy socks. Her hands moved with the same compressed efficiency I'd always felt in her prose — nothing wasted, every fold exact, the fabric submitted to an order it did not choose.

"The staying woman," she said. "She's the one who interests me. The leaving woman is sympathetic — anyone can understand leaving. The staying woman is the mystery. Why does she stay? Not because she's trapped. Traps are melodrama. She stays because she has made a calculation that the rest of the world has not made — that the house, even this house, even this failing drafty house with its crooked frame and its stain on the ceiling, is more real than whatever is on the other side of the door. The road goes to town. Town goes to a highway. The highway goes to Denver or Billings or wherever people go when they go. And all of those places are, to her, less substantial than the kitchen floor under her feet at six in the morning when the light comes through the window and hits the counter in a specific way that she has memorized without meaning to."

"That's beautiful," McMurtry said. "And I'm not sure it's true."

"It doesn't have to be true. It has to be hers."

"If it's not true, it's delusion. And delusion is another way of not taking the staying woman seriously. You've given her a beautiful reason to stay, and the beauty of the reason is a form of condescension. You're saying she stays for the light on the counter. Real people don't stay for light on a counter. They stay because the alternative is worse, or because they're afraid, or because staying is what they know and knowing is a kind of gravity that holds you in place even when the place is bad for you."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive. She can be afraid and also see the light on the counter."

"But which one is the reason?"

Proulx folded the last pair of socks and set them on the pile. "Both. Neither. The whole point is that reasons for staying are like reasons for anything — they're stories you tell yourself after the fact. She stays. That's the irreducible thing. She stays and we watch her stay and the watching is the story."

I asked about the Haruf element — the spare architecture, the patience, the way *Plainsong* lets silence do the work that most novels assign to dialogue. I said I thought the story should be quiet. Not hushed, not poetic-quiet, but genuinely spare. Sentences that do one thing and then stop.

"Haruf never uses quotation marks," McMurtry said. "That matters more than people think. Without quotation marks, speech and thought and narration exist on the same plane. Nothing is set apart. The dialogue isn't an event — it's just another kind of breathing. I think our story should do that. I think the two women should speak rarely, and when they speak, it should be indistinguishable from the wind or the creak of the house or the sound of a pot being set on a burner."

"I don't agree with the indistinguishable part," Proulx said. "Speech should be precise even when it's sparse. A woman who says 'the gate's open' is telling you something about the gate and also about herself — about her attention, her vigilance, her assumption that she is the one who notices things. The precision of Western speech is not poetry. It's survival. You say what needs saying. You say it once. If the other person doesn't hear you, that's their problem and possibly their death."

"We're both right," McMurtry said.

"We're both right and the story will have to choose."

"Or it won't choose. The story holds both and doesn't resolve the tension. That's what parallel lives do. They don't converge. They don't illuminate each other. They just go on, side by side, in the same wind."

I gathered my notebook. The laundromat was empty now except for us — the other customers had come and gone while we talked, carrying their clean clothes into the parking lot where the dog still slept under the truck, its ribs moving in the slow rhythm of something that had no reason to be anywhere else. Proulx tucked her canvas bag under her arm and walked toward the door.

"One more thing," she said, stopping but not turning around. "The house. At some point in the story, the house should be empty. Both women gone — one by leaving, one by dying or just by being somewhere else for the afternoon. The house empty. And the story should stay with the house for a paragraph or two. Just the house. The stain on the ceiling. The stuck drawer. The floor that is cold on one side and warm on the other where the sun hits. The house existing without the people who gave it meaning. Because that's what will happen eventually. That's what always happens. The people leave or die and the house goes on being a house until it doesn't, and then it's lumber and nails and a depression in the ground, and then it's grass."

She left. McMurtry watched her go, then looked at me.

"She's right about the house," he said. "She's wrong about the grass. The grass is too neat. The grass is the ending every Western writer reaches for because it sounds true and it sounds final and it sounds like the land reclaiming itself. But the land doesn't reclaim. The land just is. There's no agency in grass growing over a foundation. It's not closure. It's photosynthesis."

"So what's the ending?"

"I don't know. I know it's not the grass. I know it's not the woman on the road, diminishing into distance. I know it's not the other woman at the window, watching. Those are all endings I've written and they all feel finished and the problem with feeling finished is that real things don't finish. They just reach a point where you stop looking."

He stood up. He adjusted his collar, which didn't need adjusting, in the way of a man who wanted to do something with his hands while his mind finished a thought.

"The woman who stays," he said. "Give her something to do at the end that isn't meaningful. Give her a task. Sweeping, maybe, or checking a latch. Something her hands know how to do. Let the story end with her hands. Not her thoughts. Not her grief or her resolve or her understanding. Her hands, doing what they do, because her hands will go on doing what they do long after her mind has given up trying to understand why she's still here, in this house, in this wind, on this ground that owes her nothing and to which she owes — "

He stopped. He didn't finish the sentence. He picked up his hat from the chair — I hadn't noticed he'd brought a hat, it was just a plain straw Resistol, nothing dramatic — and he put it on and walked out into the parking lot, where the light was doing what Wyoming light does in the late afternoon, which is flatten everything into a single plane of gold and shadow, erasing the distance between things, making the near and the far the same color, the same brightness, the same quiet.
