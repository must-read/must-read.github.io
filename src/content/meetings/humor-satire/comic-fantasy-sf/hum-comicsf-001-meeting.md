The pub was called The Adjournment, which both of them had found independently hilarious and then pretended not to notice. It sat on a side street in Islington that seemed to exist only intermittently — the kind of London street that navigational apps render with visible reluctance, and which pedestrians discover only when they've given up looking for somewhere else. Adams had arrived first, which surprised everyone, including Adams. Pratchett had arrived exactly on time, which surprised no one.

I was already at the table with a pint of something amber and a notebook full of what I was beginning to suspect were the wrong questions.

"The thing about Le Guin," Adams said, before anyone had properly sat down, "is that she understood something about aliens that every science fiction writer since has been carefully ignoring."

"Go on." Pratchett settled into his chair with the deliberate ease of a man who has learned that urgency is the enemy of good listening. He'd ordered at the bar — a half of bitter, because he was driving, or because he liked the discipline of it, or both.

"She understood that the truly alien thing about aliens isn't tentacles or telepathy or having three genders. It's that they might have perfectly reasonable political systems that are completely opaque to you. Not because they're stupid, and not because you're stupid, but because the assumptions are different all the way down. Genly Ai spends the entire book trying to understand Gethenian politics, and the thing that makes it great is that he *mostly fails*."

"He fails usefully, though," Pratchett said. "That's the trick. He learns enough to get things catastrophically wrong in the right direction."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's an observation about diplomacy. All diplomacy is getting things wrong in the right direction. The wrong direction is war. The right direction is a trade agreement that everyone finds slightly humiliating, which means it's working."

I opened my notebook. "So the combination here is — "

"Don't read me the brief," Adams said, not unkindly. "I've read the brief. The brief is fine. Briefs are always fine. The problem with briefs is that they make everything sound achievable, and the moment something sounds achievable, you've already lost whatever was interesting about it."

"What was interesting about it?" I asked.

"The impossibility. Le Guin wrote a novel about a man trying to understand people who don't have fixed gender, and she did it in 1969, and the reason it works is that she didn't pretend understanding was the end state. The book ends with Genly Ai having been changed, but not enlightened. He's been broken open, not completed. Most science fiction wants first contact to be a puzzle with a solution. Le Guin knew it was a wound."

"That's rather good," Pratchett said. "But you've left out the politics."

"I've left out the politics because the politics is the part I find properly terrifying."

"You find *politics* terrifying? You wrote about the destruction of Earth as a minor inconvenience."

"Earth being destroyed is simple. A big thing happens and then it's done. Politics is the opposite — nothing ever happens and yet everything is constantly getting worse. The politics of *The Left Hand of Darkness* is two countries locked in a rivalry that neither can explain and neither can stop, and an envoy from the outside who thinks he can help but is actually just adding mass to a system that's already collapsing under its own weight."

Pratchett took a slow drink. "Now do Catch-22."

Adams groaned. "Catch-22 is the book I wish I'd written and also the book I could never have written, because Heller understood American institutional madness in his bones and I only understand English institutional madness in mine, and they're cousins but they're not the same animal."

"They're not," Pratchett agreed. "English institutional madness is polite. It apologizes while it's destroying you. American institutional madness is enthusiastic. It promotes you while it's destroying you."

"The catch in Catch-22," I said, because I felt I should contribute something, "is that the rules are perfectly logical from inside the system. You can be grounded for insanity, but asking to be grounded proves you're sane, so you can't be grounded. It's a closed loop."

"Every institution is a closed loop," Adams said. "That's what makes it an institution. If it were open, it would be a conversation, and nobody funds conversations."

"But Heller's thing," Pratchett said, leaning forward, "is that the loop *escalates*. It's not a stable absurdity. It gets worse. Every time Yossarian finds a way to cope, the system generates a new clause. The missions keep increasing. The bureaucracy metastasizes. And the people running it don't even notice, because from their perspective, everything is functioning correctly."

"Yes," Adams said. "The universe is expanding and nobody working in the expansion office thinks anything is wrong."

They looked at each other with the particular satisfaction of two people who have arrived at an analogy from different directions and found it waiting for them like a mutual friend at a railway station.

"So what's the story?" I asked.

"The story," Pratchett said, "is a document."

I blinked. "A document?"

"You've got a risk card. Story-as-document. An official report or regulatory filing. Right?"

I hadn't mentioned the risk card. He just knew. Or he'd guessed, or he'd read the brief despite what Adams said about briefs.

"I love it," Adams said, which was suspicious, because Adams loving an idea instantly usually meant he was about to complicate it past all usefulness. "A report. A formal report filed by someone in an enormous interstellar bureaucracy, concerning a planet or a culture or a — what? A situation?"

"A situation that has gotten out of hand," Pratchett said. "In a way that the report itself cannot quite acknowledge, because acknowledging it would mean admitting that the system allowed it to happen, and the system does not allow things to happen. Things happen *despite* the system, which is why we have the system."

"The report should be annotated," Adams said. He'd produced a pen from somewhere and was drawing on a napkin. "Footnotes. Marginal corrections from someone higher up the chain. And then corrections on the corrections. And possibly a memo about the proper formatting of corrections."

"Now you're just doing your thing."

"My thing works."

"Your thing works brilliantly at a certain altitude. But if you stay up there — cosmic scale, galactic bureaucracy, committees that span light-years — you lose the person. Heller never lost the person. Yossarian was afraid of dying. Properly, viscerally, specifically afraid of dying. That's what made the absurdity hurt."

Adams put down his pen. "You're right. Damn you."

"I usually am. It's one of my least attractive qualities."

"The document needs a person inside it," I said. "Not just as the author of the report, but as someone whose survival depends on the report being received correctly. Someone who is using official language because official language is the only language the system understands, but whose terror keeps leaking through the margins."

Pratchett pointed at me. "Good. That. The gap between what the document says and what the person means. That's where the comedy lives. Not in the jokes — in the distance between the official version and the truth. Le Guin did that too, by the way. *The Left Hand of Darkness* has chapters that are myths and field reports and legends, and they all tell you something that the narrative proper can't say directly."

"Because some truths can only be told slant," I said.

"Don't quote Dickinson at me. I'm trying to make a point about structure."

Adams was still drawing on his napkin. It appeared to be a flowchart, though the arrows seemed to lead in all directions simultaneously. "What if the document is a compliance assessment? An envoy has been sent to determine whether a planet is in compliance with — something. Some regulation. And the planet is, by any reasonable measure, not in compliance. But the envoy can't say that, because saying it would trigger a response that nobody wants, including the envoy, including possibly the people who wrote the regulation."

"So the envoy writes a report that technically says everything is fine while actually describing a catastrophe," Pratchett said.

"Yes, but it's not dishonesty. It's *translation*. The envoy is translating reality into a language that the bureaucracy can process, and in the process, reality gets — "

"Neutered."

"Reorganized. Reality gets reorganized into something that fits the form. And the form has boxes. And boxes have edges. And edges exclude."

I was writing fast. "Catch-22's logic, but applied to an interstellar diplomatic assessment. Le Guin's lone envoy, but trapped in the reporting structure rather than freed by it. The planet is genuinely alien — not hostile, not primitive, just *incomprehensible* to the assessment framework. And the more the envoy tries to describe it accurately, the more the framework distorts it into something assessable."

"The framework is the antagonist," Pratchett said. "Not a villain. Not a person. The form itself. The dropdown menus. The mandatory fields that don't apply but must be filled in anyway."

"I had a tax form once," Adams said, in the tone of a man about to deliver a monologue, "that asked me to describe my primary source of income in ten words or fewer. Ten words. I write novels. That's two words. But the form wanted to know about rights, royalties, foreign editions, audio licenses, and what they called 'ancillary creative revenue,' which I think meant the towel. The one from the Hitchhiker's merchandise. And I thought: this form is asking me to describe my relationship with my own work in a space designed for someone who sells plumbing supplies. And if I can't fit, the form doesn't conclude that it's the wrong form. The form concludes that I'm the wrong person."

"That," Pratchett said quietly, "is the story."

"Is it?"

"The form concluding that you're the wrong person. An envoy files a report about an alien civilization, and the report concludes — through no fault of the envoy, through the accumulated logic of every mandatory field and dropdown menu and section heading — that the civilization isn't a civilization. Because it doesn't fit the form. And once it doesn't fit the form, what happens to it?"

Nobody spoke for a moment. Outside, a bus went past, its route number briefly illegible in the rain.

"What happens to it," Adams said, "is policy."

"And policy," Pratchett said, "is where the comedy stops being funny. That's the thing about Catch-22 that people forget. It's hilarious for three hundred pages and then Snowden's guts are falling out and you realize you've been laughing at a massacre. The comedy was the anesthesia. Heller knew that. He wasn't trying to make you laugh. He was trying to make you laugh so hard that when he pulled the rug, you'd feel the floor."

"Terry," Adams said, "are you suggesting we write something that actually matters?"

"I'm suggesting that you and I are both very good at being funny and that being funny is the easiest way to avoid saying the thing you need to say. Le Guin never hid behind funny. She walked straight into the cold and reported back."

"I don't do cold. I do mildly bewildered."

"Then be mildly bewildered about something that matters. A civilization being assessed out of existence by a form. An envoy who can see what's happening and can't stop it because stopping it would mean filing a counter-report, and the counter-report would need to be approved by the committee that approved the original assessment, and the committee meets quarterly, and by the time it meets — "

"The civilization has been reclassified."

"Reclassified as what?"

Adams looked at his napkin. "Terrain."

Pratchett stared at him. Then he took off his hat — the black fedora, the one he wore everywhere — and set it on the table, which was his way of conceding that someone had said something better than he would have.

"Terrain," Pratchett repeated. "A living culture reclassified as terrain. By a form. With a dropdown menu."

"And the envoy," I said, my pen moving almost independently of my hand, "the envoy knows. The envoy understands the civilization — not completely, not the way Le Guin's envoy comes to understand Gethen, but enough. Enough to know that what the form is describing bears no relationship to what exists. But the envoy is also a functionary. The envoy exists within the system. The envoy's career, pension, housing allocation — all of it depends on the report being accepted. And accepted means: fitting the form."

"The circular logic," Adams said. "Catch-22. The envoy can't file an accurate report because an accurate report would be rejected as non-compliant with the reporting framework. But filing an inaccurate report that complies with the framework will destroy the thing being reported on. And not filing a report is not an option, because — "

"Because there's a regulation requiring reports to be filed," Pratchett finished.

"On time."

"On time and in triplicate."

They were both grinning now, and the grins were not happy grins. They were the grins of people who have located something true and found it, as true things often are, unbearable.

"The document should be funny," I said. "Genuinely, structurally funny. The language of bureaucratic reporting is inherently comic — the passivity, the euphemism, the way it talks about outcomes without ever mentioning causes. But underneath — "

"Underneath is Le Guin's question," Pratchett said. "Can you see what's alien without destroying it? Can you encounter something genuinely other and let it remain other? Or does every system of understanding eventually become a system of reduction?"

"I think," Adams said, picking up his pen again, "that the answer is no. Every system reduces. That's what systems do. The question is whether the envoy, inside the system, can find a way to — not save the civilization, that's too neat — but at least make the record show, somewhere in the footnotes, in the marginalia, in the appendix that nobody reads, what was actually there."

"A footnote as an act of witness," I said.

"Don't get sentimental about it."

"I'm not. I'm — "

"He's getting sentimental about it," Pratchett said to Adams.

"I know. They always do. Listen." Pratchett turned to me, and his eyes, behind the glasses, were unexpectedly sharp. "The envoy doesn't save anything. The footnote doesn't save anything. The civilization gets reclassified. The report gets filed. The form works exactly as designed. If you give this a hopeful ending, you've betrayed everything we've been talking about. The point of Catch-22 isn't that Yossarian escapes. It's that the system doesn't care whether he escapes."

"But he does escape," Adams said. "Yossarian does escape. He runs to Sweden."

"And Heller spent twenty years wishing he hadn't written that ending."

"Did he?"

"He wrote *Closing Time*. The whole book is an apology for letting Yossarian live."

Adams looked into his empty coffee cup — he'd switched from beer to coffee at some point without anyone noticing, which felt like a metaphor for something. "So the document. The report. It's filed. It's processed. It's archived. And the civilization — "

"Becomes terrain."

"And the envoy — "

"Fills out the next form."

The rain had picked up. Someone behind the bar switched on the heating, and the pub contracted slightly around us, the way rooms do when the weather outside becomes hostile and the warmth inside becomes a conspiracy.

"I think we've got it," I said, which was exactly the kind of thing I'd been told not to say.

Pratchett put his hat back on. "You've got the shape of something. Whether it becomes a story or a very elaborate napkin remains to be seen."

Adams folded the napkin into his jacket pocket. "I'm keeping this."

"It's got beer on it."

"The best architectural plans always do."

We sat for a while longer, not talking about the story, talking about other things — a play Pratchett had seen, a technology Adams had opinions about, a mutual acquaintance who had recently done something inadvisable with a boat. The conversation drifted the way conversations drift when the important part is over and nobody wants to be the first to acknowledge it. Outside, the rain described complicated patterns on the window, and the street that only intermittently existed continued, for the moment, to exist.
