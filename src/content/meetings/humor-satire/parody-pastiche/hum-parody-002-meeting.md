The tearoom had doilies. I want to be clear about the doilies because they became important later. They were the old-fashioned kind, crocheted by someone who believed that no flat surface should go unprotected from the speculative damage of crockery, and they covered every table with the defensive thoroughness of a field fortification. Pratchett was already seated when I arrived, examining one with the focused interest of a man who has spent decades noticing things that other people use without seeing.

"Do you know," he said, lifting the doily and peering through its lattice like an astronomer with a particularly domestic telescope, "that doilies are the perfect emblem of civilization? They serve no purpose. They protect nothing. A teacup does not damage a table. But someone, somewhere, decided that between the cup and the table there must be an intermediary, a little priest of propriety, and now we have them in every tearoom in England and no one can say why."

"I can say why," said Jane Austen, who had been there the entire time. I had not seen her come in. She had simply been present, in the way that a well-placed piece of furniture is present — so correct in her positioning that the eye accepted her as part of the room's original design. "They exist because the appearance of care is more important than care itself. A woman who places doilies is a woman who has been seen to maintain standards. The table is irrelevant. The doily is a letter of reference."

Pratchett set down his doily with the expression of a man who has just discovered that his whimsical observation was, in fact, someone else's doctoral thesis.

"Right," he said. "Yes. That's darker than I meant it."

"Most true things are darker than people mean them."

I introduced the assignment. A story in the humor-satire line, parody-pastiche specifically. The combination involved *Pride and Prejudice* and *Wyrd Sisters* — the marriage plot as social negotiation, the play-within-the-play, three collaborators of different temperaments. I mentioned the setting we had in mind: a Regency village, three witches, and a new curate who is, in fact, a demon.

"But a demon with ten thousand a year," Pratchett added, because he'd been given the brief.

"And excellent manners," Austen said.

"And the village sees no problem."

"The village would not see a problem. A village never sees a problem with ten thousand a year. The village would have married its daughters to Beelzebub if Beelzebub had a good living and danced well at assemblies." She paused. "I have known several Beelzebubs. They were all considered eligible."

This was the nerve of the thing, and we'd found it inside two minutes. I'd expected to have to steer them there. Instead, Austen had simply walked to the center of the room and stood on it.

"The witches know," Pratchett said. He had picked up the doily again and was folding it into something — a hat for the salt cellar, it turned out. "That's the engine. The witches know he's a demon. They can smell it, or see it, or whatever it is witches do — Granny Weatherwax would just *know*, in that way she has of knowing things by refusing to not know them. But the village doesn't know, and more importantly, the village doesn't want to know."

"The village has decided," Austen said, "that he is charming."

"And once a village has decided someone is charming—"

"It is very nearly impossible to unconvince them. Charm, in a village, operates as a kind of collective investment. Everyone has praised him. Everyone has accepted his invitations. Everyone has told everyone else how delightful he is. To discover, now, that he is a demon would be to admit that one's judgment was wrong, and there is nothing — nothing in the whole of human experience — that people will fight harder to avoid than the admission that their judgment was wrong."

"That," I said, "is *Pride and Prejudice*."

"That is every novel I ever wrote. The distance between what people see and what is there is not an error. It is a choice. Elizabeth Bennet is not deceived by Wickham because she is stupid. She is deceived because he confirms what she already believes, and confirmation is so much more comfortable than evidence."

Pratchett was nodding, but with the particular energy of someone who agrees with the diagnosis and disagrees with the treatment. "Right, but here's where we part company. Your villages are — forgive me — closed systems. The comedy comes from everyone being trapped in the same drawing room, watching each other with these incredibly precise instruments of social observation, and nobody can leave because outside the drawing room there's nothing. Fields. Sheep. More villages doing exactly the same thing."

"I am aware of what is outside the drawing room."

"But in Discworld — in Lancre, say, where the witches live — outside the drawing room there's *everything*. Elves. Time anomalies. Death on a horse. The comedy comes from ordinary people dealing with extraordinary things using ordinary tools. Granny Weatherwax fights an invasion of elves with nothing but bloody-mindedness and a very clear understanding of where the borders are. The joke isn't that people are trapped. The joke is that people are free, and they *still* choose the stupid option, and somehow it works, and you love them for it."

Austen set down her teacup with a precision that suggested she was restraining herself from throwing it. "I do not write about trapped people. I write about people who believe they are free and demonstrate, through every choice, that they are not. This is a different observation, and I will thank you not to confuse them."

"Fair enough," Pratchett said, with the tone of a man stepping back from a fence he'd just discovered was electrified. "Fair enough. But my point stands: the witches are your outside element. They're the people who can see through the charm because they're not invested in it. They haven't praised him at assemblies. They haven't accepted his invitations. They've just looked at him and gone, 'That's a demon, that is.'"

"And no one listens to them," I said.

"Nobody ever listens to the witches! That's — that's the whole *joke* of Lancre. Granny Weatherwax is the most powerful witch on the Disc, and she can't get the village to stop leaving their washing out when it's going to rain. Power doesn't help. Being right doesn't help. The only thing that helps is knowing how people work, which Granny does, which is why she's so bloody furious all the time, because knowing how people work means knowing exactly how they're going to ignore you."

"Mrs. Bennet," Austen said quietly.

"Sorry?"

"Mrs. Bennet knows how people work. She is ridiculous, and she is vulgar, and she is the only character in *Pride and Prejudice* who understands that her daughters will starve if they do not marry, and nobody listens to her either, because she says the right things in the wrong way, and in a drawing room, the way you say things is the only thing that matters."

There was a silence. Pratchett put down the doily hat. "That's — I hadn't thought of Mrs. Bennet like that."

"Most people haven't. Most people think she is a comic figure. She is a comic figure. She is also right. The two are not mutually exclusive. In fact, in my experience, they are almost always the same thing."

I felt something shift in the room. They'd stopped performing their disagreement and had bumped into an actual one — the question of whether the comic figure who is right is better served by sympathy or by precision. Pratchett wanted to love the witches. Austen wanted to pin them, wriggling, to the page and let the reader decide.

"The demon," I said. "Let's talk about the demon."

"The demon is not the point," Austen said.

"The demon is entirely the point!" Pratchett said.

"The demon is the occasion. The point is how the village responds to the demon. In *Pride and Prejudice*, Wickham is not the point. Wickham is barely a character. He is a mirror held up to the other characters — what they see in him reveals what they want to see, what they need to see, what they cannot afford not to see. Your demon should function identically. He should be — forgive the expression — a perfectly blank surface onto which every character in the village projects their desires."

"But he's a *demon*. He has actual powers. He's actually evil. Wickham was just a cad with good cheekbones. You're talking about capital-E Evil walking into a village and everyone going, 'What lovely manners, would he like some scones?'"

"Yes. That is exactly what I am talking about. And it is exactly what would happen. You know this."

Pratchett opened his mouth, closed it, then opened it again. "I do know this. In *Good Omens* — well. Crowley walks through London and nobody notices he's a demon because he drives a nice car and wears expensive sunglasses. That's Neil's joke as much as mine, but it's true. Evil that dresses well is invisible. Evil that uses the wrong fork at dinner would be detected instantly."

"Now you understand the Regency."

"I've always understood the Regency. I just think it's funnier when there are also pointy hats."

I raised the matter of the risk card. We'd drawn withheld-information — something crucial hidden from the reader or the characters, structurally central.

"Well, obviously," Pratchett said, "the withheld thing is that he's a demon. The village doesn't know. The reader — does the reader know?"

"That's the question," I said. "If the reader knows from the start, it's dramatic irony. If the reader doesn't know—"

"If the reader doesn't know," Austen said, "then the reader is in exactly the position of the village. Charmed. Willing. Complicit. And when the truth arrives — if the truth arrives — the reader must sit with the fact that they, too, were taken in."

Pratchett was shaking his head, but slowly, the way a person shakes their head when they know they're about to agree. "That's cruel. That's really cruel. You want to withhold it from the *reader*?"

"I want the reader to experience what the village experiences. I want the reader to find the curate charming. I want the reader to think, 'What a pleasant young man, what a pity the witches are so suspicious, can they not simply enjoy the improvement in the neighborhood?' I want the reader to be Lizzy Bennet in the first volume — clever, certain, and wrong."

"And the witches are what? The Cassandra figures? The ones who see the truth and can't get anyone to listen?"

"The witches are the Cassandra figures, yes, but Cassandra is boring if she simply points and screams. Cassandra is interesting only if she doubts herself. If the demon is charming enough — if his manners are good enough, if his conversation is intelligent enough, if he fixes the church roof and visits the sick — then even the witches should wonder, for a moment, whether they are mistaken. Whether their suspicion is just prejudice against newcomers. Whether a demon who does good works is distinguishable from a curate who does good works, and if he is not distinguishable, whether the distinction matters."

"It matters," Pratchett said.

"Why?"

"Because—" He stopped. Drummed his fingers on the doily. "Because evil is evil regardless of how polite it is. That's — I believe that. Deeply. Granny Weatherwax believes that. There are things that are wrong and they don't stop being wrong because they buy you a new pew cushion."

"And yet the pew cushion is very comfortable."

"The pew cushion is irrelevant."

"The pew cushion is never irrelevant. The pew cushion is why people go to church. Not God. Not salvation. The cushion, and the company, and the sense that one is a person who goes to church, which is a different thing from being a person who believes in God, though the village sees no difference and would be appalled if you pointed one out."

Pratchett stood up, walked to the window, came back, and sat down. I've seen authors do this before — the physical need to move when an idea is too uncomfortable to sit with.

"You're saying evil wins because it's convenient."

"I'm saying evil doesn't need to win. Evil only needs to be tolerable. Tolerable evil is invisible evil, and invisible evil is the only kind that lasts. Your demons, the ones with the horns and the fire — they get defeated because they're obvious. They stand in the village square and announce themselves. The real ones sit in the pew and pass the collection plate and nobody says a word because saying a word would mean admitting that the collection plate has been handled by something terrible, and then what do you do with the money?"

"You give it back."

"You cannot give back money that has already been spent on the roof. The roof does not care about the moral status of the money. The roof only cares about the rain."

I was writing as fast as I could. The meeting had gone somewhere I hadn't anticipated. The comedy was still there — the image of a demon being socially acceptable because of his income was still genuinely funny — but underneath it, Austen was building something that looked a lot like a theory of complicity, and Pratchett was fighting it not because he disagreed but because he needed the story to leave room for the witches to be heroes.

"The *Wyrd Sisters* element," I said. "Three witches, three temperaments. One who acts, one who thinks, one who — what? Eats?"

"Nanny Ogg doesn't just eat," Pratchett said, but he was smiling. "She's the one who understands people. Granny understands *right and wrong*. Magrat understands *how things ought to be*. Nanny understands *how things are*. And in a village with a demon, the one who understands how things are is the most dangerous, because she's the one who knows that the village will never, ever expel the demon voluntarily."

"Then what use is she?"

"She's the one who knows which battle to fight. Granny will try to defeat evil. Magrat will try to reform it. Nanny will try to arrange things so that evil defeats itself, because she knows that people won't do the right thing on principle but they'll do it out of spite."

Austen was watching him with an expression I couldn't read. It might have been admiration. It might have been the look a chess player gives when the opponent makes a move that is clever but ultimately doomed.

"Your Nanny," she said, "is my narrator."

"What?"

"Free indirect discourse. The voice that sits beside each character and reports what they think while gently, invisibly, demonstrating that what they think is absurd. That voice is not God. That voice is not judgment. That voice is Nanny Ogg. It knows everyone, it likes most of them, it sees through all of them, and it will never, under any circumstances, be the one to say directly that anyone is wrong. It simply arranges the evidence and steps back."

"That's — you're right. Damn it, that's exactly right."

"I am frequently right. It is one of my less endearing qualities."

The conversation about what exactly the witches should withhold — from the village, from the reader, from themselves — went on for another twenty minutes, circling, doubling back, arriving at conclusions and then abandoning them. Pratchett wanted at least one scene where a witch did something unmistakably magical and the village explained it away as a draft or a trick of the light. Austen wanted the demon's true nature to be revealed not through magic but through manners — a moment where his social performance slipped, where he said something that no human of his supposed standing would say, and only one person in the room noticed, and that person immediately doubted their own hearing because doubting one's hearing is easier than rethinking one's dinner invitations.

"The play," Pratchett said suddenly. "We haven't talked about the play."

"The play?"

"*Wyrd Sisters* has a play. The whole plot is about who controls the narrative — the witches, the duke, or the play itself. The play takes on a life of its own. It starts saying things the author didn't write. In our story there should be — not a literal play, maybe, but some kind of performance. Something the village stages. A charity theatrical, a nativity, a — what did Regency villages do?"

"Tableaux vivants," Austen said. "Private theatricals. The village would absolutely stage amateur theatricals, and the curate would absolutely be asked to participate, and he would be excellent, because demons are always excellent at performing humanity, having studied it from outside with the attention that only an outsider can bring."

"And the play reveals something."

"The play reveals everything, but in a form that everyone can pretend is fiction. That is what plays do. That is what they have always done. The truth walks across the stage wearing a costume, and the audience applauds, and goes home, and does not change."

Pratchett was tearing his doily into small strips, which I took as a sign of productive distress. "I don't accept that. I don't accept 'does not change.' Granny Weatherwax wouldn't accept it. The whole point of *Wyrd Sisters* is that the play changes things. It changes the Duke. It changes the kingdom. Stories have power."

"Stories have power over people who are already ready to be changed. Your duke was already guilty. The play gave him permission to collapse. The play did not create the guilt. It merely provided a stage for it."

"And if the village isn't guilty?"

"The village is guilty of comfort. That is a very difficult guilt to stage."

The tea had gone cold. The doily was in ruins. Pratchett was staring at its remains as though they contained a map to somewhere he wasn't sure he wanted to go.

"Here is what I keep coming back to," he said. "The funniest version of this story is the one where the witches are right, the demon is real, and the village defeats evil not through courage or magic but through the same petty social machinery that refused to see evil in the first place. The same forces that protected the demon — politeness, gossip, the horror of making a scene — are turned against him. Not because the village wakes up. Because the demon makes a social error. He uses the wrong form of address. He sits in the wrong pew. He does something unforgivable — not by moral standards but by *social* standards — and the village ejects him with the same cheerful ruthlessness with which it welcomed him."

"That," Austen said, "is the most realistic ending I have ever heard a man in a large hat propose."

"I'm not wearing a hat."

"You are always wearing a hat."

He conceded the point. "But is it enough? The village defeats evil by accident, because evil committed a faux pas. Is that satisfying?"

"It is true. Whether it is satisfying is a question I have never found useful. Satisfying endings are for people who believe the world is a novel. The world is not a novel. The world is a village where everyone has opinions about the new curate and nobody—"
