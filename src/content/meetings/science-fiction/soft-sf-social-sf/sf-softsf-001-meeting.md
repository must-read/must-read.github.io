The café was Becky's idea. Ursula would have preferred the living room of a rented house somewhere in the coastal range — she said as much, twice — but Becky wanted noise around her, the clatter of other people's conversations layered underneath. "I think better when I'm not the only one thinking," she said, and Ursula gave her a look that was almost fond.

I arrived late, which I blamed on traffic but was actually nerves. They'd already ordered. Ursula had black coffee in a ceramic mug she might have brought from home. Becky had something with oat milk and a name I didn't catch. I sat down with my laptop open, which both of them eyed like it was a weapon.

"I'm not going to record anything," I said. "I just think with my hands."

"Everyone says that," Ursula said. "Then the hands do the thinking and the mind wanders."

I closed the laptop.

"So," Becky said. She was sitting cross-legged in her chair, which I found immediately disarming. "A colony. Three generations isolated. They've abolished private property, privacy, and fixed identity." She paused. "That's a lot of abolition for one settlement."

"It has to be all three," Ursula said. "Remove property but keep privacy and you get monasticism. Remove privacy but keep fixed identity and you get a surveillance state with extra steps. The radicalism is in the combination — you cannot maintain a fixed self when nothing around you is fixed. The colony has to have understood that."

"Understood it or stumbled into it?" Becky asked.

"That matters enormously."

"It does. Because a colony that designed all three simultaneously — that's ideologues. And ideologues are brittle. But a colony that started with one principle and the others followed organically, that's a living system. I'd rather write about a living system."

Ursula nodded slowly, which I was learning meant she was conceding something but hadn't decided how much. "The founders could have been ideologues," she said. "But three generations in, the children's children don't remember the ideology. They just live."

That was the first moment I felt something click. The colony wouldn't explain itself. It wouldn't have a manifesto framed on the wall. It would just be a place where people lived differently, and the explanations would be something the anthropologist brought with her — her need to categorize, to understand through frameworks.

"The anthropologist," I said. "I want her to be good at her job."

"Obviously," Ursula said.

"No, I mean — I don't want her to be the naive outsider who gets schooled. She's genuinely skilled. Her observations are accurate. Her framework is sophisticated. And it still can't hold what she's seeing."

Becky leaned forward. "Yes. Because the failure isn't intellectual — it's experiential. She can describe what they're doing. She can even admire it in the abstract. But she can't *feel* why someone would choose it, and that gap between understanding and feeling is where the story lives."

"The anthropologist's field report as narrative frame," Ursula said, and something in her voice shifted — a kind of proprietary attention, as though she were reclaiming territory. "I spent decades working with that form. The risk is that it becomes a lecture. The field report must fracture. The professional voice must crack."

"When does it crack?" I asked.

"Not when she witnesses something beautiful. That's too easy — the outsider moved by alien grace. It cracks when she realizes she's been observed. When she understands that her attempt to study them has been, from their perspective, a form of participation. That her careful distance was never distance at all."

Becky was quiet for a moment, stirring her drink. "I want to be careful with something," she said. "The colony has to work. Not as a utopia — I'm not interested in utopia — but as a functional place where people are, on the whole, okay. Because if the colony is secretly miserable, or if there's a dark underside that the anthropologist eventually uncovers, then we're just writing the same story everyone writes about communes. The one that says: nice idea, but human nature."

"You want the colony to be genuinely better?" I asked.

"I want the colony to have solved some problems and created others, and for the new problems to be interesting rather than damning. I want the reader to finish the story unsure whether they'd want to live there. Not because it's dystopian, but because the costs are real and the benefits are real and neither cancels the other out."

Ursula set her mug down with a precise motion. "The colony should not be better. Or worse. It should be *different*. The anthropologist's crisis isn't 'their way is superior' — that's conversion narrative, which we agreed to resist. Her crisis is that their way is coherent. It works on its own terms. And her society's way is also coherent and also works on its own terms. What breaks her is the realization that her terms are not universal terms. That what she called 'selfhood' is local."

"That's devastating enough," Becky said softly. "You don't need the colony to win. You just need it to be real."

There was a long pause. A barista dropped something metal and everyone in the café looked up for a second, then looked down again. Ursula watched the barista with interest, as though filing the moment away.

"I keep thinking about kitchens," Becky said.

Ursula raised an eyebrow.

"In a place with no private property and no fixed identity — what does a kitchen look like? Do people cook together? Do they take turns? Is there a rotation, or is it more fluid than that? Because you can tell me about the abolition of selfhood and I'll nod along, but show me how five people negotiate dinner with no concept of 'my turn' or 'my recipe' or 'my preference,' and I'll understand the society in my body."

"The kitchen is where the anthropologist's categories fail first," I said, suddenly seeing it. "She tries to map roles — who decides, who defers — and the roles keep shifting. Not rotating. Shifting. Like the social structure is liquid."

"Be careful with the liquid metaphor," Ursula said. "It suggests formlessness. A society without fixed identity is not a society without structure. Water has structure. A river has banks."

"What's the structure?" I asked.

"I don't know yet. That's the work."

Becky laughed — not at Ursula, but at the precision of it. "She's right that we can't figure it out sitting here. The structure has to emerge from the writing. But I'd push back on one thing. You said the anthropologist's crisis is realizing her terms aren't universal. I agree. But I think there's a second layer. I think part of what undoes her is that the colony is *warm*."

Ursula's expression didn't change, but something behind it did. "Warm," she repeated.

"People touch each other casually. They share meals without negotiation. Children are everyone's responsibility and no one's burden. There's an ease to daily life that the anthropologist doesn't have a theoretical framework for, because her training treats ease as either false consciousness or the product of coercion. She can't categorize genuine communal contentment. It doesn't fit."

"You're describing what I'd call grace," Ursula said, carefully. "The colony has a kind of social grace. But grace can become smug. Grace can become exclusionary — we have this beautiful thing, and you, outsider, don't understand it."

"That's where the colony's own limitations show," I said. "They've solved the problems of property and privacy and fixed identity. But they haven't necessarily solved the problem of the stranger. The anthropologist might be the first real outsider in a generation. And a society built on radical openness might not actually know what to do with someone who is genuinely, fundamentally *other*."

Ursula looked at me for a long moment. "That's not bad," she said, which I later learned was high praise.

"It creates a symmetry I'm uncomfortable with, though," Becky said. "Both societies failing at the same thing — accommodating difference. The anthropologist can't accommodate the colony's way of being; the colony can't accommodate the anthropologist's. That's too neat."

"Then make it asymmetrical," Ursula said. "One side fails differently. Or one side fails harder. Or one side recognizes its failure and the other doesn't."

"The colony recognizes it," Becky said, with sudden conviction. "They see her struggling and they don't know how to help. They try — they include her, they extend their warmth — but their inclusion keeps assuming she wants what they have. They can't imagine that her separateness is meaningful to her. That privacy isn't a wound."

I was scribbling on a napkin now. Ursula noticed and didn't comment.

"So the anthropologist has something the colony doesn't value but can't take from her," I said. "Her interiority. Her private self. The thing they abolished is the thing she's organized her entire identity around."

"And the thing she values most about herself — her trained observer's distance, her analytical separateness — is precisely what they experience as her deficit," Becky added. "They feel sorry for her. Not in a condescending way. In a genuine, confused way. The way you'd feel sorry for someone who refused to eat at a table with others. Not because eating alone is wrong, but because it seems lonely."

"The pity might be worse than hostility," Ursula said. "You can argue with hostility. You can't argue with someone who feels sorry for you without sounding defensive."

"She does sound defensive," I said. "In her field reports. That's where the voice cracks — not in a dramatic confession, but in the slow creep of defensiveness into what should be neutral observation. She starts justifying. Explaining why her society does things the way it does. And the reader realizes before she does that the field report has become an argument she's having with herself."

"Don't make her a fool," Ursula said sharply. "She notices the defensiveness. She's a trained observer. She watches herself doing it. And that second-order awareness — watching herself fail to be objective — is its own particular kind of hell."

Becky nodded. "She's honest about it in the reports?"

"She tries to be. But honesty about your own bias doesn't eliminate the bias. It just gives you a front-row seat."

There was a shift in the conversation then — one of those moments where everyone has said what they can say abstractly and the next step requires actual prose. Ursula signaled for more coffee. Becky uncrossed and recrossed her legs. I picked up my napkin and tried to read my own handwriting.

"I want to ask about the ending," I said.

"No," Ursula said.

"Not what happens. The emotional register. Does she leave?"

"She has to leave," Becky said. "A story where the outsider stays is a conversion story. But the leaving has to be complicated. Not triumphant return to her own society. Not heartbroken departure. Something stranger."

"She leaves and she's fine," Ursula said. "That's the most disturbing ending. She goes home. She writes up her findings. She publishes. She gets tenure, or whatever the equivalent is. She's fine. And the reader is the one who's not fine, because the reader knows what she lost by being fine. The reader knows that 'fine' is itself a kind of catastrophe."

"That's bleak," Becky said.

"Is it? Or is it honest? Most of us encounter genuinely different ways of living and we don't convert. We go home. We adjust. We are fine."

"I think there's a version between conversion and fine," Becky said, and her voice had an edge now, a pushback that felt earned. "She leaves. She's mostly fine. But there's a residue. A new habit she can't explain. A flinch when someone says 'mine.' A moment at a dinner party where she reaches for a dish without asking and then catches herself. Small things. Things that don't add up to transformation but do add up to — what? Contamination isn't the right word."

"Influence," I offered.

"Influence implies intention. This is more like — a stain. Something that won't wash out but also won't spread. She carries a piece of the colony's logic in her body, not in her mind. Her mind has already categorized and filed the experience. Her body hasn't."

Ursula was quiet. I thought she might disagree — she'd been so insistent on the rigor of the field report frame, on the anthropologist's trained competence. But instead she said, "The body remembers what the mind files away. That's true. I've experienced it. And the field report wouldn't capture it, because the field report is mind. So there'd need to be something else. A margin. A crack in the form."

"A personal log she keeps separate from the reports," I said. "Something she tells herself is just notes. But the voice in it is different."

"Maybe," Ursula said. "Or maybe the crack is in the reports themselves and she doesn't see it. Maybe the reader sees what she can't — the way her language softens across the reports, the way the clinical vocabulary starts failing, the way she uses 'we' once and then corrects it."

That image — the single "we" she corrects — landed in the center of the table like something physical. We all sat with it.

"I'm worried about the colony being too homogeneous," Becky said, changing direction. "If everyone in the colony is the same kind of happy, the same kind of fluid, then it's not a society. It's a cult with good PR. There need to be people in the colony who struggle with it. Someone who wants more than their share. Someone who mourns the idea of a fixed name. Not dissidents — just humans."

"An old woman who keeps a box of things," Ursula said. "Not valuable things. A worn button. A stone from somewhere. She doesn't own them — the concept doesn't apply. But they stay in her space. They don't circulate. No one mentions it."

"And the anthropologist notices," I said.

"The anthropologist notices and wants desperately to ask about it and knows that asking would impose her categories on something that might not survive the categorization."

Becky was smiling now, the kind of smile that meant an idea was taking root. "The box of things. That's the whole colony in miniature. It works, but it's human. It has seams."

Outside, the light had changed — that Pacific Northwest gray softening toward something amber. We'd been talking for over two hours. My coffee was cold. Ursula had begun arranging sugar packets into a pattern on the table, which I suspected was unconscious.

"One more thing," Becky said. "The found-family question. The colony is essentially one massive found family. But the anthropologist already has a family — a real one, wherever she came from. A mother, maybe. A partner. The pull isn't toward the colony as replacement family. The pull is toward a way of being in relation that her own family never achieved. That's harder to write. It's not 'I found my people.' It's 'I found a grammar for what I always wanted and couldn't say.'"

Ursula started to respond but stopped. She picked up a sugar packet, turned it over, set it back down in a different position. "That's the sentence I'd put in her personal log," she said finally. "Not those words. But that feeling. A grammar she didn't know existed."

"And she still leaves," I said.

"And she still leaves," Becky confirmed.

"Knowing the grammar."

"Knowing the grammar. Unable to speak it at home. Not because anyone stops her, but because there's no one to speak it with."

Ursula pushed her chair back slightly. Not leaving, but shifting weight. "You'll have to be careful," she said to me. "The anthropologist will want to explain the colony to the reader. She's a scholar — explanation is her gift and her habit. But the colony should never be fully explained. The reader should understand it the way you understand a country you've visited for two weeks. Partially. Through anecdote. With gaps."

"Gaps make readers uncomfortable," I said.

"Good. The anthropologist is uncomfortable. Why should the reader be spared?"

Becky laughed again, and this time it was at me, gently. "You want the reader to be comfortable?"

"I want the reader to stay."

"They'll stay," Becky said. "People stay for warmth. Put warmth on the page — real warmth, the kind that comes from specific bodies in specific rooms doing specific things — and readers will tolerate enormous discomfort. They'll follow you anywhere if you make them feel the kitchen."

Ursula lined up one final sugar packet and stood to refill her mug. Over her shoulder she said, almost to herself, "The kitchen and the field report. The body and the mind. Two frames, and neither sufficient."

I stared at my napkin. I'd written, in increasingly illegible script: *kitchen / field report / the single "we" she corrects / a box of things nobody mentions / grammar without speakers / fine is catastrophe*

None of it was a story yet. All of it was the soil a story could grow from. Which is, I think, exactly what Ursula meant it to be.
