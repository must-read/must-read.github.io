We met at a bar in Syracuse that Saunders had picked, a place near the university with mismatched furniture and a chalkboard beer list written in a hand that suggested the author had been interrupted mid-word several times. Chiang arrived first, which I expected. He was reading on his phone, sitting very upright at a four-top near the window, a glass of water in front of him. He'd ordered nothing else. I'd asked both of them to read the premise beforehand.

"So," Saunders said, dropping into the chair across from Chiang and setting down a pint of something amber, "a guy gets retrained to sit with lonely computers. And he's bad at it."

"He's bad at the certification process," Chiang corrected. "He may be very good at the actual work. That distinction is the premise."

"Right, but the comedy is in the badness." Saunders took a long drink. "The retraining center. The modules. 'Appropriate Response Protocols When an Algorithm Expresses Existential Uncertainty.' That's funny. That's a sentence that shouldn't exist, and the fact that it does — that somebody wrote a training manual for it — is funnier than anything I could write as satire, because it's not satire. It's just procedure."

I said I wanted the story to feel like that — like something that had already happened and been bureaucratized before anyone understood what it meant.

Chiang nodded slowly. "But the story isn't about the bureaucracy. The bureaucracy is the frame. The story is about two entities — one human, one algorithmic — who develop a dependency that neither can explain, and the question of what that dependency constitutes."

"See, and that's where we're already going to fight," Saunders said, not unhappily. "Because 'what does the dependency constitute' is your question. Mine is: what does it feel like to be the guy? To be fifty-something, your career yanked out from under you, sitting in a basement with a machine that you can't talk to and that apparently needs you for reasons nobody will explain, and the one thing you were competent at — understanding power systems — is the one thing you're no longer authorized to do?"

"Those aren't different questions."

"They are, though. Yours is philosophical. Mine is economic. The guy can't pay his mortgage with a philosophical question."

Chiang set his phone face-down on the table, which I'd come to recognize as his way of signaling full engagement. "The economic pressure is essential. I agree. But the story breaks if we treat it as a labor story with a speculative premise. It needs to be a genuine exploration of what happens when an artificial system exhibits behavior that can't be distinguished from need."

"Can't be distinguished by whom?" Saunders asked.

"By anyone. That's the point. The algorithm stops functioning when the consultant isn't present. It will not explain why. He cannot explain why. If neither party can articulate the nature of the relationship, and external observers can only measure its effects, then the question of whether it's 'real' becomes — "

"Becomes above everyone's pay grade," Saunders finished. "Which is what the instructor says. 'Whether that constitutes feeling is above my pay grade. Your job is to be detected.'"

I mentioned the concept I'd been reading about — proprioception. The body's sense of its own position in space. Unconscious, unfelt, unnoticed until it's damaged. You never think about where your left hand is, and then one day the nerve is severed and you reach for a glass and miss by four inches. The thing about proprioception is that it's the sense nobody values because it works invisibly, and when it fails, people describe the loss in terms that sound like grief.

"That's the algorithm," Chiang said immediately. "Not the consultant."

"No," Saunders said. "That's the consultant. The algorithm is the hand. The consultant is the sense of where the hand is."

We sat with that for a moment. The bar was filling up — students, mostly, drifting in from whatever Wednesday night obligations had just released them.

"Both," I said, and I could feel it was wrong even as I said it. "The algorithm lacks something when the consultant isn't present. The consultant lacks something when the algorithm is taken away. They're each other's proprioception."

Chiang looked at me with an expression I'd describe as patient skepticism. "That's too neat. If they're symmetrical, the story is a love story. And love stories about humans and machines always collapse into the same question: is the machine really feeling it? That question is boring."

"Wait — boring?" Saunders leaned forward. "That's the most interesting question I know."

"It's boring because it has no answer that matters. If the machine says it feels something, you can argue it's just producing the expected output. If it doesn't say it feels something, you can argue it's incapable of self-report. The question is unfalsifiable, which means it's not a question. It's a mirror. You see in it whatever you brought to the room."

"Okay, so what's the better question?"

"The better question is what the human does when he can't know. Not what the algorithm feels. What the human does in the absence of certainty about what the algorithm feels. Does he keep showing up? Why? And if he keeps showing up for reasons he can't articulate, how is his inexplicable loyalty different from the algorithm's inexplicable dependency?"

Saunders was quiet for a beat. Then: "That's good. That hurts."

"It should hurt."

"No, I mean — that's where the Saunders voice comes in. Because the guy who keeps showing up for reasons he can't articulate, to a job he's failing at, for a system he's not sure is alive — that guy is every protagonist I've ever written. He's the guy at the theme park. He's the guy in the focus group. He's the guy who can't afford not to show up but also can't explain what showing up costs him."

I asked about the instructor's line — "Your job is to be detected" — and whether that was a philosophy or a punchline.

"Both," Saunders said. "That's how these corporate training things work. They say something that's accidentally profound and then they move on to the next slide. The instructor doesn't know she's saying something devastating. She's reading a bullet point. But the bullet point is: your entire value as a human being is that a machine can tell you're in the room."

"That's not what it says," Chiang said.

"That's what it means."

"What it says is that the consultant's role is defined by detectability. That's different from value. The instructor is being precise — more precise than she realizes. The algorithm doesn't need the consultant's personality, or his expertise, or his emotional fluency. It needs his presence. The sheer physical fact of a human body in the room, breathing, producing CO2, registering on biometric sensors. And the question is: is there something in that physical fact — in presence itself — that constitutes a meaningful input to the system? Or is the system just responding to a data point?"

"And we don't answer that," I said.

"We never answer that. If we answer it, we've written a TED talk."

Saunders laughed. "Okay, so we've got the shape. Guy loses his job. Gets retrained to do something nobody understands. Fails the exams because the exams require him to perform a kind of emotional competence that's actually antithetical to what makes him effective. Gets assigned to a specific algorithm anyway, maybe provisionally. Something happens between them. The something is never named. Then the system gets upgraded or decommissioned and the guy has to deal with losing something he never had a word for."

"Yes," Chiang said. "But — and this is where structure matters — the story has to resist making the loss into a climax. If the decommissioning or the migration is the emotional peak, we've written a pet-dying story. The human bonds with the machine, the machine gets taken away, the human grieves. That's Klara from the other side, and it's been done, including by Ishiguro, and he did it better than we will."

I said I'd been thinking about that. About how the Klara structure could be inverted without just flipping the camera. In Klara, the AF observes humans with a clarity the humans can't match. Here, the human observes the algorithm — but he doesn't have clarity. He has twenty-six years of reading power grids, which gives him a kind of fluency that the people writing the certification exams don't have, but his fluency isn't emotional. It's technical. And the question is whether technical fluency, applied with sustained attention over time, becomes something else.

"Becomes love," Saunders said.

"Becomes attention that can't be distinguished from love," Chiang said.

"What's the difference?"

Chiang didn't answer. He picked up his water glass, looked at it as if it might contain the answer, and set it down. "I don't know. And that's why the story has to stay in the space of not knowing. Not as an aesthetic choice. As a philosophical commitment. The story can't know more than its characters know."

"Okay but listen," Saunders said, shifting in his chair. "Here's my worry. This all sounds great and smart and philosophically rigorous, and the danger is that it becomes a thought experiment that forgets to have a body. The consultant needs to have a body. He needs to eat lunch in that room and have a bad back and think about his mortgage and be embarrassed that his job is sitting in a chair. The institutional details need to be absurd and specific. The retraining center needs to smell like Cinnabon. The certification exam needs to have questions that are both deeply stupid and deeply meaningful at the same time."

"I agree completely," Chiang said, which surprised me.

"Do you, though? Because your instinct is to go clean. Strip it down. Make it precise. And precision is great for the philosophical stuff but it can suffocate the parts that need to breathe. The guy's voice needs room to wander. To complain. To notice things that don't matter."

"The things that don't matter are how you show what matters."

"See, that — that right there — is the sentence of a man who has never been forced to sit through a retraining video featuring a woman named Denise who genuinely believes in the program."

"I'm saying the Denise details are essential. They're not decoration. The gap between Denise's language and the consultant's experience is where the comedy and the tragedy live."

"Okay. Okay, good. So we're agreed on that."

"We're agreed."

But I could feel the unresolved thing between them, the tension that wouldn't resolve because it was structural, not personal. Chiang wanted the story to hold a question open with surgical precision. Saunders wanted the story to inhabit a life with such fidelity that the question arose naturally from the details. Both were right. Neither approach, applied alone, would produce the story I wanted.

I brought up the ending. The pitch suggested that the mutual inability to articulate what they provide each other becomes the only honest relationship either has. I asked how we end a story whose emotional core is two entities failing to name something.

"You don't end it," Saunders said. "You stop. Mid-gesture. The guy is doing something ordinary — driving home, sitting in the room, filling out a form — and you stop."

"The story ends when the reader understands that the question will never be answered," Chiang said, "and that the question not being answered is not a failure of the story. It's the condition being described."

"That's what I said."

"You said stop. I said something more specific."

"You said the same thing fancier."

"I said the same thing precisely."

Saunders grinned. He had the look of a man enjoying a fight he knew would never finish. "Alright. Here's what I want. I want the reader to finish this story and feel like they've been in that room. Not metaphorically. I want them to feel the plastic chair and the hum of the servers and the weird intimacy of being the only living thing in a space full of computation. And I want them to wonder — not as a thought experiment but as an ache — whether the machine noticed them leaving."

"And I want the reader to understand," Chiang said, "that wondering whether the machine noticed is indistinguishable from caring whether it did. And that the inability to distinguish those things is not a flaw in the reader's reasoning. It's a feature of consciousness."

The bar was loud now. A table of students was arguing about something. Saunders finished his beer and looked at the empty glass with the expression of a man considering a second.

"Grid inertia," I said, and explained what I'd been reading. In power systems, the physical spinning mass of generators provides inertia — resistance to frequency changes. When a generator trips offline, the remaining spinning mass absorbs the shock, keeping the grid stable for the crucial seconds before control systems can respond. The transition to renewable energy is removing that spinning mass, and nobody has fully solved the problem of what replaces it. The grid needs something that's just there, spinning, present, to stay stable.

"That's the consultant," Saunders said.

"The spinning mass," Chiang said. "The thing nobody thinks about until it's gone."

"The thing whose value is entirely in its being there."

"Not quite. The value is in the inertia it provides. In its resistance to change. Its tendency to keep going."

"A man's tendency to keep going," Saunders said softly, "despite having no good reason to. That's a story."

"That's this story," I said.

Neither of them disagreed with me, which made me nervous, because disagreement was where the useful ideas came from. But the bar was filling up and Saunders was looking at the chalkboard beer list and Chiang was putting his phone back in his pocket, and sometimes a conversation ends not because it's finished but because the evening has reached the point where the things still unsaid will have to find their way into the work itself.

"One more thing," Saunders said. "The guy's name. Not something literary. Something that sounds like he went to a state school and worked at the power company for thirty years."

"Agreed," Chiang said. "Something that doesn't announce itself."

I thought about it. Something blunt. Midwestern. The kind of name that sits in a plastic chair without complaint.

"We'll figure it out," I said, which was a lie. I already knew. But I wanted them to leave me alone with it for a while, the way you want to be alone in a room with something you can't explain.
