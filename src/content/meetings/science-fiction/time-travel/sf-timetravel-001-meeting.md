Bradbury wanted to meet outside. Of course he did. He specified a porch, and when I couldn't arrange a porch he accepted a bench — a particular bench, wooden, slightly warped, in a park that reminded him of a park in Waukegan, Illinois, that may or may not still exist. The grass was overgrown. The light was the light of a weekday afternoon in late September, which Bradbury called "the best light there is, because it knows it's leaving."

Chiang arrived exactly on time, carrying nothing. He sat on the far end of the bench, leaving room between them that I would spend the next three hours occupying and vacating, depending on the temperature of the conversation. He wore a collared shirt buttoned to the throat. He looked at the grass as if measuring it.

"You brought a recorder," he said to me.

"A notebook."

"A notebook is a recorder with worse compression."

Bradbury laughed. It was a quick, boyish laugh, the laugh of someone who finds the world funnier than it has any right to be. "I like him," he said, pointing at Chiang. "He talks like a machine that's homesick."

This was, I think, the only time Bradbury said something that made Chiang visibly uncomfortable. Chiang shifted on the bench. Not in offense — something closer to being accurately described by a stranger and not knowing where to put the feeling.

I said I wanted to talk about time travel. About a love story inside of time travel. About what happens to a relationship when the two people in it experience time differently — not metaphorically, not as a stand-in for growing apart, but as a physical, structural fact. One person is unstuck. The other is not.

"Billy Pilgrim," Bradbury said immediately.

"And Henry DeTamble," I said. "The Time Traveler's Wife. The husband who keeps disappearing."

"I never read it."

"I did," Chiang said. "The premise is rigorous in one direction and sentimental in the other. The mechanism is genetic — a real condition, almost, rendered with enough clinical specificity to be convincing. But the emotional architecture relies on the reader believing that love is the force that keeps pulling him back to her. That his displacement isn't random. That the universe has a preference."

"Doesn't it?" Bradbury said.

Chiang looked at him. The September light did its thing between them, illuminating particles of something — pollen, dust, the raw material of a disagreement that would last the rest of the afternoon.

"No," Chiang said. "The universe has no preference. That's the point. That's what makes it interesting to write about."

"That's what makes it interesting to think about," Bradbury said. "What makes it interesting to write about is the opposite. A boy standing in a yard at dusk, knowing summer is going to end. He doesn't need the physics. He needs the feeling of the physics. He needs to feel time moving through him like wind through a screen door."

"But that's metaphor. Time doesn't move through anyone. People move through time — one second per second, in one direction. If we're writing a time travel story, the question isn't what time feels like. The question is what time does. What are the rules. What are the consequences of breaking them."

"The consequences of breaking them is the feeling," Bradbury said, and for a moment both of them stopped, and I could hear the park — some kind of bird repeating itself, a dog barking two streets away, the distant mechanical hum of a city that didn't care about any of this.

I said: "Vonnegut broke the rules by refusing to have rules. Billy Pilgrim doesn't choose when he travels. He doesn't control it. He doesn't understand it. He just — comes unstuck. And the novel's structure mirrors that. You get the firebombing of Dresden between a scene in an optometrist's office and a scene on Tralfamadore. Everything is simultaneous."

"Everything is simultaneous," Chiang repeated, and his voice had changed. Harder. More present. "That's a narrative strategy, not a physics. Vonnegut was solving an emotional problem — how do you write about something that destroyed you — by inventing a structural solution. The Tralfamadorians don't experience time linearly. They see all moments at once. Vonnegut needed that because he couldn't put Dresden in order. The trauma wouldn't sequence."

Bradbury pulled a dandelion from the grass beside the bench. He didn't pick it so much as accept it from the ground, the way you'd take something handed to you by a child.

"That's right," he said. "And that's why it works. Because the structure isn't clever. It's wounded. The book is broken the way Billy is broken. If you're going to shatter your chronology, you'd better have a reason, and the reason had better be damage."

I wrote this down so hard the pen tore the paper.

"So our story," I said. "A love story with shattered chronology. But Chiang, you want rules — a mechanism that the displacement obeys, even if the characters don't understand it. And Bradbury, you want the displacement to feel like something. To taste like something."

"It should taste like a specific October," Bradbury said. "It should taste like the October you're remembering right now, whatever that is. The reader's October, not ours."

"I don't think about taste when I think about time," Chiang said.

"That's your problem."

"It's my method. The story I care about is the one where the rules of temporal displacement create consequences that are logically inevitable and emotionally unbearable. You set up the mechanism. You show what it permits. You show what it forbids. And then you let the characters run into the wall of what's forbidden and watch what happens to them."

"You want a tragedy of physics."

"I want a tragedy that couldn't exist without the physics. The specific physics. Not generic time travel — not 'a man who travels through time.' A specific mechanism with specific constraints, and those constraints are what shape the love story. The love story is a function of the constraints."

Bradbury turned the dandelion in his fingers. He was looking at it the way people in his stories look at small objects — as though it contained a world, or a memory of a world, and the act of looking at it was the only way to keep that world alive.

"Function," he said. "You want love to be a function."

"I want love to be shaped by the physics the way a river is shaped by the geology it runs through. The river doesn't choose its path. The geology doesn't care about the river. But the specific river, in the specific canyon, is beautiful because of the specifics. Not despite them."

"That's — " Bradbury stopped. He blew on the dandelion. The seeds scattered in a way that felt rehearsed by forty million years of evolution. "That's actually quite lovely, Ted. But you're wrong about one thing. The geology does care about the river. Give it enough time, and the river changes the stone."

I asked about the woman. The one who stays. In Niffenegger's novel, Clare waits. She is the fixed point. She met Henry when she was six and he was thirty-six, and she spent her whole life in some sense already married to a man who hadn't met her yet. The waiting is the novel.

"I don't want her to wait," Chiang said. "Waiting is passive. I want her to understand the mechanism. I want her to be the one who figures out the pattern — when he comes, when he goes, what determines the intervals. She becomes a scientist of her own loss."

"A scientist of her own loss," Bradbury repeated, and something about the phrase landed wrong for him. I could see it — the way his jaw set, the way the dandelion stem bent between his fingers. "That's cold. That's the cold version of the story. A woman with a spreadsheet, tracking the appearances of the man she loves."

"Not a spreadsheet. An understanding. A map of the intervals. She begins to see the pattern before he does, because she's the one who stays and he's the one who leaves, and staying is a kind of research that leaving can never be."

"Staying is not research. Staying is staying. It's three in the morning and the bed is empty and you're listening for a sound that might be him and might be the house settling and you can't tell the difference anymore. That's not science. That's a person alone with the sound of a house."

The bird was still going. The same four notes, over and over, like a motif in a piece of music that doesn't know how to develop.

"Both," I said, and regretted it, the same way I always regret trying to synthesize two positions that are genuinely opposed. "Can she be both? Can she be someone who tracks the pattern because tracking the pattern is how she survives the three a.m. emptiness? The science as coping mechanism. Not cold — desperate."

Chiang considered this. He does this thing where he looks at a spot on the ground about four feet in front of him and his eyes go slightly unfocused, and you can see the idea being assembled behind his face, each component tested for structural integrity before he speaks.

"Desperate is fine," he said. "Desperate is better than cold. But the pattern she discovers has to be real. Not a comfort she's invented. The mechanism has to be there in the physics, and she has to find it, and finding it has to change what she's able to do. Not solve the problem — there is no solving this — but change the nature of the problem."

"From what to what?"

"From 'will he come back' to 'when will he come back.' And then, eventually, to 'what will he remember when he does.'"

Bradbury put the dandelion stem down. He laid it on the bench between them like a small offering, or a boundary marker.

"Memory," he said. "Now we're somewhere. Because time travel isn't really about time. Not for the reader. The reader has never traveled through time. The reader has remembered. And remembering is the time travel we actually know — you're in your kitchen and then you're eleven years old and your grandfather is showing you how to tie a fly, and the kitchen and the river exist at the same time, and you can't choose which one to be in."

"That's not time travel. That's neurology."

"It's the only time travel the story can rely on the reader having experienced. Everything else is hypothetical. But memory — the way memory breaks chronology, the way you can be ambushed by a smell and suddenly you're six years old standing in a room that doesn't exist anymore — that's the emotional substrate. The physics you build has to rhyme with that. Otherwise it's just a puzzle."

Chiang didn't answer immediately. He was doing the ground-staring thing again. I waited. Bradbury waited. The dog had stopped barking. The bird had not.

"Rhyme," Chiang said finally. "You want the physics to rhyme with memory."

"I want the physics to make the reader feel something they recognize. Something that's already in their body. You can give me the most elegant temporal mechanics in the history of science fiction and if I don't feel the loss — the specific, physical, gut-level loss of standing in a room where someone used to be — then you've written an essay, not a story."

"Some of my best stories are essays."

"I know. And they're brilliant. And they're cold."

The September light had shifted. It was later now. The shadows of the trees had elongated across the grass in a way that Bradbury was watching with an attention that felt almost predatory, as if he were committing the angle of the light to some internal archive for later use.

"They're not cold," Chiang said, and this was the only time during the meeting that I heard something in his voice that might have been hurt. "They're precise. Precision is not the absence of feeling. Precision is a way of honoring the feeling by refusing to exaggerate it."

Bradbury looked at him. A long look. The kind of look that in a Bradbury story would be followed by a paragraph about what it means to see another person clearly, in the particular light of a particular afternoon, and to recognize in them something you hadn't expected to find.

"All right," Bradbury said. "All right. Precision as honor. I can work with that. But then the precise thing — the mechanism, the physics, whatever you want to call it — has to be capable of producing moments that the precision can't contain. Moments where the math breaks and something else comes through. Not magic. Not sentimentality. Something the mechanism allows but didn't predict."

"Emergent properties," Chiang said.

"Is that what you call it when a man sees his wife's face and can't remember which version of her he's looking at? When he's standing in their kitchen and he doesn't know if it's Tuesday or if it's twelve years ago and she hasn't met him yet?"

"If the mechanism is well-designed, yes. That's an emergent property of the constraints."

"God," Bradbury said, and laughed again, but differently this time. "You'd put 'grief' in a footnote and label it 'emergent property of temporal constraint system' and mean it as a compliment."

"I would."

"I know you would. And the horrible thing is, you might be right. Grief might be exactly that. The emergent property of loving someone inside a system that won't let you keep them."

I said something about the ending. I shouldn't have, because neither of them wanted to talk about endings — Chiang because he believes endings are a function of the premises and shouldn't be decided in advance, Bradbury because he believes endings should arrive the way weather arrives, suddenly and with a smell.

"Don't plan the ending," Chiang said.

"Never plan the ending," Bradbury said.

This was the only thing they agreed on all afternoon without qualification, which meant I couldn't use it.

I asked instead about the Tralfamadorian problem — Vonnegut's aliens who see all of time at once and are therefore incapable of mourning. If all moments exist simultaneously, and death is just one moment among infinite moments, then grief is irrational. The Tralfamadorians look at a corpse and think: well, at this particular moment the person is dead, but at plenty of other moments they're alive. So it goes.

"That's Vonnegut's defense mechanism," Bradbury said. "He built an entire alien civilization to justify not feeling what Dresden made him feel. 'So it goes' is the scar tissue over the wound. The flatness of the prose in those passages — it's not deadpan. It's numbing. He's numbing himself with his own technique."

"The technique is the content," Chiang said. "You can't separate 'so it goes' from the structure of the novel. The flat affect and the shattered chronology are the same gesture. He broke the narrative because he couldn't tell it straight, and the brokenness is the meaning. If he'd written it as a conventional war novel — beginning, middle, end, the horror of February 1945 — it would have been a lesser book."

"It would have been a different book."

"A lesser one."

"You don't know that."

"I know that the structure of Slaughterhouse-Five is inseparable from its emotional power. You couldn't reassemble it in order and keep the same effect. The way Billy jumps — the way you're reading about his daughter's wedding and then you're in the meat locker under the slaughterhouse and then you're on Tralfamadore — that's not a gimmick. That's how trauma organizes experience. And if our story is about love under temporal displacement, then the structure should reflect what temporal displacement does to a person's ability to narrate their own life."

Bradbury was quiet. He was holding the dandelion stem again — he must have picked it back up without my noticing — and turning it between his thumb and forefinger the way a person turns a key they're not sure they want to use.

"A person who can't narrate their own life," he said. "That's who we're writing about. Someone who has a love story but can't tell it in order because time won't let them. And the woman — the one who stays — she can tell it in order, but her version is full of gaps. Absences the shape of a person."

"Absences the shape of a person," I repeated, because some phrases deserve to be said twice.

"The question is whether the reader experiences the story from the fractured perspective or the gapped one," Chiang said. "Or both. Alternating. His chapters are shattered. Hers are linear but incomplete. The two structures comment on each other."

"Now you're planning," Bradbury said. "You just told me not to plan."

"I told you not to plan the ending. Structure isn't the ending. Structure is the instrument."

"Structure is the instrument," Bradbury said, in a tone that made it clear he was filing this phrase somewhere between useful and irritating.

The light was going. Not gone — September doesn't give up its light easily — but going, in the way that is itself a kind of time travel, the gradual shift from afternoon to not-afternoon that you don't notice until someone points it out or until a shadow crosses your foot that wasn't there before. Bradbury noticed. He noticed the way a person notices who has spent a lifetime writing about light as a measure of loss.

"Can I say something about smell?" he said.

"You're going to say something about smell regardless."

"The house they share. It smells like something. Cinnamon, maybe, or old wood, or rain on warm concrete — something specific. And when he travels, when he disappears, the smell is what stays. The smell is the evidence. She walks into a room and the smell tells her how long he's been gone. Fresh: he just left. Faded: it's been weeks. Absent: she can't remember anymore, and that's when she starts to lose him for real."

Chiang opened his mouth, and I expected him to say something about smell being a chemical process, about olfactory memory residing in the amygdala, about the imprecision of using sensory cues as temporal markers. Instead he said: "The smell should decay at a rate that's consistent with the mechanism."

Bradbury stared at him.

"The decay rate of the olfactory trace should correspond to the physics of the displacement. If the mechanism involves a specific relationship between the duration of absence and the — "

"Ted."

"What."

"You just agreed with me and you don't even know it."

Chiang stopped. He looked at the ground. He looked at the dandelion stem on the bench. He looked at Bradbury with an expression I hadn't seen from him before — not surprise, exactly, but the specific discomfort of recognizing that someone else's instinct has arrived at the same place as your logic, by a completely different road.

"The smell decays," he said. "And the rate of decay is information. Fine. But she has to realize it's information. She has to be the kind of person who would notice that the smell fades faster after certain displacements and slower after others, and who would start keeping records, and who would eventually — "

"Eventually realize she's been studying the physics of losing someone," Bradbury said. "Yes. And that realization should break something in her, because there's a difference between losing someone and studying the loss. One is a wound. The other is a wound you've given a Latin name."

A jogger went past. The bird was still going. Four notes, over and over, and I realized I'd been hearing it the entire afternoon without once wondering what kind of bird it was, which is either a failure of attention or a success of attention, depending on which of these two men you asked.

"I keep thinking about the kitchen," I said. "He appears in their kitchen. Not always — sometimes he's in the yard, sometimes in a room she doesn't recognize — but mostly it's the kitchen. And she's never ready. She's cooking, or she's reading, or she's staring at the wall at two in the morning, and then he's there, and she has to figure out which version of him she's looking at. Is this the one who knows about the miscarriage? Is this the one who hasn't met her mother yet? Is this the one who's already said goodbye?"

Neither of them answered. The light was doing its last thing, the thing September light does right before it gives up, turning everything golden in a way that feels like compensation.

"Is this the one who's already said goodbye," Bradbury said quietly, to no one.
