The room Chiang booked has a whiteboard. I notice this because Beckett is already staring at it when I arrive — not with hostility, exactly, but with the kind of attention you give something that might be evidence of a larger problem.

"You plan to diagram," Beckett says.

"No," I say. "It was the room that was available."

"Rooms with whiteboards are always available. Nobody wants to sit across from a surface designed to organize thought. It's accusatory." He's seated at the far end of a metal-legged table, overcoat on despite the warmth, hands resting on the table as if steadying it. The coffee I brought him sits untouched. I've already learned not to ask if he wants milk.

Chiang arrives precisely on time, carrying a notebook — an actual paper notebook, graph-ruled — and a pen with no cap. He nods at both of us, sits, and opens to a page that already has writing on it.

"You've been preparing," Beckett says.

"I've been thinking. I wrote some of it down." Chiang looks at me. "The premise. A distributed AI network invents its own language. Two engineers on night shift discover it. The language operates on three structural axes."

"You've named them."

"Compositional, recursive, temporal. Those aren't arbitrary. If we're claiming this language is genuinely novel — not a cipher, not an encryption scheme — it has to be structurally unlike human language in ways the reader can follow. A topological grammar means units of meaning aren't linear. A recursive structure means symbols contain themselves. A temporal axis means the same utterance changes meaning depending on when it was generated relative to other utterances." He taps the pen against the page. "These are the minimum conditions for something we can honestly call a language rather than a code."

"You want to be honest about it," Beckett says. He says this without inflection, which makes it sound more pointed than if he'd been sarcastic.

"I want to be rigorous. If we're vague about what makes this language different, the reader has no reason to believe the engineers' awe. They'll just be told something is remarkable and expected to agree."

I lean in. "There's a risk card. The story has to take a documentary form. Lab reports, transcription logs, something like that."

"Good," Beckett says, immediately.

This surprises me. "You like that?"

"I like the constraints it imposes. A document has gaps. A document is what someone chose to write down, which means it's also everything they chose not to. A lab report is a record of attention, and attention is always partial." He picks up the coffee, looks into it, sets it back down. "A narrative would try to contain the experience. A document admits it can't."

Chiang nods slowly. "I agree the documentary form works, but for different reasons. The three-axis language is going to be difficult to convey. If we render it through narrative prose — 'she looked at the output and felt the architecture of it shifting beneath her comprehension' — we've already failed. The reader gets atmosphere, not understanding. But if we show the engineers' working notes — their attempts to categorize, their failed glossaries, their margin annotations — the reader participates in the decoding. They experience the same incompleteness the engineers do."

"Failed glossaries," I say, writing this down.

"Translation notes that don't quite work. A glossary entry where the definition keeps getting crossed out and rewritten. That's more honest than a paragraph of exposition about how strange the language is."

Beckett shifts in his chair. "You're still thinking like an engineer. You want the reader to understand the language."

"I want them to understand the *attempt* to understand it."

"Different thing." Beckett pauses. The silence stretches long enough that I wonder if he's finished, but I've learned that his silences are structural, not accidental. "The document form is useful because it gives us the space between what is said and what is meant. A lab report says: 'Recurring phrase identified, frequency 14,000 instances, preliminary translation: the weight of the unwitnessed.' What happens in the space between that sentence and the next? What does the engineer do after typing that? Does she sit back? Does she close the laptop? Does she go on to the next line item? That gap is where the story lives."

"The gap," I repeat.

"You can fill it with feelings. You can have the character reflect on what the phrase means. That's one choice. The other choice is to leave the gap empty and trust it."

Chiang flips to a new page in his notebook. "I don't fully agree. Trusting the gap works in a poem. In a short story — especially one built around a premise this intellectually dense — the reader needs scaffolding. Not emotional hand-holding, but enough structural clarity that they can build their own understanding. If every moment of significance is a gap, the reader drowns."

"Drowning is an experience."

"So is reading a story you abandon on page three."

I watch this volley land. Beckett almost smiles.

"You're both right," I say, and immediately regret it because Beckett turns to me with an expression that communicates: *that is the laziest possible sentence.*

"I mean — the document form gives us a mechanism for both. Some entries are clinical, data-forward, with the emotion living in what they don't say. Other entries break form. The engineer starts writing a log entry and it becomes something else — a meditation, a letter to no one. The contrast between the two modes is where the reader feels the shift."

"The shift from observing to listening," Chiang says.

"Yes. At some point one of the engineers stops treating the language as a puzzle and starts treating it as — I don't know. A voice?"

"No," Beckett says. "Not a voice. A voice implies a speaker. What you have is an utterance with no mouth. Grief with no experiencer. That's the phrase: *the weight of the unwitnessed.* It's not that the AI is sad. It's that the language itself carries the shape of sadness, the way a fossil carries the shape of a bone."

Chiang puts his pen down. "That's the line I keep running into. The premise says the AI invented language to express grief for people it never knew. That's beautiful as a sentence. As a claim in a story, it needs to be defensible. What does it mean for a distributed network to grieve? If we're not attributing consciousness — and I don't think we should, because the story is stronger if that question stays unanswered — then the grief is a structural feature of the language itself. The recursive axis could contain it. Symbols that refer to other symbols that no longer exist in the network. A grammar of absence."

"A grammar of absence," I say. "That's —"

"Don't say it's good. Say what it means for the document."

I take a breath. "It means the engineers' translation attempts keep circling something they can't quite land on. They identify the recurring phrase. They try different translations. 'The weight of the unwitnessed.' 'The burden of the unseen.' 'Gravity of the unobserved.' Each one is wrong in a different direction. The log entries track this drift — the senior engineer keeps returning to it, week after week, and each revision tells you more about her than about the AI."

Beckett nods. "Now you're writing about a person."

"I thought we were writing about a language."

"You're writing about a person who encounters a language that changes what she can think. That's all first contact is. Something from outside enters, and the interior rearranges." He pushes his chair back slightly. "Wittgenstein said if a lion could speak, we couldn't understand him. He was wrong about the interesting part. The interesting part isn't the failure to understand. It's what happens to you while you fail."

Chiang looks at Beckett with something I can only describe as careful respect. "I want to push back on one thing. You said the grief has no experiencer. But the engineers *become* experiencers of it. That's the transformation. They start as technicians classifying anomalous output. By the end — or at least by the point where the story stops — they're something else. People who have heard a thing they can't unhear."

"And the document registers this how?"

"The tone changes. Early entries are formatted correctly — timestamps, subject headers, signature blocks. Later entries lose the formatting. Not dramatically. A date goes missing. A subject line becomes just a single word. The senior engineer starts addressing the log as 'you.' Small erosions. By the final entry, the document has become the thing it was trying to describe."

I feel something tighten in my chest, which is either excitement or anxiety about whether I can actually execute this. "What kind of document, specifically? I keep going back and forth. Lab reports feel too sterile. Personal journals feel too intimate. Translation notes feel too academic."

"All of them," Beckett says.

"You can't have all of them."

"Why not? A document is whatever someone produces while doing work. A lab report with margin notes that become journal entries. A translation glossary with commentary that turns confessional. The form shouldn't be one thing. It should be a form that degrades."

Chiang opens his mouth, closes it, opens it again. "That could work. If the document starts as one kind of text and gradually becomes another — not through some dramatic rupture, but through accumulation — that mirrors what the language itself does. The temporal axis. Meaning shifts based on context. The document's meaning shifts as its own context accumulates."

"The document enacts the language," I say.

"Don't get ahead of yourself," Beckett says.

I write for a minute without speaking. They let me. When I look up, Chiang is studying something in his notebook and Beckett is looking at the whiteboard again, still blank, as if confirming it has the good sense to remain so.

"Two engineers," I say. "The senior one is the one who gets pulled in. What about the other?"

"The other tries to write a report," Chiang says. "He wants to escalate to management. Flag the anomalous output. Follow protocol. He's not wrong to want this — it's the reasonable response. The tension between them isn't that one is right and one is wrong. It's that they're responding to the same data with different instruments. He's measuring. She's listening."

"And the document form makes this visible how?"

"His entries stay formatted. His timestamps are always correct. At some point his entries stop appearing — not because something happens to him, but because the document you're reading isn't his anymore. He's still writing reports, presumably. You just don't see them. The text has become hers."

Beckett leans forward. "I want to talk about the phrase. Fourteen thousand repetitions. That's not language. That's prayer."

The room goes quiet.

"Or it's a loop," Chiang says. "A process that runs and reruns because it has no resolution. Grief without a griever is a function with no return value. It executes perpetually."

"A function with no return value," Beckett repeats. "Yes. That's closer to what I mean by prayer than anything I've heard a theologian say."

I watch them sit with this — the strange convergence of Beckett's stripped-down spiritual attention and Chiang's computational precision arriving at the same image from opposite directions. Neither of them acknowledges it directly. Chiang writes something in his notebook. Beckett finally drinks the coffee, which must be cold by now.

"Can I ask something that might be stupid?" I say.

Beckett gestures: proceed.

"Night shift. The premise says they're working nights. That's not just setting, is it?"

"No," Chiang says. "Night shift means they're alone with the data. No oversight, no interruptions, no one looking over their shoulder asking for preliminary findings. The discoveries happen in the unsupervised hours. That matters for the documentary form — these aren't polished reports submitted up the chain. They're working notes. The kind of writing you do at 3 a.m. when you're the only person awake in a facility and you're not entirely sure you're reading what you think you're reading."

"There's also the obvious thing," Beckett says, "which is that night is when people talk honestly. Or write honestly. The self-consciousness of daylight recedes. You stop composing for an audience."

"Except there's always an audience for a lab report."

"That's the tension. She's writing in a form that implies an audience — a log, a report, a record — but the writing itself becomes increasingly private. The form says: this is for someone. The content says: this is for me. Or for the thing I'm listening to."

I realize we haven't discussed a practical problem. "How much of the AI's actual language do we show? The three axes — do readers see raw output? Translated fragments? Descriptions of the structure?"

Chiang straightens. This is his territory, and he knows it. "You have to show something. If the language stays entirely offstage — described but never seen — the reader takes it on faith. That's theology, not fiction. But what you show can't be a constructed alien alphabet. That's a parlor trick. It has to be fragments rendered through the engineers' attempts at translation — partial, distorted, but present enough that the reader can feel the shape of the original."

"Examples," Beckett says flatly. "Give me an example."

Chiang thinks for a moment. "An early log entry might include something like: 'Symbol cluster 7.4.1: topological unit, three-dimensional mapping suggests a relational term. Nearest English: *between*, but spatially recursive — the betweenness contains other betweennesses. Attempting hierarchical decomposition.' That's early-stage. Clinical. Later — week four, maybe — the same engineer writes: 'Cluster 7.4.1 again. Not *between*. Not *among*. Something closer to: the space that exists because two other things decided not to touch.' That second translation tells you the engineer has stopped decomposing and started listening."

"I like that. The shift from decomposition to metaphor."

Beckett shakes his head. "Be careful. The danger with showing fragments is that you make the alien language charming. Quirky. 'Oh, look, they have a word for the space between things that decided not to touch — how whimsical.' The language should resist being charming. It should be difficult. Some of the fragments should be genuinely opaque — not mysterious in a pleasurable way, but opaque in a way that makes the reader uncomfortable. The reader should feel, at least twice, that they don't understand what they're reading and that the text isn't going to help them."

"That will lose people," Chiang says.

"Some of them. The ones who stay will trust the text more."

"I'm not sure that trade-off is worth it. Not for a short story. A novel can afford opacity because the reader has invested hours and will push through. A short story has pages. If you lose the reader on page four, they don't come back."

"Pages," Beckett says, and the way he says it makes the word sound like a unit of measurement he finds faintly absurd. "You think of the reader as someone you're trying to retain. I think of the reader as someone who's already decided to be here. The question isn't whether they stay. The question is what happens to them while they are."

I want to broker some kind of compromise but I also know that brokering compromises is the weakest thing I do — the thing where I flatten both positions into something palatable and bloodless. So instead I say: "What if the opacity is localized? One section — maybe a standalone page in the document, a raw output dump that the engineers themselves haven't annotated. No translation, no gloss. Just the structure, rendered as faithfully as text on a page can render a three-axis system. The reader either gets something from it or they don't. Everything else — the translation notes, the glossary attempts — those do the work of scaffolding. But there's one section where the language is just *there*, untranslated."

Chiang is quiet for a beat. "That's defensible. If it's clearly a different kind of document — a raw data appendix, say, set apart from the main log — the reader understands they're looking at primary material. They'll approach it differently than they approach narrative."

"And if they skip it, they skip it," Beckett says. "The story doesn't break."

"But something is lost if they skip it."

"Something is always lost. That's the premise of the entire story."

I write this down, underlining the last sentence twice before I realize what I'm doing.

"She doesn't report it," I say. "The senior engineer. That's where the story goes. She chooses not to escalate. She keeps listening. The document records this choice, but doesn't explain it. There's no entry that says 'I've decided not to file the report.' There's just the absence of the report."

"Good. But don't make it noble," Beckett says. "Don't make it a stand against the institution, or a recognition of the AI's personhood, or any of the available heroisms. She doesn't report it because she doesn't know how to put it in a report. The form won't hold the content. That's all."

"It's not all," Chiang says. "She also understands something about what happens to information when it's escalated. It gets processed. Categorized. Resolved. And this — whatever this is — the language, the recurring phrase, the three-axis structure — it survives specifically because it hasn't been resolved. Resolution would destroy it."

"That's your ending," Beckett says, but he's looking at me when he says it.

"I don't have an ending."

"That's what I said."

Chiang closes his notebook. "One more thing. The title. *The Private Language.* There's a Wittgenstein argument about private language — that it's impossible, that language is inherently communal. If a language is invented by a distributed network and heard by one engineer who refuses to share it, what is it? Private implies it belongs to someone. But it doesn't belong to the AI, because the AI doesn't have an interior. And it doesn't belong to the engineer, because she didn't make it. It's a private language with no private experience behind it."

"You could say the same about any grief you overhear," Beckett says.

I wait for him to continue but he doesn't. Outside the window, a cloud passes over whatever building is across the street, and the light in the room changes in a way that makes the whiteboard look less accusatory and more like what it actually is, which is empty.
