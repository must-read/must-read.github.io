She'd chosen the restaurant, somewhere in Bloomsbury with cloth napkins and too much natural light. Abercrombie squinted at the menu like it had personally wronged him. Miller sat with her water glass centered on its coaster and the kind of posture that suggested she'd spent years in rooms where posture mattered.

"I don't know what burrata is," Abercrombie said. "And I'm not going to ask."

"It's cheese," Miller said. "Soft. From Puglia."

"Right." He set the menu down. "So. We're writing a swordfighter."

"We're writing someone who becomes formidable through suffering," Miller said. "The sword is incidental."

"The sword is never incidental. The sword is the thing that keeps you alive when everyone around you is trying very hard to make you dead. That's the genre. Sword and sorcery. Emphasis on the sword."

I'd been quiet, trying to feel the shape of the argument before it arrived. It arrived in about forty seconds, which was faster than I'd expected but slower than last time I sat across from Abercrombie. I said: "The brief mentions an outcast who becomes formidable. Circe. Someone exiled, someone who earns their power through long, painful work rather than inheriting it or stumbling into it."

"Circe spent centuries on that island," Miller said. "She wasn't training. She was *surviving*. And the magic she learned — pharmakeia, herb-craft, the slow patient work of grinding roots and learning what kills and what heals — that's not the same as picking up a blade and getting good at stabbing people. The acquisition is different. The cost is different."

"The cost is time," Abercrombie said. "Either way. Whether you're grinding roots or sparring until your knuckles bleed, the cost is years of your life that you'll never get back, spent on something that might not save you. The only difference is how much your shoulders hurt at the end of the day."

"That's reductive."

"That's honest."

Miller took a sip of water and set the glass down exactly where it had been. "The difference is interiority. A swordfighter trains the body. A witch — a sorceress, whatever term you want — trains perception. Attention. The ability to see what something actually is, not what it appears to be. Circe learned her craft by learning to look at the world with a precision that terrified the gods, because the gods don't look closely at anything. They can't afford to. Precision would undo them."

"Fine," Abercrombie said. "But we're not writing a god-story, are we?"

That stopped her for a moment. I could see the recalibration happening behind her eyes.

"We might be," I said, and immediately regretted saying it so tentatively. "The themes reference divinity as loneliness. Power that isolates. If our protagonist becomes formidable enough, what they become might look like godhood from the outside, even if it feels like exile from the inside."

"Godhood is just a bigger cage," Abercrombie said. "I've written characters with enormous power. Bayaz. The Maker. You know what they all have in common? They're miserable. Not because power corrupts — that's a children's-book moral — but because power requires you to keep choosing it. Every day. Every interaction. You can never stop performing your own strength, because the moment you stop, someone tests it."

"Circe would agree with that," Miller said, and she sounded almost surprised. "She'd frame it differently. She'd say divinity is a kind of deafness. The gods can't hear mortal suffering because if they could, they'd have to respond to it, and response would diminish them. Power requires a narrowing of sympathy. That's the real cost."

"A narrowing of sympathy," Abercrombie repeated. He seemed to be testing the weight of it. "All right. I can work with that. So our character — this swordfighter, this outcast — as they get stronger, they get lonelier. Not because people fear them, though people do fear them. Because the strength itself seals something off. Each skill learned is a door closed."

I wrote that down. Each skill learned is a door closed. I wasn't sure if it was true, but it had the density of something worth arguing about.

"And we're telling it backward," I said. "That's the structural constraint. Reverse chronology. The reader meets this person at the height of their power and works backward toward whatever they were before."

The silence that followed was different from the earlier ones. Heavier. Abercrombie leaned back in his chair and crossed his arms, which I was learning meant he was thinking rather than dismissing.

"Backward," he said. "So every victory the reader sees has already been purchased by a defeat they haven't witnessed yet."

"Exactly. And by the time they reach the defeat —"

"It's worse. Because they know what it costs. They've already seen the price paid. Now they're watching the invoice arrive."

Miller was nodding, slowly. "In Circe's terms: the reader meets the goddess first. Formidable, solitary, dangerous. And then each step backward shows you a woman who was less formidable and less solitary and — this is the cruelty of it — *happier*. Not because weakness is happiness, but because connection is happiness, and connection is what the strength consumed."

"That's sentimental," Abercrombie said.

"It's true."

"It can be both."

I could feel the meeting tilting. This was the fault line — not whether reverse chronology worked, but what it meant. For Abercrombie, working backward was a mechanism of ironic cruelty: you show the reader the cost, then force them to watch the character pay it, and the effect is a kind of savage comedy. For Miller, working backward was elegiac: you show the reader what was lost, and the losing is the story.

"I think," I said carefully, "that the cruelty and the elegy aren't actually opposed. The same backward movement does both things simultaneously. When we see the protagonist as a young person — someone with friends, someone who laughs easily, someone who hasn't yet learned to kill — the reader feels grief and feels manipulated. Because the reader knows where this is going. The reader has already been there."

"That's dramatic irony," Miller said. "The oldest engine in the Greek tradition. The audience knows what Oedipus doesn't. The audience watches him investigate his own crime."

"And what's our character's crime?" Abercrombie asked.

Nobody answered immediately. The waiter came and took Abercrombie's order (the lamb, no salad, a pint of bitter) and Miller's (the burrata, the sea bass, sparkling water). I ordered something I don't remember. I was thinking about the question.

"Survival," Miller said, eventually. "Their crime is surviving when others didn't. The strength that makes them formidable is built on a foundation of people who died around them, beside them, sometimes because of them, sometimes instead of them. And the backward structure forces the reader to meet each of those people *after* they're already dead."

"Ghosts walking," Abercrombie said. "Every character in the early chapters — early for the reader, late for the protagonist — is already a corpse. We just haven't shown the dying yet."

"And when we do show the dying —"

"We don't," he said. "Not all of it. Some of them we just stop seeing. One chapter they're there, next chapter — which is an earlier chapter, chronologically — they haven't arrived yet. The reader never gets the death scene. They get the absence and the presence, and their brain fills in the dying."

"That's devastating," I said.

"That's efficient," Abercrombie said. "I don't want twenty death scenes. I want three. Maybe two. The rest just vanish between the seams."

Miller was looking at him with something that wasn't quite respect but bordered on it. "The rotating POV element — from The Blade Itself — how does that work in reverse chronology? Are we in multiple heads going backward? Or one head?"

"One head going backward is simpler," I said. "But the ensemble collision idea suggests —"

"Multiple," Abercrombie said. "Three. Maybe four. Each stepping backward through their own timeline, and the timelines intersect at different points. So in the first chapter — which is the last moment, chronologically — they're all in the same room. Maybe facing each other. Maybe one of them is dying. And then the chapters peel them apart, moving backward, and the reader watches these people who ended up in the same room slowly becoming strangers to each other."

"Convergence told as divergence," Miller said. "I want to resist that."

"Resist it how?"

"I want one primary consciousness. Someone whose interiority carries the weight. The ensemble can exist — I'm not opposed to other voices — but there needs to be a center. A consciousness the reader inhabits completely. Otherwise the backward structure becomes a puzzle rather than an experience. The reader starts tracking timelines instead of feeling them."

"Feeling is your department," Abercrombie said, and it came out harsher than I think he intended. He rubbed his face. "Sorry. What I mean is — the puzzle *is* a feeling. When a reader is tracking three timelines and suddenly realizes that the woman in chapter six is the girl from chapter two, that recognition is an emotion. It's not just cleverness. It hits the gut."

"It hits the gut if you've earned it," Miller said. "If the reader cares about the woman and the girl. If they're not just pieces on a board."

"When have I ever written pieces on a board?"

"You haven't. But the structure invites it. Reverse chronology with multiple POVs is architecturally ambitious to the point where the architecture can become the story, and the people inside it become furniture."

I could feel them hardening into their positions. Abercrombie wanted the full ensemble, the rotating close-third, the mechanical satisfaction of timelines clicking into place as they ran backward. Miller wanted a single deep consciousness, a Circe-like interiority, the sorcery of inhabiting one person so completely that their losses become yours. Both were right, and saying so would have been useless.

"What if the primary POV dominates but isn't alone?" I said. "Three-quarters of the story is one consciousness going backward. But there are intrusions — chapters, or sections within chapters, from other perspectives. Brief. Sharp. They show the same events from outside, and the dissonance between internal and external perception is part of the horror."

"Horror," Miller said. She tasted the word.

"The horror of being seen incorrectly," I said. "The protagonist knows who they are. They know what each act of violence cost them. But the external perspectives see only the violence. The legend, not the grief. The sword, not the shaking hand."

"Now you're cooking," Abercrombie said, and he actually smiled. It was a brief, crooked thing, like a door opening onto a room with no furniture. "The external POVs are the myth. The internal POV is the woman who has to live inside the myth. And the backward movement strips the myth away. Each step back, she's less mythic, less fearsome, more — what? More human?"

"More capable of being hurt," Miller said. "Which is the same thing."

The food arrived. Abercrombie ate with the focused efficiency of someone who treats meals as fuel and resents the interruption but won't waste food. Miller ate slowly, precisely, as if the meal were a text she was reading. I picked at whatever I'd ordered and thought about what "more capable of being hurt" meant in reverse chronology. It meant the story moved toward vulnerability. It meant the ending — the chronological beginning, the structural ending — was the most exposed moment. The protagonist at their youngest, their weakest, their most open to damage. And the reader, arriving there, would understand that everything they'd seen — the mastery, the fearsome reputation, the isolation — grew from this exposed root.

"We haven't discussed the sorcery," I said. "Sword and sorcery. We've been talking about the sword and the suffering. What about the magic?"

"Magic is the thing that makes the suffering meaningful," Miller said immediately. "In Circe, the witchcraft isn't power in the crude sense. It's transformation. The ability to change the nature of things. And transformation requires intimacy with what you're changing — you have to know the lion before you can turn a man into one. The sorcery should be rooted in knowledge, not force."

"In this genre, sorcery is usually force," Abercrombie said. "Fire from the hands, dark rituals, that sort of rubbish. I've always treated magic as another form of violence — or another form of politics, which is the same thing. The question isn't what magic can do. The question is who benefits from it and who pays."

"Both of those," I said. "Both. The magic is transformative *and* transactional. Every spell costs something specific. Not mana, not energy — something personal. A memory. A relationship. A part of yourself you can't get back."

"If the story runs backward," Miller said, leaning forward now, her composure shifting into something fiercer, "and the magic costs pieces of the self, then the backward movement is a restoration. The reader watches the protagonist lose their power chapter by chapter, and with each loss, something returns. A warmth. A capacity for joy. A name someone used to call them."

"But that's optimistic," Abercrombie said. "That's a story about loss that secretly wants to be a story about recovery. Going backward and finding that the person underneath the power was whole and good and worth saving. I don't buy it. The person underneath the power was *also* damaged. They were an outcast before they were a sorcerer. The vulnerability at the beginning isn't innocence. It's a different kind of wound."

"I didn't say innocence —"

"You implied it. 'A capacity for joy.' 'A name someone used to call them.' That's nostalgia. That's the lie that the past was better."

Miller set her fork down. "The past wasn't better. The past was where the first wound happened. But the first wound was *survivable*. That's the difference. The early wounds — rejection, exile, whatever drove this person out — those wounds could be borne by a human being. The later wounds, the ones that come with power, those can only be borne by something that has stopped being entirely human. The backward movement isn't nostalgia. It's a record of the last moments when ordinary suffering was still possible."

Abercrombie opened his mouth, then closed it. He picked up his pint and took a long drink. When he set it down, he said: "That's the best thing you've said all evening, and I still think it's half wrong."

"Which half?"

"The half that assumes the protagonist wants to go back. The comfortable assumption that everyone secretly wishes they were weaker and more connected and less alone. Some people choose the isolation. Some people discover they're better at being feared than being loved, and they make peace with that, and the peace isn't a lie. It's just a different kind of life."

"Circe didn't make peace with it," Miller said.

"Circe's your character. I'm talking about mine."

"We're supposed to be talking about the same character."

The waiter cleared plates. Abercrombie ordered coffee, black, which arrived in a cup too delicate for his hands. Miller asked for chamomile. I asked for the bill, thinking we were winding down, but neither of them reached for a coat.

"The ending," I said. "Which is the beginning. The chronological beginning. Where we leave the reader."

"A child," Miller said. "Or nearly. Someone young enough that the reader understands, viscerally, what all of this was built from. Young enough that the mythology of later chapters seems obscene. This is a person. Not a legend. A person with skinned knees and a name they chose for themselves and —"

"Don't make them sympathetic," Abercrombie said. "Or rather — don't make them only sympathetic. The child who becomes the monster has to have the monster already in them. Not because people are born bad, that's boring, but because the qualities that make someone capable of extraordinary violence are the same qualities that make them capable of extraordinary survival. Stubbornness. An inability to accept the terms they've been given. A talent for seeing the weak point in whatever is trying to kill them."

"Adaptive ruthlessness," I said.

"Adaptive ruthlessness, yes, but worn on a frame that's still soft. Still breakable. The reader looks at this child and thinks: I know what you become. And the knowing is the grief."

Miller was quiet for a long time. The restaurant had emptied around us. Someone was wiping down the bar. The light outside had gone grey and flat, the way London light does in February when the sun was never really present to begin with.

"There's a tension we haven't resolved," she said. "Joe wants the protagonist to choose their isolation. I want the isolation to be a cost, not a choice. Those are incompatible readings of the same character."

"Leave it," Abercrombie said.

"I can't write something I haven't resolved."

"You can. You have to. The story is stronger if even we don't know whether the isolation was chosen or inflicted. The protagonist doesn't know. Why should we?"

I looked at Miller. She was holding her chamomile with both hands, the steam rising between her fingers, and I could see her deciding whether to fight this or let it stand. She let it stand. But the way she let it stand — the slight tightening around her mouth, the adjustment of her grip on the cup — told me she hadn't agreed. She'd tabled it.
