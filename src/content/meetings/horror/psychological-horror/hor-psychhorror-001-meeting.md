The rental house had been Paul Tremblay's idea. He said he wanted somewhere that felt like a setting, and he'd found this place outside of North Adams through some listing service I'd never heard of. It was a saltbox colonial, white with green shutters, perched on a lot that the surrounding woods were slowly reclaiming. The driveway was cracked in a pattern that reminded me of dried riverbeds. The kitchen smelled like someone else's childhood. Not mine. Not anyone's I knew. Just the abstract concept of a childhood kitchen — dish soap and something baking and underneath that, faintly, bleach.

Shirley Jackson was already there when I arrived. She was standing in the front room, hands in her coat pockets, looking at the wallpaper. It was floral. Faded. The kind of pattern that must have been chosen with great confidence in 1974 and had been slowly losing the argument with itself ever since. Vines twisted into vines. Roses consumed other roses.

"They never removed it," she said, running one finger along a seam where two strips met. The seam was beginning to separate, just slightly, a dark line of adhesive and old plaster showing through. "They just painted over it in places. Look. You can see where they gave up."

She was right. Near the ceiling, the wallpaper was original, its pattern fully articulated. At eye level, someone had rolled flat white latex paint over sections of it, as if trying to correct the room one panel at a time before abandoning the project entirely. The painted-over sections were worse than the exposed ones. You could still see the shapes underneath, pressing through. Ghosts of roses.

"This is exactly right," she said. She was not smiling.

Paul showed up twenty minutes late with two bags of groceries and an apology about the GPS sending him to a cemetery first. He was taller than I expected, and he moved through the house with the particular attentiveness of someone cataloguing everything for later use. He opened cabinets. He tested light switches. He stood in the center of the living room and looked up at the ceiling with an expression I couldn't read.

"The foundation's off," he said. "You can feel it in the floors. Everything tilts about two degrees east."

"I thought that was me," I said.

"It's not you." He set the groceries on the counter — bread, cheese, olives, a bottle of red wine that was nicer than the house deserved. "So. Confinement."

"Confinement," Jackson repeated. She had settled into an armchair in the corner of the front room, the one with the wallpaper, and she looked like she had been sitting in it for years. "You want to start there."

"I want to start with what we're both circling. A woman in a room. A woman who may or may not be losing her mind. The room is the thing, right? The room is always the thing."

"The room is never the thing," Jackson said. "The room is what the people around her decided about her. The room is a verdict someone dressed up as concern."

I poured wine into three mismatched glasses I found in the cabinet and brought them out. Jackson accepted hers without looking at it. Tremblay held his up to the light from the window, watching the red catch the last of the afternoon sun, which came through the trees in shifting fragments.

"Here's my problem with confinement narratives," Tremblay said. "They're too sympathetic. The reader knows from page one that the confined person is the sane one. The husband is the villain, the institution is the villain, society is the villain. The reader gets to feel righteous. That's comfortable. Comfortable isn't horror."

"You think Gilman's narrator is sane?" I asked.

"I think the question of her sanity is the least interesting thing about that story. What's interesting is the performance. She's writing a journal she knows might be read. Every sentence is calibrated. Even her madness is —" He paused, searching. "Authored."

Jackson set her glass down on the windowsill. "That's closer to something. The performed self versus the actual self. She doesn't know which one is seeing the woman behind the wallpaper. Neither does the reader. You think you're watching someone unravel, but you might be watching someone construct."

"Construct what?" I said.

"The only narrative in which she gets to act."

We sat with that for a moment. The house made a sound — not a creak, exactly, but a kind of exhalation, the way old houses settle when the temperature drops. The light was going. Tremblay got up and tried the overhead switch, which produced a thin, buzzing glow from a fixture that was probably original to the house.

"Okay," I said. "So what if we have a narrator who's documenting something — observing, recording — and the documentation itself becomes the illness? Not that she's documenting because she's ill. That she's ill because she's documenting."

Tremblay pointed at me with his wine glass. "Now you're in my neighborhood. That's the *Head Full of Ghosts* question. The Barretts put their daughter's potential possession on reality television, and the show didn't just document her — it produced her. The camera created the thing it was supposed to be capturing."

"I wrote about that long before anyone put cameras in living rooms," Jackson said, and there was an edge to it. "Eleanor Vance didn't need an audience. Hill House was already watching."

"But Hill House was real," Tremblay said. "Or at least, you let it be real enough that the reader has to take it seriously. I don't do that. I don't let anyone off the hook. Everything in my work could be explained. Every horror has a mundane reading. That's what makes it unbearable."

"Unbearable for whom?" Jackson asked.

"For the reader who wants to know."

"Most readers don't want to know. They want to feel that they know. There's a difference."

Tremblay leaned forward. "And that's where I think we should plant this story. In that gap. Between knowing and feeling that you know. A narrator who's so precise, so careful in her observations, that you trust her completely — until you realize her precision is the symptom."

I was writing notes on the back of a grocery receipt. My handwriting was getting smaller, which it does when I'm trying to keep up with something that's moving faster than I am. "What is she observing?" I asked. "What's the equivalent of the wallpaper?"

"Something in the house," Jackson said immediately. "Something that was there before she was. Something the house did, or had done to it, that she starts to notice and cannot stop noticing."

"That's too literal," Tremblay said.

"I wasn't finished. She starts to notice it, and at first the noticing is reasonable. Anyone would notice it. But then the noticing becomes detailed. Then the detail becomes obsessive. Then the obsession begins to look like expertise. And the people around her — if there are people around her — can't tell whether she's discovered something real or whether she's built something real out of nothing."

"And neither can the reader," I said.

"The reader least of all."

Tremblay got up and walked to the wallpaper. He put his hand flat against it, over one of the painted-over sections. "This is good," he said quietly. "This is actually very good. Because the painted-over sections are the key, right? Someone tried to fix this. Someone tried to cover it up. And the covering up made it worse, because now you've got the original pattern AND the evidence that someone found the original pattern disturbing enough to try to erase it. That's two layers of information. The thing itself, and the response to the thing."

"Three layers," Jackson said. "The thing, the response, and the failure of the response."

"Four," I said, without meaning to. They both looked at me. "The thing, the response, the failure of the response, and our narrator counting the layers."

Jackson almost smiled. Almost. "The narrator is the fourth layer. Yes. She's not discovering something about the house. She's adding to it."

"And she doesn't know that," Tremblay said.

"Does she?" Jackson's voice was careful. "That's the real question. Does she know she's adding to it and perform ignorance, or is she genuinely lost in her own pattern-recognition? Because those are different stories. The first is about agency. The second is about illness."

"The third option," I said, "is that she knows sometimes and doesn't know other times, and the story doesn't tell you which sections are which."

Tremblay sat back down. He was quiet for long enough that I thought he was going to disagree, but when he spoke, he sounded almost reluctant — like he was admitting something. "That's harder to write. Ambiguity that alternates is harder than ambiguity that's consistent. If she's always ambiguous, the reader settles into it. If she shifts — if there are paragraphs where she's clearly performing and paragraphs where she's clearly not and paragraphs where you genuinely cannot tell — the reader never settles. The ground keeps moving."

"The ground should keep moving," Jackson said. "That's what houses do. They settle. They shift. Everything tilts two degrees east."

Tremblay laughed, short and surprised. "You're using my own observation against me."

"I'm using the house against you. Which is what the house does."

I refilled the glasses. The wine was a Côtes du Rhône, and it was better than it had any right to be in this kitchen with its sticky linoleum and its single functioning burner. Outside, the woods had gone fully dark. You couldn't see where the lawn ended and the trees began.

"I want to talk about the family," Tremblay said. "Because I think the horror of this — whatever we're making — has to be domestic. Not gothic domestic. Not woman-in-the-attic domestic. Contemporary domestic. The kind of family where everyone is trying very hard and the trying is part of the problem."

"Where everyone is performing wellness," Jackson said.

"Exactly. Where the family has a vocabulary for their dysfunction. They've been to therapy. They have phrases. 'I feel seen.' 'That's my boundary.' 'I'm holding space for your experience.' And underneath all that language, underneath all that performance of self-awareness, nobody has the faintest idea what's actually happening in their own house."

"That's terrifying," I said, and I meant it.

"It's terrifying because it's real," Jackson said. "The most frightening thing I ever wrote — and I'm not talking about Hill House or the lottery, I'm talking about the domestic novels, the ones people call comedies — the most frightening thing is a family that agrees. A family that has decided what its story is and enforces the story without anyone ever acknowledging the enforcement. That's what neighbors do. That's what towns do. That's what families do in their living rooms at seven o'clock every single evening."

"And when someone in the family starts noticing," Tremblay said, "starts documenting, starts keeping track of the small wrongnesses —"

"The family's immune system activates," Jackson finished. "Not violently. Quietly. With concern. With gentleness. With the suggestion that perhaps she's been working too hard, that she should rest, that they've found someone very good for her to talk to."

"And the reader doesn't know if the family is right."

"The reader should be afraid that the family is right."

That landed in the room and stayed. I realized I had stopped writing notes. The grocery receipt was full on both sides, and my last entry was just the word *layers* circled three times.

"I have a question," I said. "And I'm not sure either of you will like it. Who is the audience for her documentation? Gilman's narrator writes a journal. Our narrator — who is she writing for? Who is she performing for?"

Tremblay stared at the ceiling. "Herself."

"That's not an audience."

"Of course it is. It's the only audience that matters. She's constructing a version of events that she can live inside. The documentation isn't a record. It's architecture. She's building a room she can survive in."

"Or a room she can't leave," Jackson said.

"Same thing."

"It is not the same thing."

They looked at each other across the dim kitchen, and I understood that this was the real disagreement — not a performative one, not something that would resolve into synthesis, but a genuine fracture in how they understood confinement. Tremblay saw the room as something the narrator built. Jackson saw the room as something the narrator was built into. One was about the horror of agency. The other was about the horror of its absence.

I didn't say that out loud. I didn't need to. They both knew where the fault line was, and neither was going to cross it.

"We should talk about the ending," I said.

"No," Jackson said, standing up. She carried her glass to the sink and rinsed it, and I watched her look out the kitchen window at nothing — at the dark and the trees and the absence of any light that wasn't ours. "The ending is the one thing we should not discuss. If you know where you're going, you'll write toward it, and the story will feel solved. The ending should come from the writing, not from us."

"I agree with her," Tremblay said. "Which I want noted, because it may not happen again tonight."

Jackson was still looking out the window. "There's something moving in the yard," she said, with absolutely no change in her tone.

"There isn't," Tremblay said.

"There is. Something low. Maybe an animal."

I stood up and went to the window. The glass was old and slightly warped, and the darkness outside was the kind of darkness you only get in places where no streetlights compete with the trees. I couldn't see anything moving. I could barely see the yard.

"I don't see anything," I said.

"That doesn't mean it isn't there," Jackson said. Then she picked up her coat from the back of the chair and put it on, buttoning it up to the collar. "I'm going for a walk."

"It's dark," I said.

"Yes," she said. "It is."

She went out the side door. Tremblay and I listened to her footsteps on the gravel, then on the grass, then nothing. The house settled around us. The wallpaper, in the bad light, looked like it was breathing.

"She does this," Tremblay said quietly. He was peeling the label off the wine bottle, slowly and with great precision. "She finds the exit that leaves everyone else unsettled. It's a talent."

"Should we go after her?"

"Absolutely not." He set the peeled label on the table, smoothed it flat. The vines on the wine label looked, in that moment, remarkably like the vines on the wallpaper. "We should sit here and think about what she said about the ending. Because she's right, and I hate that she's right. The story has to not know where it's going."

"Can a story not know where it's going and still arrive somewhere?"

He looked at me. "That's the whole question, isn't it? For the narrator. For the story. For all of us sitting in this house with its tilting floors and its painted-over walls." He paused. "For whoever is moving around in the yard."

"You said there was nothing in the yard."

"I said there isn't. Present tense." He picked up the wine label and held it to the light. "I didn't say anything about five minutes from now."
