The café was one of those places that believes in exposed ductwork and twelve-dollar oat milk, and Sedaris had already made three observations about it before we sat down — the barista's visible disdain for anyone who ordered a small, the inexplicable chalkboard art featuring a lobster in a beret, and the woman at the next table who appeared to be conducting a job interview with her cat. Smith arrived late, carrying a tote bag from an institution neither of us could identify, and said she'd gotten lost because she'd been thinking about something and walked past the street twice.

"What were you thinking about?" Sedaris asked.

"Whether competence is a kind of hiding," she said, and sat down as though this were a perfectly normal thing to announce.

I ordered a coffee. Sedaris ordered tea, which he examined with suspicion when it arrived. Smith ordered nothing and then drank half of my coffee when I wasn't looking.

I'd asked them both here because I wanted to write a humor essay about learning something new as an adult — about the particular mortification of being a beginner when you've spent decades accumulating the quiet authority of someone who knows where the forks go. I told them this, and there was a silence that I misread as encouragement.

"What's the thing?" Sedaris said. "What are you learning?"

"I was thinking maybe pottery," I said. "Or swimming."

"No," he said, with a speed that suggested I'd proposed arson.

"No to which one?"

"Both. Pottery is too cute. Everyone writes about pottery. You sit at a wheel, you make a metaphor, the pot collapses, you learn something about yourself. I've read that essay four hundred times. And swimming — swimming is all body, no embarrassment. You can be bad at swimming invisibly. The water covers you."

Smith was nodding, which I found more unnerving than Sedaris's bluntness because her nod had the quality of someone who'd already thought through the next three implications. "He's right about the visibility," she said. "The thing has to be witnessed. The humiliation has to happen in front of people — not in some back room where you can fail privately and then present the edited version of yourself."

"Music," Sedaris said. He was looking at the ceiling. "An instrument. Something that makes noise you can't hide."

"Adult recorder class," Smith said, and laughed — a real laugh, the kind that comes from the diaphragm and surprises the person making it. "Sorry, I don't mean — well, actually I do mean. Adult recorder class. Think about it. You're forty, or forty-five, and you're in a room full of other adults, and you're all holding these absurd plastic instruments that you associate with being seven, and you're making sounds that are indistinguishable from a bird being stepped on."

"The recorder is funny," Sedaris said, and the way he said it — flat, appraising, the way you'd say "that's a good knife" — made me understand that he was already three paragraphs ahead of me.

I suggested it could be something more dignified. Piano, maybe. Or a language, which would give us the *Me Talk Pretty One Day* framework directly.

"But you don't want the framework directly," Smith said. "Or rather — I don't. The language essay is David's, and he's already written the best version of it. You can't out-Sedaris Sedaris on the subject of struggling through French class. What you can do is find the instrument that's so undignified that the narrator can't even take their own suffering seriously."

Sedaris made a face. "I don't know. I think there's something to the language idea. Not French. Something weirder. Mandarin, or — no, actually, she's right. The recorder." He paused. "I hate agreeing with her this early."

"I don't love it either," Smith said.

They sat with that for a moment.

"Here's what I keep circling back to," I said, trying to find solid ground. "Sedaris, your stuff — the comic engine is always that you're the fool of your own story. You set yourself up as incompetent and the humor comes from your willingness to report your incompetence accurately. But Smith, in your essays, the comedy is different. It's the comedy of someone catching themselves in the act of being pretentious, or noticing that the thing they've been arguing passionately about has a hole in it the size of a bus."

"Self-deprecation versus self-awareness," Smith said. "They're not the same move."

"They're not," Sedaris agreed. "Self-deprecation has a punchline. Self-awareness has a — what? A pause."

"A comma," Smith said. "Self-awareness is a comma. You notice the thing and then you keep going, and the noticing changes the sentence but it doesn't end it."

I wrote that down. "So in this essay — the narrator is bad at the recorder. That's the Sedaris engine. But the Smith engine is the narrator stepping back and asking why being bad at the recorder feels like a crisis of identity. Why incompetence at a trivial thing triggers something that looks a lot like existential dread."

"Don't say existential dread," Sedaris said. "Ever. In anything."

"Fair."

"But the question is right," Smith said. She'd taken the other half of my coffee. "There's a real question there. Why does an adult struggling with 'Three Blind Mice' on a plastic recorder feel — not embarrassed, because embarrassment is too simple — but almost morally compromised? Like they've revealed something about themselves that was supposed to stay hidden?"

Sedaris leaned forward. "Because adulthood is a performance of competence. That's the whole thing. You spend your twenties pretending you know what you're doing, and then somewhere around thirty-five you stop pretending and start actually believing you know what you're doing, which is worse. And then you pick up a recorder and you can't make it through a C major scale and you're back to being a person who doesn't know anything."

"Yes," Smith said, "but it's not just that. It's that the recorder was the first instrument you were handed as a child. It's literally the starter instrument. So when you pick it up again at forty-five, you're not just incompetent — you're incompetent at the thing you were supposed to have surpassed. It's archaeological. You're digging down through the layers of yourself and finding the original foundation, and the foundation is bad."

"The foundation is always bad," Sedaris said, and there was something in his voice that wasn't comic — or was comic in the way that things are comic when they're also true in a way that makes your chest hurt.

Nobody spoke for a while. Smith was turning her empty cup in her hands. I was trying to figure out whether we'd just arrived at the center of the essay or whether we were still circling.

"I took a drawing class once," Sedaris said. "In my forties. It was supposed to be relaxing. That's what the brochure said. 'Relaxing.' I had to draw a pear. I drew something that looked like a kidney with ambitions. The woman next to me drew a pear that actually looked like a pear and I hated her with a purity I haven't felt since seventh grade. I went home and threw away the charcoal pencils and told Hugh the class had been cancelled. It hadn't been cancelled. I went back the next week because I'd paid for six sessions and I'm not someone who wastes money on things I don't use. I'm someone who wastes money on things I use badly."

"Did you get better?" I asked.

"I drew something that the teacher said was 'coming along,' which is the art-class equivalent of 'bless your heart.' But that's not the point. The point is that for those six weeks, every Tuesday from seven to nine, I was a person with no authority. No publishing history, no audience, nothing. Just a man who couldn't draw a pear. And it was — I don't want to say liberating, because that's a word people use when they're about to lie about how much they enjoyed something. It was clarifying. It clarified that I had been walking around thinking I was a certain kind of person, and that kind of person was a fiction I'd been telling myself for twenty years."

Smith was watching him with an expression I couldn't quite read. "That's the essay," she said. "What you just said. The fiction of the competent self."

"No, that's my story. You can't have it."

"I don't want your story. I want the feeling underneath it. The narrator picks up a recorder and suddenly the whole edifice of 'I am an adult who knows things' starts to wobble. Not collapse — wobble. Because collapse would be dramatic, and drama is the enemy here. The comedy lives in the wobble."

"The wobble," Sedaris repeated, as though tasting the word. "Fine. The wobble is good."

I asked about the teacher. There has to be a teacher.

"The teacher is kind," Sedaris said immediately. "The teacher has to be kind. That's worse. A mean teacher you can write off. A kind teacher — someone who genuinely believes you can learn this, who encourages you with a sincerity that borders on the clinical — that's devastating. Because you can't be angry at them. You can only be angry at yourself, and self-directed anger in the context of recorder class is so disproportionate to the situation that it becomes funny again."

Smith was shaking her head. "I don't want the teacher to be kind. Or rather, I don't want kindness to be the whole note. I want the teacher to be someone who takes the recorder seriously. Not kind — earnest. Someone who has devoted their life to an instrument that everyone else considers a joke, and who has made a kind of peace with that, and whose peace is more threatening to the narrator than cruelty would be."

"Because the teacher has solved the problem," I said.

"Exactly. The teacher has found a way to be a beginner — to live permanently in the state of learning — without it destroying them. And the narrator looks at this person and thinks: how? How have you made peace with being someone who plays the recorder? And the deeper question underneath that is: how have you made peace with not being exceptional?"

Sedaris was quiet for a long time. "That's the essay," he said. "Not being exceptional. The recorder is just the delivery device."

"I thought you hated agreeing with me," Smith said.

"I do. This is me hating it."

I asked about structure. How much of the essay should be scene — the narrator in class, fumbling through exercises, watching the other students — versus the essayistic thinking-aloud?

"Mostly scene," Sedaris said. "You earn the thinking by being in the room. The observations have to come out of specific moments. The narrator notices a particular student — someone who's worse than they are, which should be comforting but isn't. Or the teacher demonstrates something and for a second you hear what the recorder can actually sound like and it's — not beautiful, that's too much. But it's coherent. It's a sound that has intention behind it."

"I'd want more space for the thinking," Smith said. "Not a lot. But there should be a passage — maybe two pages, maybe less — where the narrator steps outside the scene and follows an idea. Not about the recorder specifically, but about what it means that our culture treats beginnership as a phase rather than a state. We say 'beginner' as though it's a stop on the way to somewhere else. But what if it's a place? What if some people live there?"

"That's an essay within the essay," Sedaris said. "It'll kill the pacing."

"It'll deepen the pacing."

"Same thing, if you're not careful."

They stared at each other. Neither blinked.

"Maybe it's one paragraph," I offered. "Not a whole excursus. One paragraph where the narrator has the thought, and then the thought gets interrupted by the teacher asking everyone to try the B-flat again, and the narrator has to stop being an essayist and go back to being a person who can't play the B-flat."

Smith considered this. "One paragraph. But a long one. A paragraph that does real work."

"Fine," Sedaris said. "But if it goes over half a page I'm pulling the fire alarm."

We talked about the other students. Sedaris wanted specifics — the man who holds the recorder like a weapon, the woman who practices with her eyes closed as though channeling something, the teenager who was dragged there by a parent and is better than everyone and visibly bored. "The teenager is key," he said. "There has to be someone in the room for whom this is easy, and they have to be young enough that their ease feels like an insult."

Smith wanted the narrator to notice something about the group dynamic — the way adults in a learning environment revert to adolescent social patterns. Alliances form. There's a star student and a class clown and someone who sits in the back and doesn't talk.

"And the narrator?" I asked. "Where does the narrator sit?"

"Middle," Sedaris said. "Always the middle. Close enough to be seen, far enough to pretend they're not trying."

"The narrator is the person who is trying very hard to look like they're not trying," Smith said. "That's the comic engine and the emotional engine. They're the same engine."

I brought up voice. The essay needs to sound like one person, not two, and these two have such different relationships with comedy on the page. Sedaris writes jokes that land like punches — setup, misdirection, payoff, all within a single sentence. Smith writes passages where the comedy sneaks up on you three sentences later, after you've already moved on, and you have to go back and realize that the funny thing wasn't the observation but the way the observation undermined itself.

"You're asking who wins," Sedaris said.

"I'm asking how they coexist."

"They don't," he said. "Not comfortably. And that's fine. A humor essay that's comfortable is a humor essay that isn't doing anything. The voice should feel like someone who wants to make you laugh and also wants to ask you a question they don't know the answer to, and those two impulses should be pulling in different directions for the entire piece."

Smith tilted her head. "I think the voice should sound like someone who is smarter than they're letting on but less in control than they'd like to be. The jokes are a way of managing the anxiety. The questions — the real ones, the ones about competence and adulthood and whether we're all just pretending — those are what happen when the management fails."

"So the voice cracks," I said.

"Not visibly," Sedaris said. "The reader should feel the crack, but the narrator shouldn't announce it. The moment you write 'and that's when I realized' you've lost. The realization has to live in the white space between the joke and the next sentence."

"Between the punchline and the pivot," Smith added. "That gap is where the essay actually lives."

I thought about that — the idea that the most important part of a humor essay might be the silence between the laughs. Neither of them seemed to think this was a revolutionary insight. For them, apparently, it was just Tuesday.

I asked about the ending. Should it be funny? Should the comedy crack open at the end and reveal something underneath?

Sedaris put his cup down. "If you write an essay that's funny for ten pages and then turns sincere in the last paragraph, I will find you and I will take your laptop."

Smith laughed. "He's right, actually. The sincerity should be distributed throughout. Not concentrated at the end like a lesson. If the essay works, the reader should feel the emotional weight accumulating inside the jokes, not after them."

"So the ending should be funny," I said.

"The ending should be a joke that's also the truest thing in the piece," Sedaris said. "Those are the only endings I believe."

"I want the ending to be incomplete," Smith said. "I want it to stop before the narrator has finished learning. Not because they quit — because the essay ends. It just stops. The way a lesson stops. The teacher says 'same time next week' and everyone packs up and the narrator walks out into the street and they're carrying this ridiculous plastic instrument and the world is going on around them and nothing has changed and also something has."

"That's not an ending," Sedaris said. "That's a trailing off."

"Trailing off is a kind of ending."

"It's a kind of failure."

"Maybe." Smith smiled. "Maybe that's the point."

We sat with that. The café had gotten louder around us. The woman with the cat had left. The barista was making a drink with the grim concentration of a surgeon.

Sedaris said, "The essay should be called 'First Lesson.' Not 'My First Lesson' or 'The First Lesson.' Just 'First Lesson.' Like a chapter heading. Like there's more coming."

"Is there more coming?" Smith asked.

"That's not for us to say."

I wanted to ask more — about voice, about how much of the narrator should be performed versus transparent, about whether the humor should be dry or broad or some unstable compound of both. But Sedaris was putting on his jacket, and Smith was already looking at something on her phone with the distracted intensity of someone who'd had another idea and was chasing it before it dissolved.

"One last thing," Smith said, not looking up. "The narrator should be genuinely bad at the recorder. Not charmingly bad. Not endearingly bad. Bad in a way that makes you wince. The comedy only works if the failure is real."

"Agreed," Sedaris said, from halfway to the door. "Nobody wants to read about a person who's bad at something in a way that's actually fine."

I sat there after they left, looking at my notes, which were mostly illegible and included the phrase "the foundation is bad" circled three times. The barista asked if I wanted anything else. I said no. He looked relieved.

I still didn't know exactly what I was going to write. But I knew it involved a plastic recorder, an earnest teacher, the specific shame of being forty-five and unable to play "Hot Cross Buns," and a question about whether incompetence is a place you visit or a place you live. I also knew that the ending was unresolved, which both of them had agreed on without noticing they'd agreed, which felt like the most honest thing that had happened all afternoon.
