The bar was called something optimistic — The Golden Mile, or The Golden Hour, I can't remember which — and it was neither golden nor optimistic. A fluorescent tube behind the bottles gave everything that blue institutional cast you associate with hospital corridors or bus station waiting rooms at 2 a.m. Didion had ordered a gin and tonic, which arrived without ice, and she was holding it at an angle that suggested she was studying the liquid rather than planning to drink it. Thompson had a tumbler of something brown and a cigarette he kept gesturing with but hadn't lit, because the bartender had pointed to a No Smoking sign and Thompson had looked at it like it was written in a language he didn't recognize.

"Receipts," Didion said, as though testing the word for structural integrity. "You want to tell a story through receipts."

I said yes. Annotated travel ephemera — boarding passes, hotel bills, the carbon copies of credit card transactions from places that still use carbon copies. The document becomes the narrative. Each piece of paper is a vignette, and the annotations underneath are where the real writing happens.

"That's a filing system," she said. "It's not a story."

Thompson laughed, which was less a laugh than a bark that startled the bartender. "Joan, everything you've ever written is a filing system. You file experience into those perfect little sentences, and the reader opens the drawer and finds devastation arranged alphabetically."

Didion didn't smile, but something shifted around her eyes. A concession she wouldn't voice. "The difference," she said, "is that I don't announce the filing system. You're proposing a piece that walks in and says, 'Hello, I'm structured as receipts.' That's a gimmick until you prove otherwise."

She was right, and I told her so. But I also said: the receipts are the landscape. A receipt is a record of exchange — you gave money, you received a bed, a meal, a bus ticket from one declining town to another. The transaction is the movement. The annotation is the meaning you failed to extract at the time and are now trying to reconstruct from paper evidence.

"Evidence," Didion repeated. She set down the gin and tonic. "That's closer. Evidence of what?"

Of passage. Of having been somewhere that is in the process of no longer being there.

Thompson leaned forward. "Where?"

I said I didn't know yet. A region. Somewhere with enough entropy to justify the melancholy. Former industrial, maybe, or coastal towns losing their coastlines, or—

"Don't pick the Rust Belt," Thompson said. "Every writer with a laptop and a Substack has picked the Rust Belt. It's been elegized to death. You can't elegize a place that's been elegized; it becomes a performance of elegy."

Didion nodded, and I marked this as the first time they'd agreed on anything without visible discomfort. "The place has to resist the narrator's impulse to mourn it," she said. "If it's too obviously decayed, you'll describe ruin. That's photography, not writing."

"There's a difference between a place that's dying and a place that's been dead so long it's started doing something else," Thompson said. He was turning the unlit cigarette between his fingers. "I was in a town in Nevada once — this was '71, maybe '72 — where the last business was a taxidermy shop that had become a kind of bar because the owner kept offering people drinks while they waited for their elk heads. There was no sign outside. You just walked in and there were animals everywhere and a man pouring whiskey. That's not decay. That's mutation."

"Mutation," Didion said, with the precise intonation of someone filing the word for later use. "Yes. The place should have mutated past its original purpose into something the narrator can't quite categorize."

I asked if that was the narrator's problem — the inability to categorize. If every receipt is an attempt to pin the experience to a transaction, a known quantity, and the annotations are where categorization fails.

"Now you're getting somewhere," Didion said. "The receipt says: Room 14, one night, forty-three dollars. The annotation says: The window looked onto a courtyard that had been a swimming pool. There was still a diving board. The room smelled like the cleaning product they use when they've given up on actually cleaning."

Thompson grinned. "The receipt is the controlled version. The annotation is where you lose control."

"No," Didion said, and the correction was immediate, almost reflexive. "The annotation is where you discover what control costs. They're not the same."

This is where their disagreement lives, I think, and I told them so. Thompson's instinct is toward the loss of control as revelation — the moment where the drugs kick in, the car goes off the road, the story lurches into the ditch and finds something true down there. Didion's instinct is the opposite: revelation through the effort of maintaining control even as everything around you warrants losing it. Her sentences get more precise as the world gets worse. His sentences get wilder.

Thompson pointed the cigarette at me. "Don't psychoanalyze us. Write the piece."

"He's not wrong, though," Didion said, which surprised me. "About the difference. The question is which mode serves this particular piece. And I'd argue both, which is why it's interesting and also why it will be difficult."

I asked her to say more.

"The receipts themselves are controlled. They're documents. They have dates, amounts, itemized charges. That's my territory, if you want to be crude about it — the artifact, the fact, the thing you can point to and say 'this happened.' But the annotations..." She paused. "The annotations are where the narrator's composure starts to crack. Not all at once. Incrementally. A factual note on an early receipt. A longer, stranger note on a later one. By the end, the annotations are doing something the receipts can't account for."

Thompson was nodding. "The gonzo seeping in at the margins. Literally at the margins."

"I wouldn't call it gonzo."

"You never would."

"I'd call it — " She stopped. Picked up the gin and tonic, put it down again. "I'd call it the narrator discovering that the documentary impulse is insufficient. That you can record everything — every charge, every hotel, every bus fare — and still miss the thing you went there to find. If you went there to find anything. Which you probably didn't."

"Nobody goes anywhere to find anything," Thompson said. "That's a lie travel writers tell. You go somewhere because you can't stay where you are. The reason comes later, when you need a grant application."

I laughed. Didion didn't. "That's not true," she said. "Some people go somewhere because they believe the going will impose a structure on their confusion. A departure, a route, an arrival. Beginning, middle, end. It never works, but the belief is real."

Thompson looked at her for a long moment. "Is that what you did? Sacramento to New York to Los Angeles to — wherever?"

"I didn't go anywhere to impose structure," Didion said. "I went places because John was working or because I was reporting or because the alternative was staying in a house that was already too full of a particular kind of silence. But I understand the impulse. I've interviewed people who organize their grief into itineraries."

That phrase — *grief organized into itineraries* — landed in the room and none of us touched it for a while. The bartender replaced a bottle. Someone outside was arguing about a parking space.

I said: What if the narrator doesn't know the region is in decline until the receipts start accumulating evidence? The first few are normal — a bus ticket, a lunch, a motel. The annotations are observational, almost cheerful. Then something shifts. The receipts start recording absences. A restaurant that's closed. A hotel that charges for a room but has no other guests. A bus route that exists on the schedule but hasn't actually run in months. The narrator keeps the receipt for the bus that didn't come.

"That's good," Didion said. "Keeping the receipt for the bus that didn't come. That's the whole piece in one gesture."

"Except it's too neat," Thompson said. "If you structure it as a clean decline — things are fine, then they gradually get worse — you're writing a fable about entropy. Entropy doesn't work like that. It's not a slope. It's a series of lurches, and between the lurches, everything looks almost normal."

"Almost normal is harder to write than ruined," Didion said.

"Almost normal is the only thing worth writing about," Thompson said, and there was no irony in it. He'd put the cigarette down. "The moments when you're in a place and everything is functioning — there's a bus, there's a restaurant, the hotel has towels — but something is off. The towels are wrong. Not dirty. Wrong. The wrong color, or the wrong texture, or they're beach towels in a motel three hundred miles from any coast. And nobody mentions it because mentioning it would require acknowledging that the framework has shifted."

Didion was watching him with something that approached respect. "Beach towels three hundred miles from the coast. That's a receipt annotation."

"That's a receipt annotation," Thompson agreed.

I said the risk, which I was starting to feel in my chest as an actual constriction, was sentimentality. A narrator traveling through a landscape of loss, collecting paper evidence of decline — it could tip into requiem. Into beautiful sadness. And beautiful sadness is a lie, or at least a simplification so severe it functions as one.

"Then don't let it be beautiful," Didion said, as though this were obvious. "Keep the prose dry. A receipt is not beautiful. It's a record. Let the sadness arrive through accumulation, not through any single lyrical moment."

"Joan wants you to be Joan," Thompson said.

"I want the piece not to weep," Didion said. "There's a difference."

"The piece should weep once," Thompson said. "Exactly once. A single moment where the narrator's annotative composure ruptures completely and something raw and unprocessed comes through, and then it's over, and the next receipt is a parking garage in some town the narrator can barely remember. Two dollars and fifty cents. No annotation."

Didion was quiet for long enough that I thought she was going to dismiss this. "Where in the piece?"

"Not the end," Thompson said. "God, not the end. The middle. Two-thirds through, maybe. And it shouldn't be about the landscape or the decline. It should be about something personal — a memory, a person, a thing the narrator brought with them and hasn't mentioned until now."

"The undisclosed cargo," Didion said.

"The undisclosed cargo," Thompson said.

I asked: What is it? What did the narrator bring?

Neither of them answered. Thompson picked up the cigarette again. Didion lifted her gin and tonic and actually drank from it this time, a small sip, testing for poison or flat tonic or both.

"That's not for us to decide," Didion said finally. "That's for you to discover while writing. If you know what it is before you start, you'll announce it. You'll foreshadow. The reader will see it coming from the second receipt."

"She's right," Thompson said. "Don't know it. Find it."

I said I was afraid of that.

"Good," Didion said. "Fear is the correct response to not knowing what your piece is about. The incorrect response is to pretend you know."

Thompson stood up, stretched, and patted his jacket as though looking for something — keys, a lighter, a different version of the evening. "One more thing. Sebald walked. Chatwin walked. Walking is the engine of this kind of writing because it's slow enough to notice things and monotonous enough that the mind drifts into the strange. But your narrator isn't walking. Your narrator is taking buses, staying in motels, eating in restaurants. The movement is transactional. Every movement costs money and generates paper."

"Which means the narrator can't drift," Didion said. "Can't wander into meaning. Has to purchase each stage of the journey and document the purchase."

"The documenting IS the wandering," I said, and I wasn't sure if I believed it or if I wanted them to tell me I was wrong.

"Maybe," Thompson said. He was pulling on a jacket that looked like it had survived several decades and at least one fire. "Or maybe the documenting is what prevents the wandering, and the piece is about a narrator who thought they could substitute records for experience and is slowly realizing that the stack of receipts is getting thicker and the understanding is not."

I told him that felt true and also that it terrified me, because it meant the piece might end without arriving anywhere. A final receipt with no annotation. Or an annotation that admits to not understanding.

"That's your ending," Thompson said.

"That's one possible ending," Didion corrected. She had no interest in endings being decided in bars. "There's another possibility, which is that the final document isn't a receipt at all. It's something else — a photograph left in a hotel room, a bus schedule the narrator kept even though they'd stopped traveling, a handwritten note from someone who worked at one of these places. Something that doesn't fit the documentary system. Something that breaks the form."

Thompson paused at the door. "Now you sound like me."

"Occasionally it happens," Didion said. "I recover."

He walked out. Through the window I watched him argue with someone about a taxi, or possibly about the concept of taxis, his voice carrying in fragments that sounded like dispatches from a country none of us had visited.

Didion stayed seated. She turned her glass a quarter turn on the bar.

"The place," she said, not looking at me. "You still haven't chosen the place."

I know. I keep circling it. Maybe that's the problem, or maybe the circling is the method — you pick a place by exhausting the alternatives, and whatever's left when you're too tired to keep eliminating is where you were always going.

"That sounds like something Hunter would say."

It does. I'm worried about that.

"Don't be worried about that," she said. "Be worried about the receipts. Each one has to carry weight it wasn't designed for. A boarding pass is engineered to convey information: seat number, departure time, gate. You're asking it to convey grief, or confusion, or the particular quality of light in a town where the light is the last thing that hasn't deteriorated. That's a lot to ask of a piece of thermal paper."

She finished the gin and tonic. Set the glass down with that deliberate placement she does, where every object occupies exactly the space she intends for it.

"Thermal paper fades," she said. "Did you know that? If you keep a receipt long enough, the ink disappears. The transaction happened, but the evidence is gone."

She left a twenty on the bar and walked out without waiting for change or for me to respond, which was, I think, her way of telling me where the piece ends.
