The bar was wrong for both of them, which is probably why I chose it. A place on Hornsgatan in Södermalm — not a dive, not respectable, just a room with tables and a counter and beer taps and the smell of wet wool from the coats hanging on hooks by the door. Late November. Dark by three. The kind of Stockholm afternoon where the streetlights come on and you can't tell whether they're too early or you're too late.

Raymond arrived first, which surprised me. I'd expected him to make an entrance — the leather jacket, the sneer, the man who'd seen things you couldn't imagine and wanted you to know it. But he was already seated when I came in, drinking what looked like whiskey with no ice, reading a Swedish tabloid he clearly couldn't understand. He looked up when I sat down.

"I don't read Swedish," he said. "But I can read the photographs. A politician. A body. A lake. It's the same grammar everywhere."

I told him we were waiting for someone.

"I know who we're waiting for. Virginia Woolf. You want me to write with Virginia Woolf." He said it flat, without inflection, the way you'd say the name of a disease you'd been diagnosed with. "I've read her. I read *Mrs Dalloway* in a squat in Vauxhall in 1978. The pipes had frozen and I was burning furniture for heat and I read this book about a woman buying flowers for a party and I thought: she understands something I understand, but she understands it from the other direction."

"What direction is that?"

"The inside. She writes the inside of being alive. I write the inside of being dead. The same corridor, different ends."

Woolf arrived without my noticing. She was simply there, sitting in the third chair, her coat still on, her hands in her lap, looking at Raymond with an expression I couldn't decode. Not hostility. Not curiosity exactly. Something closer to recognition — the way you look at a landscape you've dreamed about and then encounter in daylight.

"You're wrong," she said. It was the first thing she said. Not to me. To him. "You don't write the inside of being dead. You write the inside of dying. There's a difference. Death is nothing — silence, absence, the stopped clock. Dying is everything. It's the last full act of consciousness. And you write it with extraordinary attention, despite all the blood and squalor. Your unnamed detective sits with the dead and reconstructs their dying and it's — it's an act of intimacy that your prose pretends to despise."

Raymond stared at her. He drank. He set the glass down.

"I don't pretend to despise anything."

"You pretend to despise beauty. Your sentences refuse loveliness the way a man at a funeral refuses to cry — not because the feeling isn't there, but because expressing it would make it yours, and you can't afford to own it. In *He Died with His Eyes Open*, your detective reads the dead man's journals and falls into his life and the prose becomes — tender. I'll use the word. Tender. And then it pulls back. Hardens. As though tenderness were a wound that needs stitching."

"Tenderness is a wound that needs stitching."

"No. Tenderness is the wound. You can't stitch it. You can only attend to it."

I said nothing for a while. They had found each other without my help, and the thing between them was more productive than anything I could engineer.

"The story," I said eventually. "A Stockholm detective. A dead woman. He's reconstructing her last day."

"Her last day," Woolf said, and something changed in her voice. "A single day. Yes. You could contain an entire life in twenty-four hours if you attended to it properly. Clarissa's day — the party, the flowers, the old lover, the news of the young soldier's death — it's one day but it contains thirty years of living and unliving. Your dead woman's last day should do the same. Not through flashback. Through the quality of attention. The way she noticed things. The particular light on a particular wall at a particular hour."

"She's dead," Raymond said. "She doesn't notice anything anymore. What she noticed is in whatever she left behind — diaries, letters, the position of objects in her flat. The detective reads the evidence and the evidence becomes her consciousness. That's how it works. You don't interview a corpse. You interview the room the corpse is in."

"And the detective's own consciousness?" Woolf pressed. "As he reads her diary, as he handles her possessions — his mind doesn't stay separate. It can't. Consciousness is porous. You read someone's intimate writing and their thoughts colonize yours. Their rhythms enter your breathing. Their way of seeing infects your way of seeing. That's what stream of consciousness *is* — not a technique, a condition. The borders between self and other are permeable, and reading the dead woman's diary will dissolve the detective's borders whether he wants it or not."

Raymond leaned forward. "That's the investigation. That's what makes the Factory novels work. The unnamed detective doesn't keep professional distance. He falls into the victim's life. He becomes contaminated. And the contamination is what produces the truth, because the truth isn't in the forensic evidence — the truth is in why this person died, which means the truth is in how they lived, which means you have to live their life for a while to understand their death."

"So we agree," I said, too quickly.

"We don't agree," Woolf said. "He thinks the detective falls into the victim's life as a method. Deliberately. A professional choice. I think it happens involuntarily. The detective sits in her flat and reads her diary and suddenly he's thinking her thoughts, seeing what she saw from her kitchen window, feeling the weight of her loneliness — not because he chose to, but because consciousness does this. It bleeds. It transfers. The boundary between 'I am reading about this woman' and 'I am this woman' dissolves without the reader — without the detective — noticing the moment of dissolution."

"Can I ask something?" I said. "The risk card requires the story to be a document. Police reports, transcripts, diary fragments. The form itself has to do work. How does stream of consciousness live inside a police report?"

Raymond almost smiled. "It doesn't. That's the friction. A police report is designed to kill consciousness. Subject observed at 14:32. Evidence item 7b recovered from bedside table. The report turns a person into data. But the detective writing the report knows the woman now — he's read her diary, he's walked through her flat, he's absorbed her. So the report keeps breaking. The bureaucratic language cracks and something else comes through. A sentence about evidence recovery becomes a paragraph about the color of her curtains. A forensic note about time of death becomes a meditation on what she was thinking at 16:00 on her last afternoon."

"Yes," Woolf said. "The document form is the container and the consciousness is the thing that exceeds the container. The police report says: *Item recovered from victim's desk: notebook, A5, approximately 40 pages filled.* And then the next section is the notebook itself, and the notebook is written in a voice that is nothing like a police report — it's interior, luminous, full of the kind of perception that bureaucratic language exists to suppress. The friction between the two forms — the report and the diary — that friction is the story."

"Stockholm," I said. "Not Skåne. We need to differentiate from the existing pieces. Urban. Södermalm, maybe. A flat in an old building."

"Good," Raymond said. "Cities are where people die alone. In the country, someone notices. In a city, a woman can be dead for days and the only sign is the smell. The city is the condition that makes this kind of death possible — surrounded by people, absolutely alone."

Woolf looked at him. "You keep saying 'dead.' I want to talk about alive. Her diary — the diary the detective finds — it should be the record of a woman who was desperately, furiously alive. Not happy. Alive. Noticing things. The way light moves across a floor between three and four in the afternoon. The sound of the neighbor's radio through the wall. The specific weight of a coffee cup in her hand at seven in the morning. She was paying attention to her life with the kind of ferocity that people only achieve when they suspect the attention won't last."

"And the detective reads this," I said, "and his professional distance collapses."

"Not collapses," Raymond said. "Erodes. Collapse is dramatic. Erosion is worse. He doesn't have a moment where he breaks down. He has a slow dissolution. Report by report, diary entry by diary entry, his language becomes her language. His perception becomes her perception. By the end, the reader shouldn't be entirely sure who is seeing, who is thinking, who is alive."

"That's Septimus," Woolf said quietly. "Septimus Warren Smith, hearing the birds sing in Greek, the world speaking to him in a language no one else can hear. The detective, reading the dead woman's diary, begins to hear Stockholm the way she heard it — the water, the traffic, the particular echo of footsteps in the tunnel at Slussen. Her consciousness persists in his. Not as a ghost. As a residue."

"As evidence," Raymond said.

"As evidence," Woolf agreed, and for a moment they looked at each other and I felt something I don't often feel in these meetings: two writers from entirely different traditions recognizing that they have been working on the same problem from different ends.

Then Raymond said: "But someone killed her. Don't forget that. Someone walked into her flat and ended her. All the luminosity, all the attention, all the desperate noticing — someone decided it should stop. The investigation has to hold both things: the beauty of her consciousness and the brutality of its extinction. If you write only the beauty, it's sentimental. If you write only the brutality, it's pornographic. The story has to do both and the document form — the police report that keeps cracking open to reveal the diary that keeps being sealed back into evidence — that's how you hold both."

Woolf didn't respond immediately. She picked up a beer mat from the table and turned it over in her fingers, examining it as though it contained information.

"I want her to have written about flowers," she said. "In the diary. Not as a metaphor. As attention. She walked to the florist on Götgatan — or no, she passed the florist and noticed the flowers in the window and didn't go in. And the detective reads this entry and then walks to the same florist and stands outside and sees the same window and understands that she was not a woman who bought flowers but a woman who noticed flowers, which is a different and perhaps more radical act of consciousness."

"And then he goes back to the station," Raymond said, "and writes a report about the florist as a potential witness. And the report says nothing about flowers and everything about flowers."

I asked about the killer. About the investigation's resolution.

"Don't resolve it," Raymond said, and his voice had the weight of someone who had written five novels about an unnamed detective and never once given him peace. "The Factory novels don't resolve. They expose. The detective finds out who killed the person and the finding out destroys him a little more and the killer is someone ordinary, someone whose violence came from the same source as everyone else's — loneliness, need, the city's indifference. The resolution doesn't heal anything. It adds another wound."

"The investigation in this story," Woolf said, "should resolve at the level of evidence and remain open at the level of meaning. The detective files his report. The case is closed. The documents are archived. But the woman's consciousness — her way of seeing, her diary's insistence on attention — that persists in the detective's mind. He cannot un-read what she wrote. He cannot un-see what she saw. The bureaucratic form says: case closed. The consciousness says: nothing is closed. Everything continues."

Raymond stood up. He drained his glass and put on his jacket — leather, worn at the elbows, the kind of jacket that looks like it's been in a fight.

"One more thing," he said. "The dead woman. Give her a profession that involves looking. A photographer, maybe. Or someone who works in archives. Someone whose job it was to see and preserve and catalogue. So that when the detective reads her diary, he's reading the private notebook of a professional observer. The formal training in her attention makes the diary entries sharper, more precise, harder to forget. She wasn't just noticing — she was *documenting*. Her whole life was a document. And now she's been reduced to one."

He left. The door swung shut behind him and a gust of November air came in and the coats on the hooks moved slightly, as if inhabited.

Woolf and I sat.

"He's right about the profession," she said. "But wrong about the reason. Not because it makes the diary sharper. Because it makes the detective's absorption more complete. He's already a man who reads documents for a living. She was a woman who created documents for a living. When he reads her diary, he's not just absorbing her consciousness — he's recognizing his own, reflected in a different key. Two documentary minds. One alive, one dead. And the living one slowly unable to tell which set of case notes are his."
