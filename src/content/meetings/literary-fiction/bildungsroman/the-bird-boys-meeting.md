We met in the back room of a cantina in Ciudad Obregon that Johnson had found the night before and already spoken about twice with the reverence he usually reserved for visions. It was a concrete room with a tin ceiling and no air conditioning, and the beer was warm because the refrigerator had broken sometime in the previous decade and the owner had simply stopped noticing. Jones arrived in a pressed shirt, buttoned to the throat despite the heat, and sat in the chair nearest the wall. Johnson was already there, drinking something from a glass he'd brought himself.

"You picked this place," Jones said, not as a complaint.

"It picked me," Johnson said. "I was walking past and a man was asleep in the doorway with a rooster on his lap, and I thought, this is where we should talk about boys in a wheat field."

I had my notebook open. The table was sticky. I said I wanted to talk about Reyes Vega — the boy who discovers he has a gift for cross-pollination, whose hands are faster than the scientist's, who stays in the field after everyone else has left.

"A boy whose hands know something his mind hasn't caught up to," I said.

Jones folded his arms. "That's most of the people I've ever written about. What do you want me to say about that?"

"I want to know how you'd tell this story. How you'd make the reader understand a kind of intelligence that has no institutional address."

He was quiet for a long time. Johnson drank. The ceiling fan above us made a sound like a bird hitting a window, over and over, and nobody mentioned it.

"I would start by not apologizing for him," Jones said. "He cannot read. He cannot write. He does not know what wheat rust is. These are facts, and they matter, but they are not the thing that matters most. The thing that matters most is that he can feel the difference between a ripe anther and one that's a day too early, with his fingers, without looking. That is a form of knowing. It is the only form of knowing that the wheat cares about. The wheat doesn't care that a man has a degree. The wheat responds to the touch."

Johnson set his glass down. "I knew a man in Iowa City who could tell you the make and year of any car by listening to it idle. He was sleeping in a shed behind the Foxhead. His brain was — well, you know, he was on something most of the time. But he'd hear a car go past and say, '72 Impala, bad lifter,' and he was always right. The knowing was in a part of him that the rest of his life couldn't touch. Couldn't ruin. That's what I'm interested in. The part that survives everything."

"But Vega hasn't survived anything," I said. "He's fifteen. He's been hired to chase birds."

"He's survived being poor in the Yaqui Valley in 1946," Jones said, with a flatness that functioned as a correction. "He has survived not going to school. He has survived the particular silence of a boy who is smart enough to know that no one is measuring his kind of smart. That is its own kind of survival, and if you miss it, you will write him as a charming innocent, which he is not."

That landed. I scratched out the first three lines in my notebook.

"The scientist," Johnson said, leaning back so his chair creaked against the wall. "Tell me about the scientist."

"An American agronomist. Tall, sunburned, speaks terrible Spanish. He's running a wheat-breeding program in a field outside Ciudad Obregon. He hires these four boys — teenagers — to scare birds off the test plots. Eventually he teaches them to do the crosses."

"And we're telling it from the boy's perspective."

"From Vega's, yes. The scientist is seen from outside. We know what Vega sees: a strange, obsessive man who sits on a stool in the heat and does something delicate and incomprehensible to the heads of wheat plants."

Johnson's eyes went somewhere else for a moment. "I want the scientist to be a little bit holy. Not saintly — holy in the way of someone who has given himself entirely to something the world hasn't decided to care about yet. The saints I've known in my life were mostly people nobody was watching. They were doing the thing they were doing with total commitment in total obscurity, and if you happened to see it, you understood something about devotion that churches get wrong."

Jones looked at him. "You want to make the scientist holy."

"I want the boy to see something in the scientist that the boy can't name. Something about how the man touches the wheat. The attention he gives it. And the boy recognizes it because it's the same kind of attention he will eventually give the wheat himself, but he doesn't know that yet. He just sees this strange man whose hands are careful."

"That's fine," Jones said. "As long as the story doesn't become about the scientist. The danger is that the white American with the grand vision becomes the center of gravity, and the Mexican boy becomes the instrument of that vision. I've read that story. I've read it a hundred times. I will not help you write it."

"No," I said quickly.

"The boy does not exist to complete the scientist's project. The boy exists. The wheat exists. The field exists. The scientist is weather. He is a condition the boy encounters, like the heat or the birds. He changes things, yes. But the story belongs to the hands in the field, not to the mind with the plan."

Johnson nodded slowly. "But there's something else. There's the thing that happens when Vega starts to get good at the crosses. When his hands start moving faster than anyone else's. I want that moment to arrive the way moments of grace arrive in real life — which is to say, without announcement. He's doing it one morning and he realizes he's been doing it differently, and he doesn't know when the difference started. There was no decision. His hands just changed what they were doing. And he looks at the wheat head and it's done, and it's done better than the way he was taught."

"The scissors technique," I said. "Historically, Vega invented a faster way to remove the glumes — using small scissors instead of the method the scientist showed them."

"Don't call it a technique," Jones said. "Not in the story. A technique is something someone names and describes and publishes. What the boy does is something his hands figure out in a wheat row at five in the morning when no one is watching. It becomes a technique later, when someone decides it needs a name. In the moment, it's just his fingers finding the faster way."

Johnson pointed at Jones. "That. That's the whole story."

"It's not the whole story."

"It's the whole question. Is it his? The thing his hands can do — does it belong to him? Because the scientist's program will use it. The program will benefit. The wheat will grow in places it couldn't grow before, and millions of people will eat because of it, and the boy will still be in the field, and he will not be in the book that gets written about it. He will be a footnote, if he's lucky. So: is the gift his?"

Jones looked at me. "This is where your story either means something or doesn't. The boy stays. That's the end of your pitch — he stays. The question is what staying means. Is it devotion? Is it limitation? Is it the only world he knows and therefore the only world he can imagine? Or is it something else — is it that the work has become him, has become the shape of his body and his days, and leaving would require him to become someone he doesn't know how to be?"

"All of those," I said.

"No. Pick one, and let the reader suspect the others. If you try to hold all of them at once, you'll write a story about a symbol instead of a person."

I wrote that down.

Johnson picked at the label on a bottle that may or may not have been his. "I want the other boys. There are four of them at the start. I want to feel each one of them leave. Not big dramatic departure scenes. Just — one morning there are three. And then one morning there are two. And Vega doesn't talk about it much. He notices, the way you notice when a sound you've been hearing for months stops. The silence where Tomás used to be. The empty row."

"Denis is right about that," Jones said, and it was one of those concessions that seemed to cost him nothing, which I think meant it cost him something. "The departures should be geological. Slow. The reader should almost not notice each one until the accumulation becomes the point. And Vega — the boy — he should not process them as losses. He should process them as facts. Alejandro is married now. Roberto went north. They are gone. He is here. The wheat needs crossing."

"But there is a loss," I said. "There has to be."

"There is a loss," Jones agreed. "But he does not know how to feel it. He has no vocabulary for it. He has a vocabulary of wheat, of scissors, of mornings in the field. He does not have a vocabulary of grief. So the loss is there and it is real and the reader feels it, but the boy does not feel it the way the reader does. That gap — between what the boy can say and what the reader understands — that is where the story lives."

Johnson stood up and walked to the window. Outside, the street was empty in the midday heat. A dog was sleeping against a wall in a strip of shade that was narrowing.

"I want one scene," he said, without turning around. "The boy is in the field and the light is a certain way. Early morning, maybe. The dew is still on the wheat. And he's working, and his hands are moving, and for a moment he sees himself from outside — not an out-of-body experience, nothing mystical, just that thing that happens sometimes where you catch yourself in the middle of your own life and you think, this is what I am doing. This is what I do. And it's not a revelation. It's more like vertigo. The ground shifts and then settles again and you keep working. But for that one second he saw the whole shape of it — the field, the wheat, his hands, the mornings that would be the same morning for years. And he didn't flinch."

"And he didn't understand it," Jones added.

"No. He didn't understand it. He just kept going."

I closed my notebook. Not because I was done, but because the thing I needed wasn't going to arrive through more conversation. It was somewhere in the space between what Jones insisted on — the boy's intelligence as complete in itself, unrecognized and unconcerned with recognition — and what Johnson wanted: that one flare of accidental vision, the moment when the ordinary becomes briefly luminous before the light settles back into the dirt.

"There's something I haven't figured out," I said. "The ending. He stays. But what does staying look like? What's the last image?"

Jones shook his head. "You're asking me to write the story for you."

"I'm asking what you'd want to see."

"I'd want to see morning. Another morning. The field. The wheat at a new stage. His hands doing what they do. And nothing in the prose telling me this is beautiful or tragic or meaningful. Just the hands. Just the wheat. The reader brings the rest."

Johnson turned from the window. "I'd want one wrong thing. One detail that doesn't fit. A bird he doesn't chase. A sound from somewhere past the edge of the field. Something that suggests the world goes on past what we're seeing, and what we're seeing is not all there is."

They looked at each other. Neither of them was going to bend toward the other's ending.

"Fine," I said. "I'll figure it out."

"You won't figure it out," Johnson said. "You'll write it and it'll tell you."

Jones said nothing. He was looking at his hands on the table, and I wondered if he was thinking about the boy's hands, or about something else entirely, and I knew better than to ask.
