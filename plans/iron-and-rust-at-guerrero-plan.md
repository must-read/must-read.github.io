## Research Notes

### Prose Samples Collected

**Roberto Bolaño (By Night in Chile, 2666)**:
- Long, flowing sentences without paragraph breaks; stream-of-consciousness that accumulates detail rather than organizing it. "One has to be responsible, as I have always said. One has a moral obligation to take responsibility for one's actions, and that includes one's words and silences, yes, one's silences, because silences rise to heaven too."
- Forensic cataloguing of marginal lives reduced to statistics. In 2666's "Part About the Crimes," hundreds of pages about lives the official record compressed to one-sentence reports. Clinical precision rendering the human to the bureaucratic.
- The Amalfitano section: "they want to watch the great masters spar, but they have no interest in real combat, when the great masters struggle against that something, that something that terrifies us all."
- Characteristic: digression within digression, enumeration of emotional states, deadpan juxtaposition of cosmic and domestic.

**Svetlana Alexievich (Secondhand Time, Voices from Chernobyl)**:
- Testimony-as-form: "My final wish is that you record the truth. But my truth, not yours." The subject resists the recorder.
- The register of pride-and-bewilderment simultaneous: people who built impossible things under impossible conditions and can't quite believe they succeeded, but also can't imagine not having tried.
- "We won, indeed. But our great victory didn't make our country great."
- Her subjects speak with matter-of-fact precision about technical work, then suddenly veer into emotional territory without transition — because for them the technical and the emotional are the same thing.

**Selected concepts**:

1. **Backlash (engineering)** — "the maximum distance or angle through which any part of a mechanical system may be moved in one direction without applying appreciable force or motion to the next part in mechanical sequence." This is perfect for a scene where the brothers discover that the gears they've salvaged from different machines have incompatible backlash — when the threshing cylinder engages, there's a dead zone, a gap of nothing where motion should transfer but doesn't. Vicente will diagnose this by sound. The concept also works metaphorically: the backlash between what the brothers build and what the American report records. The gap where force is applied but nothing moves.

2. **Gudgeon pin** — the small, critical component connecting piston to connecting rod. The brothers need one and don't have the right size. This becomes a concrete engineering problem: the gudgeon pin from a Ford truck engine is 22mm, but the bore in the connecting rod they're using (from a Chevrolet) is 20mm. They can't just force it. Jose wants to machine it down; Vicente says machining will weaken the case hardening. The solution they find — a shimmed bushing made from a shell casing — is the kind of improvisation that looks crude but requires precise understanding of load distribution. A tiny component that holds the whole machine together.

### Historical Research

**Rockefeller Foundation Mexican Agricultural Program (1943-1965)**:
- Headquartered Mexico City, field operations at National School of Agriculture in Chapingo, ~15 miles away
- 150 acres donated by Mexican government for experimentation
- Key staff: Edwin Wellhausen (corn), William Colwell (soil), John Niederhauser (potato), Norman Borlaug (wheat/pathology)
- "In 1941 Mexico had no academics with advanced degrees in agriculture"
- Over 550 Mexican agriculture graduates trained as apprentices
- Mexico achieved corn self-sufficiency by 1948, wheat by 1956
- Mexican field technicians contributed to seed production; their names rarely recorded

**Texcoco/Ejido Context**:
- Ejido system (communal land) central to 1940s Mexico after Cárdenas reforms
- Haciendas almost entirely disappeared from central/southern Mexico by late 1930s
- Small holdings of 10-20 acres + ejidos became dominant
- Texcoco basin: government invested in lakebed reclamation, tree planting, dust storm prevention

**Combine Harvester Technical Details (1940s era)**:
- **Header**: reciprocating sickle bar cuts stalks, reel pushes grain onto platform, canvas apron delivers to threshing cylinder
- **Threshing**: rotating drum with rasp bars (grooved steel bars) against concave (shaped half-drum with meshed grill). Cylinder-concave action separates grain from straw
- **Separation**: straw walkers use shaking motion above sieves; grain falls through holes, straw carried out the rear
- **Cleaning**: chaffer sieve and cleaning shoe; air blast from fan separates chaff from grain
- **Drive**: power from engine through belt systems; multiple gear ratios needed for different components
- **Key problem areas for scrap-built machine**: belt tension, cylinder-concave clearance, sickle bar alignment, gear backlash in drive train, header platform levelness

## Title

**Iron and Rust at Guerrero**

The title uses the family name as both surname and place — the Guerrero brothers, the state of Guerrero, and "guerrero" meaning warrior. Iron is the material; rust is the condition of the material they start with. The "at" construction places us specifically, like a field report or location marker. Does not start with "The."

---

## Premise

Vicente and Jose Guerrero are brothers who run a junkyard in Texcoco, near Mexico City, in 1944. The Rockefeller Foundation's agricultural program — Americans trying to modernize Mexican farming — hires them through a government intermediary to build farm equipment from scrap. They first construct a usable tractor from three dead machines. Then the program asks for something harder: a combine harvester. Neither brother has seen one intact. They receive a photograph and a parts manual in English. Over six months they build the machine from junkyard scrap, working from the photograph and their own understanding of how metal, torque, and motion relate to each other. The story follows the construction — the arguments, the substitutions, the failures, the one photograph consulted like scripture — and ends not at the triumph but after it, back in the junkyard, where nothing has changed.

## Protagonists

### Vicente Guerrero (elder brother, ~42)
- The diagnostician. Listens to metal — can tell by the sound of a gear train which tooth is stripped, which bearing is failing. His intelligence is acoustic and spatial.
- **Specific flaw**: He cannot delegate or accept that Jose's solutions might be better than his own. When Jose is right about something, Vicente doesn't concede — he reframes it as his own idea arriving late. This is invisible to him and maddening to Jose.
- Married to **Pilar**, who keeps the books for the junkyard and understands exactly how much the government program isn't paying them. She is skeptical not of the brothers' ability but of the arrangement: they build the machine, the government takes it, and the brothers get a wage that doesn't cover the scrap they're consuming.
- Hands: scarred across the knuckles from a fan belt incident in 1938. The right index finger bends slightly wrong from a reset fracture.

### Jose Guerrero (younger brother, ~38)
- The fabricator. His intelligence is in his hands — he can shape metal to tolerances that shouldn't be possible with the tools he has. Where Vicente diagnoses, Jose builds.
- **Specific flaw**: He defers to Vicente in all arguments, then quietly does what he wanted to do anyway. Passive resistance disguised as compliance. This has been their dynamic since childhood, since their father gave Vicente the better tools.
- Married to **Dolores**, who feeds the workers who come to the junkyard for parts and treats the combine project as proof that her husband's skills deserve more than a junkyard. She's not sentimental about it — she's practical. These skills could be employed elsewhere, for more money, if the brothers would leave.
- Hands: thick calluses on the palm ridges from years of hammer work. No visible scars — Jose doesn't get cut because his hands know where the metal is going before it gets there.

### Pilar (Vicente's wife, ~40)
- Manages the junkyard's finances. Understands money as a system in the way the brothers understand machines as systems.
- She sees the combine project clearly: unpaid labor dressed up as opportunity. The tractor they built — the government took it and it's pulling a plow on an ejido forty kilometers away and the brothers got nothing but the next contract.
- Not bitter. Precise. She tallies what the combine costs in scrap they can't sell, hours they can't bill, and she tells Vicente the number every Sunday.

### Dolores (Jose's wife, ~36)
- Feeds people. The junkyard operates as an informal community resource — neighbors come for parts, for advice about trucks, for Dolores's coffee. She sees the combine as the brothers' best work and doesn't understand why their best work always belongs to someone else.
- She's the one who tacks the photograph of the intact combine to the workshop wall. Not because the brothers asked her to — because she saw Jose holding it in both hands, studying it in the light from the door, and she found a nail.

## Structure

The story is organized in **six sections** across approximately six months (March-September 1944), plus a brief coda. Not chapters — sections separated by white space, each centered on a specific engineering problem and its human substrate.

### Beat 1: The Photograph (March 1944) — ~800 words
The photograph arrives with the contract. An American has taken a picture of an intact Allis-Chalmers All-Crop combine in a field in Iowa. The photograph is black-and-white, slightly overexposed, showing the machine in three-quarter profile. The brothers study it at the kitchen table. Vicente holds it at arm's length; Jose brings it close to his face. They're reading different things: Vicente is tracing the drive train, the power transfer paths; Jose is looking at the welds, the brackets, the joints. The parts manual accompanies it — 84 pages in English. They cannot read English. Jose opens the manual to the diagrams and ignores the words. Dolores pins the photograph to the workshop wall.

**Engineering focus**: Introduction of the machine's anatomy as read from a photograph. What's visible, what's behind the visible, what has to be inferred.

**Bolaño register**: The photograph as an object that will be consulted like scripture. The grease stains that will accumulate on it. The paragraph should list what the brothers can and cannot see, in the forensic-cataloguing mode.

### Beat 2: The Scrap Audit (April 1944) — ~900 words
The brothers walk the junkyard, identifying what they have. The narrative inventories: fourteen truck axles, nine engine blocks (four Ford, three Chevrolet, two unknown), six sets of leaf springs, a pile of angle iron from a collapsed water tower, two hundred feet of chain of varying gauge, belt pulleys from a cotton gin that closed in 1940. Against this inventory, they map the combine's requirements. What they have, what they lack, what can be substituted. Jose draws the header assembly in chalk on the workshop floor — full scale, six feet wide. Vicente argues it should be eight feet. The argument is not about the header — it's about a truck they repaired differently in 1938, about which brother their father trusted with the better files.

**Engineering focus**: The specific scrap-to-part mapping. A Ford truck axle becoming the main drive shaft. Cotton gin pulleys repurposed for the belt system. The canal pipe that will become the threshing cylinder housing.

**Alexievich register**: The inventory as testimony. Each piece of scrap has a provenance: the water tower fell in the earthquake of '41, the cotton gin belonged to Don Esteban, the Ford engines came from American cars that couldn't be repaired after the roads washed out. The junkyard is an oral history of the valley's last decade.

### Beat 3: The Gudgeon Pin Problem (May-June 1944) — ~1,200 words
The longest section. The brothers have the frame built, the header partially assembled, but the engine adaptation is failing. The gudgeon pin from a Ford truck engine is 22mm; the connecting rod bore from the Chevrolet block they're using is 20mm. Jose wants to machine the pin down on the lathe. Vicente says machining will cut through the case-hardened surface into softer steel beneath — the pin will fail under load. They argue for two days. Pilar arrives on Sunday with the running cost of the project: the scrap consumed is worth more than the contract pays. She tells Vicente this while Jose is underneath the machine, listening. Dolores brings coffee and tortillas. The wives sit on the tailgate of a dead Dodge while the brothers argue inside the workshop, and their conversation — about the price of corn flour, about whether the ejido will get irrigation this year, about Pilar's sister's husband who went north — is the story's clearest window into the world outside the junkyard.

Jose solves the gudgeon pin problem at three in the morning, alone, with a shimmed bushing made from a .30-06 shell casing. The brass is the right hardness. The wall thickness, when turned on the lathe, seats the pin at the correct interference fit. He doesn't tell Vicente how he solved it. In the morning Vicente inspects the assembly, hears the engine turn over smoothly, and says nothing about the shell casing gleaming inside the bore.

**Engineering focus**: The gudgeon pin as the story's central technical problem. Specific dimensions, specific materials, specific solutions. The shell casing bushing is the kind of detail that should make a reader who knows engines stop and think: that would actually work.

**Dual register**: The brothers' argument (Bolaño: personal rivalry expressed through technical dispute) intercut with the wives' conversation (Alexievich: the economy of daily life as testimony, the practical reality surrounding the machine).

### Beat 4: Harold's Visit (July 1944) — ~700 words
An American from the Rockefeller Foundation program arrives to inspect progress. His name is Harold. He is young, sunburned, speaks textbook Spanish. He walks around the combine frame, touches the welds, looks at the header. He asks about the threshing cylinder-concave clearance. Vicente tells him — "three millimeters." Harold nods. He does not understand what he is looking at. He sees a combine. He does not see the six months, the fourteen failed approaches to the belt drive, the night Jose stayed until four in the morning. Harold produces a small notebook and writes something in English. The brothers watch him write. He shakes their hands, gets in a government truck, and leaves.

After he's gone, Jose says: "What did he write?"

Vicente says: "One sentence."

**Engineering focus**: The cylinder-concave clearance as a number — a number that represents weeks of adjustment, shimming, testing. Harold hears the number and writes it down. The brothers lived inside the number.

**The 2666 operation**: This scene is the story's structural thesis — the moment where the brothers' six months of work is compressed into a single sentence in someone else's report. The reader has spent four sections watching the work. Harold reduces it. The reader should feel the violence of that reduction.

### Beat 5: The First Pass (September 1944) — ~800 words
The combine is transported to a wheat field on an ejido outside Chapingo. The brothers drive it — Vicente at the controls, Jose walking alongside watching the header. The sickle bar engages. The reel turns. Wheat enters the machine and the threshing cylinder takes it. There's a sound the brothers have never heard before: the sound of their machine working. It's not the sound from the photograph — there is no sound in a photograph — but it's the sound they've been building toward for six months, and it's wrong. Not broken-wrong. Wrong the way their machine is wrong: asymmetrical, heavier in the low frequencies, with a rattle in the straw walkers that Vicente can hear and Jose can't. The wheat comes out clean. The combine works.

The wheat comes out clean. This sentence again, because the brothers would say it twice.

**Engineering focus**: The sound profile of the working machine as the sensory climax. What the sickle bar sounds like cutting. What the threshing cylinder sounds like engaged. The rattle in the straw walkers. Sound, not image, is how the brothers experience their creation.

**Emotional register**: Not triumph. Something closer to bewilderment — the Alexievich register where people who've done an impossible thing can't quite believe they've done it, and also can't imagine not having done it.

### Beat 6 / Coda: The Junkyard (same day, evening) — ~700 words
Back at the junkyard. The combine is on the ejido. The government will keep it. The brothers are back among the same scrap piles, the same broken axles, the same dogs sleeping under the same truck bodies. Nothing has changed. The photograph is still on the wall, grease-stained, smudged at the corners. Dolores has made dinner. Pilar has a new tally — the final cost of the combine, materials and labor, versus what the contract paid. The deficit is specific. She tells Vicente the number and he nods.

Jose is already looking at something in the junkyard — a piece of scrap, a piece of a machine that has a shape he recognizes as the beginning of something else. He hasn't been asked to build anything new. Nobody has given him a photograph. But his hands know what scrap looks like when it's not scrap yet, when it's a machine that hasn't been assembled.

The story ends here. Not at the triumph. Not at the loss. At the junkyard, where the brothers' intelligence lives, unnamed and unrecorded, in the gap between the photograph and the pile of rust.

## Emotional Trajectory

1. **Curiosity tinged with skepticism** (Beat 1) — The photograph as both promise and impossibility
2. **Inventory as archaeology** (Beat 2) — The junkyard reveals its history; the scale of the task becomes real
3. **Friction and intimacy** (Beat 3) — The brothers' rivalry as the engine of the work; the wives as the story's other intelligence
4. **The violence of compression** (Beat 4) — Harold's visit as the story's structural wound
5. **Bewildered achievement** (Beat 5) — The combine works, and the working is stranger than the not-working
6. **Return to the real** (Beat 6) — Nothing has changed except the brothers, and even that change has no name

The emotional arc is NOT: failure → struggle → triumph. It IS: competence → friction → partial understanding → invisible erasure → continuation. The last beat should feel like the first beat of a story nobody will write.

- **Identifiable passages**: Pilar's Sunday cost tallies. Dolores pinning the photograph to the wall. The wives on the tailgate of the dead Dodge, talking about corn flour prices while their husbands argue about bore tolerances.

### From Work X — 2666
- **Structural operation**: The entire story performs the 2666 move: taking a single sentence from a report ("a combine was built from scratch") and expanding it into the world that sentence erased. Harold's visit makes the operation visible — the reader watches the expansion, then watches the re-compression, and understands what reports do to lives.
- **Tonal register**: The clinical, accumulating quality of Bolaño's catalogues. The way 2666 renders extraordinary events in the same flat register as ordinary ones.

## Key Technical Details for the Writer

The combine must be rendered with mechanical specificity. The writer must get these details right:

1. **Header assembly**: Reciprocating sickle bar (the brothers fabricate one from hay-mower blades welded end-to-end), reel (made from bicycle wheel rims and steel rod), canvas apron (actual canvas, oiled against moisture)
2. **Threshing mechanism**: Cylinder with rasp bars (the brothers use hardened steel angle iron, ground to profile on a bench grinder) rotating inside a concave (formed from heavy-gauge expanded metal, curved over a barrel form). Cylinder-concave clearance of 3mm is critical — too tight and the grain is crushed, too loose and it passes through unthreshed.
3. **Drive train**: Belt-driven from a truck engine adapted to run at lower RPM. Multiple pulleys of different diameters for different components. The backlash in salvaged gears is a recurring problem.
4. **The gudgeon pin solution**: Ford pin (22mm) into Chevy bore (20mm) via brass bushing turned from .30-06 shell casing. This is the story's signature technical detail.
5. **Sound**: The combine's sound is how Vicente experiences its correctness. The writer should describe what the machine sounds like at each stage — the sickle bar, the threshing cylinder, the straw walkers. The final working sound should be specific and imperfect.

---

## Things the Writer Must NOT Do

1. Do NOT have the brothers learn English or read the manual's text. They work from diagrams and the photograph.
2. Do NOT make the combine's completion a triumphant climax. The wheat comes out clean. The brothers experience bewilderment, not joy.
3. Do NOT explain the brothers' intelligence in abstract terms. No "embodied cognition," no "practical genius." Show the wrench, the bolt, the sound of the engine. Let the reader do the naming.
4. Do NOT give Harold (the American) more than 700 words. He inspects, writes one sentence, leaves. He is not a character — he is the system made briefly visible.
5. Do NOT end on a note of hope or quiet triumph. End in the junkyard. End with Jose looking at a piece of scrap. End with continuation, not resolution.
6. Do NOT announce the theme. The gap between the photograph and the machine, between the machine and the sentence in the report — these are the story's concerns. They must never be stated.
7. Do NOT use banned names (Marcus, Chen).
8. Do NOT give the brothers tidy epiphanies or moments of recognition. The knowledge is in their hands. It stays there.

---

## Narrative Voice

Third person, close alternation between Vicente and Jose. The prose should be concrete and technical when describing the machine, warmer and more elastic when the wives are present. Long sentences for the inventory sections (Bolaño register). Shorter, more declarative sentences for the engineering problem-solving (the brothers' own thinking rhythm). The narrator should not editorialize — should not tell the reader what to think about intelligence, credentials, or the gap between making and recording. The closest the narration comes to commentary is in the structure itself: the expansion of one sentence into a world, then the world's compression back to one sentence.
