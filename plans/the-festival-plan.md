# Story Plan: the-festival

**Genre**: horror / folk-horror
**AuthorA**: Robert Aickman (oblique dreamlike narration, British understatement, stories that refuse to explain)
**AuthorB**: Mariana Enriquez (Latin American settings, folk tradition + urban poverty, supernatural as collective historical violence)
**WorkX**: Midsommar (outsider in rural community's rituals, grief weaponized, sunlit horror)
**WorkY**: The Lottery by Shirley Jackson (ritual performed without questioning, selection mechanism, communal violence as necessity)
**Target**: 3,000 words (2,700–3,300)
**Risk Card**: None

## Writing Samples

### Source: Robert Aickman (Author A)
**Origin**: Aphorisms and stylistic excerpts collected across Aickman's "strange stories"
**Why representative**: Demonstrates his measured, epigrammatic prose — each sentence placed like a card on a table, emotional restraint masking deep unease

> "It is amazing how full a life a man can lead without for one moment being alive at all."

> "Dreams … are misleading, because they make life seem real."

> "There are no beautiful clocks. Everything to do with time is hideous."

Aickman's technique in "The Hospice": A travelling salesman named Maybury, lost and low on petrol, arrives at a Victorian establishment offering "GOOD FARE / SOME ACCOMMODATION." He is seated at a long table with other guests, served enormous quantities of rich food in oppressive heat. The hospitality is suffocating — too much food, too much warmth, too much kindness — and every attempt to leave is deflected with gentle insistence. The horror is never named. The protagonist's bewilderment is social rather than existential: he feels he is being rude by wanting to leave. The story never explains what the Hospice is. The food keeps coming.

**Key Aickman techniques for the writer**:
- British understatement as horror vehicle: the protagonist's distress expressed as social discomfort, not terror
- Refusal to explain: the reader is given just enough to feel dread but never enough to name it
- Precise, measured sentences — "each word placed like a card on a table" (from the meeting)
- Characters who respond to the uncanny with embarrassment rather than fear
- Food and hospitality as instruments of entrapment

### Source: Mariana Enriquez (Author B)
**Origin**: Style analysis from *Things We Lost in the Fire* and "The Dirty Kid"
**Why representative**: Shows her technique of grounding supernatural horror in material poverty and social reality

Enriquez's opening of "The Dirty Kid": "My family thinks I'm crazy because I choose to live in the family house in Constitución, my paternal grandparents' house, a hulk of stone and green-painted iron doors on Virreyes Street, with Art Deco details and old mosaics on the floor so worn out that, had it occurred to me to wax them, I could have set up a skating rink."

Her prose is described as "hypnotic prose that gives grace to the grotesque" and "relentlessly visceral." She shifts "psychic interiority outward into a landscape made ghastly by political and economic forces." The supernatural in her fiction is never separable from social conditions — poverty, historical violence, class oppression. A key technique: stating horror flatly, in the same register as domestic detail. The ghosts and the grocery list occupy the same sentence structure.

**Key Enriquez techniques for the writer**:
- Root the supernatural in material conditions: drought, poverty, distance from power
- Flat, declarative sentences for maximum impact — horror stated as fact, not performed
- Specific Argentine geography and food culture as atmosphere (not set-dressing)
- The supernatural as infrastructure: "The dead are maintained the way roads are maintained"
- Social realism bleeding into the uncanny without a clear border

### Source: Midsommar (Work X)
**Origin**: Ari Aster's 2019 folk horror film
**Why representative**: The outsider-among-welcoming-ritualists structure; grief weaponized into belonging; sunlit horror that denies the protagonist darkness to hide in

Dani Ardor, devastated by the murder-suicide of her family, travels to rural Sweden with her emotionally distant boyfriend. The Hårga commune absorbs her grief — they mirror her crying, hold her, validate her pain in ways her boyfriend never did. The horror and the healing are the same event. The rituals are presented as beautiful, communal, ancient, and they involve human sacrifice. The film's central insight: the community's warmth is genuine AND predatory. These are not opposites. The May Queen selection is both an honour and a death sentence, and Dani chooses to participate because participation is the only form of belonging available to her.

**Key Midsommar elements for the writer**:
- Sunlit horror: everything visible, nothing hidden, yet the protagonist cannot see
- The outsider's grief makes them vulnerable to absorption
- Genuine kindness as the mechanism of entrapment
- Rituals presented through bureaucratic organisation (schedules, roles, procedures)
- The protagonist chooses complicity over isolation

### Source: "The Lottery" by Shirley Jackson (Work Y)
**Origin**: Published in The New Yorker, 1948
**Why representative**: The masterwork of communal violence performed as administrative routine

Jackson's technique: the story opens on "a clear and sunny" June morning. Children gather stones. Adults chat about planting and taxes. The lottery is run by Mr. Summers, who "had time and energy to devote to civic activities." The black box is shabby, familiar, boring. The selection process is procedural — names called alphabetically, slips of paper, a bureaucratic mechanism indistinguishable from any village business. The violence, when it comes, arrives in the same flat prose as the weather report. No one questions the lottery because no one remembers questioning it. The ritual's horror is its normalcy.

**Key Jackson elements for the writer**:
- Bureaucratic prose for unbearable content: committee minutes syntax for human sacrifice
- Mundane daylight — "the light you do errands in" (from the meeting)
- The selection mechanism embedded in petty administrative business
- Characters who participate because participation is the default, not a choice
- The reader understands before the characters do (or before they admit they do)

## Wikipedia Concepts

Research yielded the following culturally grounded details (drawn from searches on Argentine northwest traditions, Pachamama rituals, algarroba/patay, and the Fiesta de la Telesita):

### Concept 1: Patay (algarroba flour bread)
Patay is an unleavened bread made from the flour of the white carob tree (Prosopis alba), eaten by the Diaguita, Wichí, Huarpe, and other indigenous peoples of the Argentine northwest since pre-Columbian times. By the 1630s, Spanish settlers had adopted it. It has 50% carbohydrate and 10% protein content. No yeast, no gluten. A food that predates the colonizers, made from a tree that was there before anyone.

**Integration**: The bread at the festival meal is patay — dense, sweet, unfamiliar to the protagonist. She eats it and cannot stop. The bread basket refills. The specificity of patay (its pre-Columbian origins, its connection to the land itself, the fact that the tree was there before any human settlement) grounds the horror in something older than any culture. The bread is not symbolic. It is material. It feeds.

### Concept 2: Corpachada (feeding the earth)
The corpachada is the central ritual of Pachamama worship in the Argentine northwest. At midnight on July 31st, families dig a hole in the ground — "the mouth of Pachamama" — and place offerings: a clay pot with cooked food, chicha, coca leaves, lit cigarettes stuck in the earth, bits of coloured alpaca wool, serpentine and confetti. The earth eats first. Reciprocity: the earth feeds the people, the people feed the earth.

**Integration**: The festival in the story is timed around August 1st. The organising committee's minutes include a line item about "preparing the mouth" — a hole to be dug, offerings to be placed. The protagonist, reading the agenda, assumes this is a cultural demonstration for the academic visitors. It is not a demonstration. The mouth is real. The earth does eat. And the question of what the earth is fed, specifically — what constitutes a sufficient offering in a year of drought — is the thing the committee discusses in the same tone they use for folding chairs.

## 1. Premise

A British anthropologist attends a harvest festival in a remote village in the Argentine northwest at the invitation of her closest colleague, a woman who grew up there. The festival has an organising committee, a printed schedule, disagreements about logistics. Embedded in the committee's agenda — between the line item about borrowed tables and the one about parking — is a selection process the anthropologist cannot quite parse. By the time she understands the grammar of what is happening, she is already eating at the long table, and her appetite is not her own.

The engine: Can you refuse hospitality that is genuine? At what point does gratitude become complicity?

---

## 2. Protagonist

**Name**: Bridget Lowe
**Age**: 41
**Situation**: A British anthropologist specialising in syncretic folk ritual in post-colonial contexts, based at a mid-ranking university (not Oxbridge — she's acutely aware of the distinction). Unmarried, childless, not unhappily so but aware that colleagues have stopped asking. She has published a well-received monograph on the persistence of pre-Columbian offering practices in the Argentine northwest. She has visited Argentina seven times but never this village.

**What she WANTS**: To observe the festival with scholarly precision, to see the corpachada performed authentically rather than for tourists, to gather material for her next paper.

**What she NEEDS**: To stop treating other people's traditions as texts to be read. To recognise that she is not a reader but a participant — that she was invited not as an observer but as a guest, and that guest-hood carries obligations she has not considered.

**Her flaw**: She believes her education makes her a good reader of situations. She trusts her own interpretive framework so completely that she cannot distinguish between understanding a ritual and being subject to one. Her anthropological training is her blindfold: she keeps translating what she sees into categories (syncretism, cultural performance, post-colonial negotiation) and the categories insulate her from the thing itself.

**Specific wound**: Three years ago, she supervised a PhD student named Lucía Robles from Tucumán. Lucía's thesis was on harvest festivals in the Quebrada de Humahuaca. During fieldwork, Lucía had what Bridget called "a crisis of methodology" — she began participating in the rituals she was meant to observe, then refused to write about them, then abandoned the PhD entirely. Bridget understood this as a failure of professional boundaries. She wrote Lucía a careful letter about the importance of critical distance. Lucía did not reply. Bridget still thinks she was right. She was not right.

## 3. Structure

### Opening (~400 words): The Agenda
Bridget sits in a plastic chair in a community hall, reading a photocopied agenda for the Festival de la Tierra. The agenda is in Spanish, handwritten, then photocopied badly so that some items are illegible. She can read most of it: logistics, food preparation assignments, a schedule of music and dancing, the time the priest will arrive (he is driving from Tucumán and may be late). One line item, near the bottom, uses a word she doesn't recognise — not Spanish, possibly Cacán or Quechua. She underlines it to ask her colleague Sofía about later. The community hall smells of floor wax and empanadas. A man is arguing with a woman about whether they have enough folding chairs. Everything is completely ordinary.

### Inciting Disruption (~500 words): The Hospitality
Sofía, her colleague and host, arrives and greets everyone by name. She is genuinely warm — not performing warmth but radiating it. She introduces Bridget to committee members who shake her hand and say they've heard so much about her, that her monograph was read by Don Aurelio (the village's oldest resident, now 94, who keeps a copy in his kitchen). Bridget is flattered and unsettled in equal measure. The hospitality is too much — not aggressive, not suspicious, but too attentive, as though she is more important than she should be. She tells herself this is simply Argentine warmth, the culture of welcome she has written about. But there is a quality to Sofía's attention — a tenderness, almost maternal — that she has not seen before. Sofía says: "I am so glad you came. You have no idea how glad." Bridget does not ask why.

### Escalating Middle (~1,200 words): The Committee and the Daylight
Over two days, Bridget observes the festival preparations. Everything happens in brilliant August sunlight — the sky absurdly blue, the dry hills golden, the shadows short and hard. She attends a committee meeting (she is invited; she assumes this is because she's an academic guest). The committee discusses parking, the generator, whether the bread will be enough. There are seven women and two men. They bicker about the schedule with the comfortable friction of people who have done this many times. Then: a pause. A shift in register so subtle Bridget almost misses it. One of the women — Doña Carmen, perhaps sixty, with a face like dried leather and an apron that has not been removed all day — says something about "the portion." The others nod. Someone makes a note. The discussion moves to whether the priest's car has air conditioning.

Bridget asks Sofía about "the portion" later. Sofía says it is the food allocation — how much meat, how much bread, who eats first. "The older families eat first. Then the guests. It is a question of respect." This is plausible. This is also not the whole answer, and Bridget knows it, and she does not press, because pressing would be — what? Rude. Colonial. The anthropologist interrogating the native. She has spent her career arguing against that posture. So she accepts the answer and writes it in her field notebook and underlines "portion" and does not look up.

A second scene: Bridget watches the corpachada preparation. Women dig a hole in the packed earth behind the church. They line it with fabric — not alpaca wool (she notes the anachronism, the syncretism, the modern substitution) — and place offerings: a pot of locro, ears of corn, a cigarette, a small bottle of cane spirit. Bridget photographs everything. She is doing her job. She is also, she realizes with a start of something that is not quite fear, being watched — not with hostility but with the patient attention of someone watching a child do something predictable. Doña Carmen stands at the edge of the group, arms folded, watching Bridget photograph the hole. When Bridget lowers her camera, Carmen says, in clear English: "The mouth is very hungry this year." She smiles. It is a kind smile.

### Crisis Point (~500 words): Recognition at the Table
The evening of the second day. The long table is set in the plaza, under strings of lights, in the remaining daylight that refuses to fade. Fifty or sixty people. Bridget is seated between Sofía and Don Aurelio. The food begins: empanadas, then locro, then grilled cabrito, then bowls of a dark, dense bread she does not recognise. "Patay," Sofía tells her. "From the algarroba. The tree was here before any of us. Before the Diaguita. Before anyone. Taste it." Bridget tastes it. It is sweet and heavy and unlike anything she has eaten. She eats another piece. The basket refills.

The meal does not end. More dishes arrive. Bridget is full but continues eating because everyone is eating and to stop would be to separate herself from the table. She looks down the row of faces. They are all eating. They are all happy. And she understands, suddenly, with the clarity of someone reading a sentence in a language they forgot they knew — she understands the grammar. Subject: the earth. Verb: eats. Object: what is given. And the selection — the "portion" — is not the food allocation. The portion is who is placed closest to the mouth. The portion is who the earth eats first.

She looks at Sofía. Sofía is smiling. The smile is the same one she has worn all weekend — genuine, warm, full of love. It is the smile of someone who has given a gift and is watching it be opened.

Bridget picks up another piece of patay. She eats it. It is delicious.

### Ending (~400 words): The Bread Basket
The meal continues. The sky darkens but the string lights keep everything visible — no shadows, no hiding. Don Aurelio tells her about the festival when he was a boy, how his grandmother baked the patay in a clay oven behind the church, how the algarroba trees used to be so thick you could walk in their shade for a kilometre. He does not mention the mouth. He does not mention the portion. He tells her about the bread.

Bridget's field notebook sits closed on the table beside her plate. She has not written anything for hours. She reaches for another piece of patay. The basket is full. She does not remember it being refilled but it is full. She eats. Sofía puts a hand on her arm — not restraining, not urging. Just resting there. The weight of it is the weight of a friend's hand on your arm. The weight of it is the weight of a lock clicking shut.

Down the table, someone begins to sing. Others join. The song is in a language Bridget does not recognise — not Spanish, not Quechua, something older or something that has never been any language at all, just the sound a community makes when it is feeding. The song does not end. The food does not end. The bread basket is full.

## 4. Key Scenes

1. **The agenda scene** (opening): Bridget reads the photocopied schedule and underlines a word she cannot translate. Establishes her as a reader — someone who believes the world is a text. Also introduces the committee's mundane bureaucratic texture (Jackson). Does double duty: sets up the mystery word AND characterizes Bridget's faith in legibility.

2. **The committee meeting**: Seven women and two men discuss parking and generators, then shift register for thirty seconds to discuss "the portion." Bridget notices the shift but categorises it as a food allocation question. This is the Jackson core — communal violence administered through committee procedure. The horror is that the committee minutes would read exactly the same whether they were planning a potluck or a sacrifice.

3. **The corpachada / Doña Carmen's English**: Bridget photographs the ritual hole. Carmen watches her with patient amusement and says "The mouth is very hungry this year" in English — a language she clearly doesn't often use, deployed specifically for Bridget's benefit. This is a warning, or an invitation, or both. Bridget does not know which. This scene carries the Enriquez weight: the ritual is rooted in specific earth, specific drought, specific hunger. The mouth is not a metaphor.

4. **The meal / the patay**: The long table scene. The bread she cannot stop eating. Sofía pressing food on her. The moment of recognition — she grasps the grammar — and the moment of complicity that follows immediately, without pause, without resistance. She picks up another piece. This is the story's gravitational centre. Midsommar's complicity-through-belonging meets Jackson's everyone-participates. And Aickman's Hospice: the meal that will not end, the food that keeps coming, the guest who cannot leave because leaving would be rude.

5. **The ending / the song**: The song in no language. The notebook closed. Sofía's hand on her arm. The bread basket full. Everything visible, nothing hidden, nothing understood. This is Aickman's ending: the story is a condition, not a puzzle. Understanding makes no difference.

## 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Opening**: Intellectual curiosity, mild professional vanity (they've read her monograph!), the comfortable pleasure of being in a place she has studied but never visited. The reader should feel what Bridget feels: this is interesting, these people are kind, this is going well.

**Middle**: A slow leak of unease. Not dread — something quieter. The feeling of being in a conversation where everyone knows something you don't and no one is being unkind about it. The reader begins to see what Bridget refuses to see, but the reader also understands WHY she refuses — because naming it would make her the colonial observer she's spent her career opposing. The reader is trapped by the same politeness.

**Crisis**: Recognition without relief. The grammar clicks into place. But the recognition does not liberate — it implicates. She understands AND she keeps eating. The reader should feel the specific horror of complicity that arrives not as a choice but as a continuation of what you were already doing.

**Ending**: The reader should feel held at the table. Not terrified — settled. The song, the hand on the arm, the full bread basket. The horror is that it is genuinely warm. The horror is that the horror is hospitality. The reader puts the story down and feels hungry, and the hunger is not entirely their own.

## 6. Formula Integration

**Robert Aickman (Author A)** — Most visible in: the entire narrative voice. The prose should be measured, precise, understated. Bridget's internal register is social discomfort, not terror. She responds to the uncanny with embarrassment and professional self-consciousness. Key passage: the moment after the committee meeting when she analyses her own unease and concludes it is probably ethnocentric bias. Aickman's technique of the protagonist who cannot accept the story is about them. Also: the Hospice parallel in the final meal — food that will not stop, hospitality as entrapment, the guest who stays because leaving would be a breach of etiquette.

**Mariana Enriquez (Author B)** — Most visible in: the village itself, its material reality. The drought, the dust, the distance from Buenos Aires. The corpachada scene: specific ritual practices rooted in specific soil. Doña Carmen's flat declaration about the hungry mouth. Enriquez's insistence that the supernatural is not symbolic but functional — the earth eats, the earth is fed, this is infrastructure. The food specificity (patay, locro, cabrito) is Enriquez: horror grounded in cuisine, in the body, in what enters the mouth. Also: the backstory of Lucía Robles, the student who went native — Enriquez's theme of the educated outsider who discovers their education is irrelevant.

**Midsommar (Work X)** — Most visible in: the daylight. The August sun that refuses to let Bridget hide. The community's genuine warmth — they are not predators wearing kindness as a mask; they are kind AND they are performing a ritual that requires a guest. Sofía's love for Bridget is real, and the invitation is both a gift and a sentence. The selection embedded in welcome. The protagonist's grief (here reframed as intellectual loneliness, the wound of the Lucía Robles failure) makes her vulnerable to belonging. She wants to be welcomed. The welcome is real. The welcome is also the mechanism.

**"The Lottery" (Work Y)** — Most visible in: the committee meeting. The bureaucratic texture of the selection process. The folding-chair argument that transitions seamlessly into "the portion." The agenda printed on photocopied paper. Mr. Summers running the lottery with civic efficiency becomes seven women and two men discussing logistics with the comfortable friction of annual routine. The participation-as-default: everyone eats, everyone is at the table, everyone is part of it because they are there. Also: the daylight quality — Jackson's "the light you do errands in," not Aster's operatic sun.

## 7. Title

**Bread and Portion**

"Bread" grounds it in the material — the patay, the algarroba tree, the food that keeps coming. "Portion" carries the double meaning: the food allocation AND the selection, the thing the committee decides in the same breath as the folding chairs. The title is plain, domestic, the kind of phrase that could appear on a committee agenda. It does not announce its horror. It sounds like something you'd bring to a potluck.

Alternative considered: "Enough Chairs" — too cute. "Patay" — too obscure, would read as a typo. "Guest and Portion" — too on-the-nose.
