## Work 1: fan-lowfantasy-001

- **Genre**: Fantasy
- **Subgenre**: Low Fantasy (NEW)
- **AuthorA**: Brandon Sanderson — style: Crystal-clear, invisible prose; elaborate rule-based magic systems; structural precision; the mechanics of constraint as story engine
- **AuthorB**: Neil Gaiman — style: Matter-of-fact prose amid the fantastical; myth made accessible and domestic; dark whimsy; the ordinary rendered strange
- **WorkX**: *The Master and Margarita* by Mikhail Bulgakov — structure: Dual narrative threading the mundane and the supernatural; the devil arrives in a realist setting and exposes its hypocrisies; satire and magic cohabiting the same sentence
- **WorkY**: *Piranesi* by Susanna Clarke — themes: A world that operates on hidden rules the protagonist doesn't fully understand; memory as unreliable architecture; the wonder and terror of a place that shouldn't exist but does
- **Target Word Count**: 5,800 words (reading time: 23 min)
- **Risk Card**: None

**Combination logic**: Sanderson's systematic approach to magic — rules, costs, limitations — collides with Gaiman's instinct to leave magic unexplained, creating a low-fantasy world where something genuinely uncanny operates within ordinary life but follows laws the protagonist can almost, but never quite, articulate. Bulgakov's structure provides the dual-register template (realism and impossibility in the same breath), while Clarke's Piranesi supplies the themes of a bewildered inhabitant navigating a world whose rules are real but hidden.

## Work 2: mys-whodunit-001

- **Genre**: Mystery / Thriller
- **Subgenre**: Whodunit / Classic Detective (NEW)
- **AuthorA**: Agatha Christie — style: Deceptively simple prose; intricate plot construction; the puzzle as everything; misdirection elevated to art form; the clue hidden in plain sight
- **AuthorB**: Dennis Lehane — style: Working-class voice; emotional devastation beneath crime narrative; noir with genuine heart; Boston rain-soaked atmosphere
- **WorkX**: *In the Woods* by Tana French — structure: A cold case investigation that unearths the detective's own buried trauma; the mystery that solves the detective rather than the other way around; procedural form subverted by psychology
- **WorkY**: *The Secret History* by Donna Tartt — themes: Guilt as a corrosive force among people who believe themselves above ordinary morality; the weight of a shared secret; intellectual hubris unraveling into paranoia; justice arriving not through institutions but through the crime eating its perpetrators alive
- **Target Word Count**: 6,400 words (reading time: 26 min)
- **Risk Card**: #10 THE PROTAGONIST IS WRONG — The main character's worldview, goal, or moral framework is fundamentally flawed, and the story reveals this not through a tidy epiphany but through consequences the character may or may not fully understand. The reader sees what the character cannot.

**Combination logic**: Christie's puzzle architecture meets Lehane's emotional roughness — a whodunit where the deductive machinery is impeccable but the detective's conviction about justice is the real blind spot. The detective solves the case correctly, mechanically, but the solution reveals something about their own moral framework that the reader grasps before they do. French's structure (the investigation that turns inward) gives the blueprint, while Tartt's *Secret History* provides the thematic engine — guilt festering among people who consider themselves above common morality, justice arriving not through the system but through the crime's own corrosive weight.

## Work 3: hor-gothichorror-001

- **Genre**: Horror
- **Subgenre**: Gothic Horror (NEW)
- **AuthorA**: Edgar Allan Poe — style: Fevered, rhythmic prose; the unreliable narrator as foundational form; obsession, guilt, and premature burial; confined spaces as psychological landscapes
- **AuthorB**: Mariana Enriquez — style: Latin American social realism bleeding into the supernatural; poverty and violence as the real horror; ghosts that emerge from political history; unflinching, matter-of-fact dread
- **WorkX**: *The Turn of the Screw* by Henry James — structure: A frame narrative that destabilizes everything inside it; the governess's account that may or may not be trustworthy; horror that depends entirely on whether you believe the narrator
- **WorkY**: *Mexican Gothic* by Silvia Moreno-Garcia — themes: A house that feeds on its inhabitants; colonial violence encoded in architecture; the family as organism consuming its members; beauty and rot occupying the same space
- **Target Word Count**: 3,600 words (reading time: 14 min)
- **Risk Card**: #12 DUAL TIMELINE — Two timelines run in parallel (present and past, or two different presents). The timelines must speak to each other through echo, contrast, or causation. The connection between them is the story's real subject.

**Combination logic**: Poe's claustrophobic interiority meets Enriquez's socially grounded supernatural — a Gothic horror where the crumbling estate is not merely atmospheric but a product of specific historical violence. The dual timeline card demands that the past (when the house was built, what it was built on, whose labor and suffering shaped it) runs alongside the present (a character trapped inside, experiencing the architectural consequences). James's frame-narrative structure provides the instability — whose version of events do we trust? — while Moreno-Garcia's themes root the Gothic in colonial exploitation rather than generic haunting.

## Work 4: hum-parody-001

- **Genre**: Humor / Satire
- **Subgenre**: Parody / Pastiche (NEW)
- **AuthorA**: P.G. Wodehouse — style: Intricate comic plotting; Edwardian farce; baroque similes that perform triple axels; the comedy of incompetent aristocracy; Jeeves-and-Wooster machinery
- **AuthorB**: Jonathan Swift — style: Savage indignation masked by deadpan reasonableness; "A Modest Proposal" school of escalation; irony as moral weapon; the rational voice proposing monstrous things
- **WorkX**: *Pale Fire* by Vladimir Nabokov — structure: A text-and-commentary form where the commentary hijacks and distorts the original; the annotator as unreliable narrator; scholarly apparatus as comic and tragic vehicle
- **WorkY**: *Don Quixote* by Miguel de Cervantes — themes: A character so devoted to a literary genre that they mistake reality for its conventions; the comedy and pathos of living inside a story; fiction's power to deform perception
- **Target Word Count**: 4,200 words (reading time: 17 min)
- **Risk Card**: None

**Combination logic**: Wodehouse's immaculate comic machinery meets Swift's moral savagery — a parody that is simultaneously hilarious and cutting. Nabokov's *Pale Fire* structure (a text with increasingly unhinged annotations) gives the formal vehicle: the piece presents itself as a scholarly edition of something, where the commentary reveals far more about the commentator than the text. Cervantes supplies the thematic engine — a figure so immersed in genre conventions that they cannot see the world clearly. The target genre being parodied should emerge from the combination: a literary-critical apparatus applied with Wodehousian glee and Swiftian bite.

## Work 5: hf-epic-001

- **Genre**: Historical Fiction
- **Subgenre**: Historical Epic / Saga (NEW)
- **AuthorA**: Colson Whitehead — style: Speculative-inflected historical fiction; Black American history rendered through alternating registers from lyrical to brutal; sentences that shift from documentary to mythic mid-paragraph
- **AuthorB**: Ken Follett — style: Sweeping multi-plot architecture; accessible, cinematic prose; the engineering of suspense across decades; medieval and 20th-century settings rendered through material detail
- **WorkX**: *The Sympathizer* by Viet Thanh Nguyen — structure: A confession/memoir form; first-person account that doubles as political argument; the narrator as double agent, literally and figuratively; a single voice carrying multiple loyalties
- **WorkY**: *Beloved* by Toni Morrison — themes: The past that will not stay buried; the cost of survival; how the body remembers what the mind tries to forget; the haunting of a people by their own history
- **Target Word Count**: 7,800 words (reading time: 31 min)
- **Risk Card**: None

**Combination logic**: Whitehead's ability to make historical fiction feel speculative (the Underground Railroad as literal railroad) meets Follett's architectural instinct for multi-decade sweep — an epic compressed into short-story form, covering generations through strategic compression. Nguyen's confession structure gives the narrative its spine: a single narrator recounting a family's passage through history, their account shaped by what they need to justify. Morrison's *Beloved* provides the thematic foundation — history as a haunting, the past as a presence that demands acknowledgment. The saga form here is not leisurely but compressed, each generation rendered in a few devastating pages.
