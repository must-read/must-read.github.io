## Writing Samples

### Source 1: Author A — Donna Tartt

**From *The Secret History*, Prologue**
*Representative of: lush confessional narration, guilt as permanent condition, the voice that has already decided it cannot be forgiven.*

> The snow in the mountains was melting and Bunny had been dead for several weeks before we came to understand the gravity of our situation. He'd been dead for ten days before they found him, you know. It was one of the biggest manhunts in Vermont history—state troopers, the FBI, even an army helicopter; the college closed, the dye factory in Hampden shut down, people coming from New Hampshire, upstate New York, as far away as Boston. It is difficult to believe that Henry's modest plan could have worked so well despite these unforeseen events. We hadn't intended to hide the body where it couldn't be found. In fact, we hadn't hidden it at all but had simply left it where it fell in hopes that some luckless passer-by would stumble over it before anyone even noticed he was missing. This was a tale that told itself simply and well: the loose rocks, the body at the bottom of the ravine with a clean break in the neck, and the muddy skidmarks of dug-in heels pointing the way down; a hiking accident, no more, no less, and it might have been left at that, at quiet tears and a small funeral, had it not been for the snow that fell that night; it covered him without a trace, and ten days later, when the thaw finally came, the state troopers and the FBI and the searchers from the town all saw that they had been walking back and forth over his body until the snow above it was packed down like ice.

**Key Tartt techniques to channel:**
- Long, hypnotic sentences that lull the reader past confessions
- Narrator who is complicit and knows it — past-tense confession as permanent condition
- Intellectual environments rendered as dangerously seductive
- Specificity of guilt: "the muddy skidmarks of dug-in heels"
- "Beauty is terror. Whatever we call beautiful, we quiver before it."
- "Does such a thing as 'the fatal flaw,' that showy dark crack running down the middle of a life, exist outside literature?"
- "I suppose at one time in my life I might have had any number of stories, but now there is no other. This is the only story I will ever be able to tell."

### Source 2: Author B — Agatha Christie

**From *Witness for the Prosecution*, courtroom scene**
*Representative of: testimony as performance, misdirection through social role, the twist that reframes everything.*

> She had seemed a pale, quiet woman in the house at Paddington, but in court she had flamed out against the sober background, flaunting herself like a tropical flower. If he closed his eyes he could see her now, tall and vehement, her exquisite body bent forward a little, her right hand clenching and unclenching itself unconsciously.

**Key Christie techniques to channel:**
- Prose that is direct and functional, moving briskly through revelation
- Misdirection through plain-sight clues: small details (an overheard conversation, a misplaced object, a seemingly offhand remark) that reframe when reconsidered
- Foreboding deployed early — characters sense something dreadful before they have rational cause
- Courtroom as theater: "the witness stand is the most theatrical piece of furniture in Western civilization"
- The critical reversal in *Witness*: Romaine testifies AGAINST her husband to save him — "The evidence of a woman devoted to him would not have been enough." The lie serves truth; the betrayal IS the loyalty.
- Social performance as deception: characters who deceive by playing exactly what they appear to be

### Source 3: Work X — *The Secret History*

**Structure and themes to draw from:**
- Small group bound by shared transgression, turning on each other under institutional pressure
- Classical education as moral corruption — knowing Greek tragedy while living one
- Narrator who participated in the crime and must now account for it
- The confession that comes too late and changes nothing
- Every version of events implicates the teller
- The group's secret creates a sealed world — beautiful, intoxicating, suffocating

### Source 4: Work Y — *Witness for the Prosecution*

**Structure and themes to draw from:**
- Courtroom as locked room — a space where truth must be extracted from performance
- Testimony that is simultaneously true and misleading
- The twist that inverts victim and perpetrator (or witness and accused)
- Loyalty tested under oath: the person who lies for you may be lying about everything
- The devastating final reversal: deception as the only available form of devotion
- The audience (jury, reader) watching a performance without knowing it's a performance

### Selected Concept 1: Bern Banking Crisis of 1720

In 1720, two Bernese banking houses (Malacrida & Cie. and Samuel Müller & Cie.) were entrusted to invest public and private funds in the London and Paris stock exchanges. They speculated wildly during the Mississippi and South Sea bubbles, initially generating enormous profits. When the bubbles burst, 500 creditors across all social classes lost everything — 440,000 thalers. The trauma paralyzed Bern's financial sector for nearly thirty years; no new bank was founded in that time. The city missed an entire generation of economic development because of the breach of trust.

**Integration:** Professor Linnea Gould's scholarly work focuses on trust and institutional betrayal in ancient contexts. In her testimony, she references — or the narrative deploys — the specific mechanism of the Bern crisis as an analogy she taught in seminar: a trusted intermediary who gambles with what has been placed in their care, the way 500 people can be ruined by two men in whom they placed reasonable faith. This is the lecture the dead student, Petra, once quoted back to her in office hours. The analogy maps onto the professor's own position: the students trusted her intellectual authority, and she gambled with their devotion to classical ideals by letting the ritual proceed (or by not stopping it, or by not knowing she should have stopped it). The detail of the thirty-year paralysis — a city unable to trust again — echoes the trial's aftermath.

### Selected Concept 2: Lopushna Monastery (Bulgaria)

A monastery in northwestern Bulgaria, originally established during the Second Bulgarian Empire. During the Chiprovtsi Uprising of 1688 against Ottoman rule, hundreds of women and children sought refuge in the monastery. Ottoman forces burned it to the ground, killing everyone inside. The monastery was later rebuilt and became a center of Bulgarian cultural revival — national writer Ivan Vazov wrote part of *Under the Yoke* there.

**Integration:** The ritual the students performed took place at a decommissioned chapel in the woods near campus — a stone building that had been a Congregationalist meeting house in the 1780s, later used as a cider barn, then abandoned. The professor taught a comparative unit on sacred spaces that become sites of destruction: Lopushna, the burning of the Library of Alexandria, the sack of Delphi. The Lopushna detail — people running toward a sacred space that becomes their death — is the unconscious template for what happened that night. The students ran toward the ritual, toward the transcendence they craved, and one of them died there. The professor does not make this connection explicitly on the stand. The reader can.

## 1. Premise

A classics professor is compelled to testify in the murder trial of her most brilliant student, who is accused of killing another student during a nighttime ritual the professor's seminar inspired but she never authorized. The trial forces her to publicly narrate a night she has been narrating privately — and differently — for two years. The engine is not *what happened* but *what her testimony reveals about the gap between what she saw, what she believes, and what she has allowed herself to remember*, with each cross-examination answer shifting the reader's understanding of whether the accused is a murderer, the dead student a victim, or the professor the one most responsible for all of it.

## 2. Protagonist

**Name:** Linnea Gould, 51
**Situation:** Associate professor of classical languages and literature at Aldwyn College, a small liberal arts institution in western Massachusetts. She has held the same position for twenty-three years. Unmarried. Lives in a rented house walking distance from campus with two shelves of first-edition Greek texts and a persistent leak in the kitchen ceiling she has never fixed. She has published one well-regarded monograph (*Trust and Ruin: Sacred Obligation in Pre-Socratic Institutions*) and has not published in nine years.

**What she WANTS:** To deliver testimony that protects the narrative she constructed on the night Petra Alderman died — the version where the ritual was the students' own idea, where Linnea's seminar was academic and bounded, where the brilliant student (Jonah Alcott) was the instigator and she was merely the teacher who arrived too late.

**What she NEEDS:** To acknowledge that she wanted the ritual to happen — not the death, but the transgression. That she cultivated the intellectual conditions for it, that she was intoxicated by Jonah's willingness to take ideas seriously enough to act on them, and that Petra's death is the cost of a desire she still cannot name honestly.

**Her specific flaw:** She mistakes intellectual precision for emotional honesty. She can articulate the moral philosophy of guilt and obligation in six languages but cannot say "I wanted this" about her own life. Every feeling she has is immediately translated into analysis — and the analysis, being elegant and rigorous, substitutes for the feeling so completely that she genuinely does not know the difference. On the witness stand, this makes her testimony sound evasive when it is actually sincere: she is giving the jury her most honest account, and her most honest account is a performance of honesty, because that is all she has ever had.

**Her specific wound:** Nine years ago, she submitted a second monograph for review and it was rejected — not harshly, but with the observation that her work was "meticulous but airless, like a cathedral with no congregation." She has not written since. Jonah Alcott's seminar work was the first intellectual engagement in years that made her feel the presence of air.

## 3. Structure

The story is structured as a single day of testimony, broken into sections that alternate between **courtroom narration** (what Linnea says aloud, rendered in close third person with legal dialogue) and **interior monologue** (what Linnea is actually thinking, remembering, and constructing as she speaks). Neither track is reliable. The spoken testimony omits and reshapes; the interior monologue romanticizes and deflects. The truth lives in the gap between them.

### Opening image/scene:
Linnea sitting in the witness waiting room at 7:40 a.m., reading a water-stained copy of Euripides' *Hecuba* — the play about a queen forced to testify to the murder of her children. She is not reading it for comfort. She is reading it because it is the only text she trusts to describe what the next eight hours will require of her. She notices that someone has underlined a passage in pencil — "The gods hear the just and the unjust alike; it is the listening that differs" — and she closes the book because the handwriting might be Petra's and she cannot afford to think about Petra yet.

### Inciting disruption:
The prosecution's first question is not about the night of the ritual. It is about a lecture Linnea gave three weeks before the death — a lecture on the Bacchic mysteries in which she told her seminar that the ancient Greeks "understood that certain truths can only be reached through the body, through action that the rational mind would forbid." The prosecutor reads this from a student's notes. Linnea recognizes the handwriting as Petra's. The disruption is not the question but the realization that Petra's notes — the dead student's faithful transcription — are being used to build the case, and that Petra, even dead, is the most reliable witness in the room.

### Escalating middle:
Cross-examination by the defense forces Linnea to describe the night itself in detail. She narrates arriving at the old chapel in the woods after midnight, hearing chanting, finding four students in a circle with a fire. The defense attorney asks precise questions about what she saw Jonah do, what Petra was doing, where the others were standing. Each answer adjusts the picture: first it sounds like a ritual that went wrong (an accident), then like a confrontation between Jonah and Petra (murder), then like something Petra herself initiated (complicity). The escalation is in the reader's understanding — each new detail from Linnea doesn't clarify, it multiplies the possible interpretations.

Meanwhile, the interior monologue reveals that Linnea is selecting her details carefully — not lying, but choosing which truths to offer first. She remembers the Bern banking crisis lecture, how she told her students about the trusted intermediaries who speculated with 500 families' savings, and how Petra had said afterward, "But you're describing yourself, Professor Gould. You speculate with us." Linnea had laughed. She is not laughing now.

### Crisis point:
The prosecutor asks Linnea directly: "Did you want the ritual to take place?" The interior monologue floods: the years of airless scholarship, Jonah's electric intelligence, the nights of late-seminar discussion that felt like the only real thing in her life. The spoken answer is: "I did not instruct my students to perform a ritual. I taught classical texts. What they did with that knowledge was their own choice." It is technically true. It is a lie so close to the truth that Linnea herself cannot locate the seam.

Immediately after, the defense introduces a handwritten note — from Linnea's desk, seized during the investigation — that reads: "The chapel. Walpurgis. Let them try." Three words that could mean "let them attempt the academic exercise" or "let them risk the real thing." Linnea is asked which she meant. Her interior monologue is silent for the first time in the story.

### Ending:
Linnea finishes her testimony. She is excused. In the corridor, she passes Jonah Alcott being led back to the defense table. They do not speak. She walks to her car in the courthouse parking lot. It is raining. She sits in the car without starting the engine and opens the copy of *Hecuba* to the underlined passage. The handwriting is not Petra's. It is her own, from years ago, before any of this. She does not remember writing it.

The verdict is not given. We do not learn whether Jonah is convicted. What we learn is that Linnea will drive back to Aldwyn College, and that the leak in her kitchen ceiling will still be there, and that she will teach her seminar again next semester because no one has told her not to, and that the chair she sat in on the witness stand will be occupied by someone else tomorrow, and that the gap between what she said and what she knows will be the only story she has left.

## 4. Key Scenes

**Scene 1: The Waiting Room (Opening)**
Linnea with *Hecuba*, the underlined passage, the texture of the morning. Establishes the dual-track structure: we see her preparing her testimony (external) while her mind circles the question of whether testimony is an act of truth or an act of composition (internal). Also establishes the physical world — the institutional waiting room, the bad coffee, the bailiff who calls her "Professor" with a deference that makes her feel like a fraud. Does two things: hooks the reader with a woman who is clearly hiding something even from herself, and establishes the intellectual register that will be challenged by the courtroom's demand for plain speech.

**Scene 2: Petra's Notes (Early Testimony)**
The prosecutor reads Linnea's own words back to her from Petra's notes. This is where the dead student becomes a presence in the courtroom — not as a ghost, but as a handwriting. The notes are meticulous, faithful, slightly formal. Linnea recognizes phrases she used in lecture and hears them differently in the prosecutor's voice — stripped of irony, made literal, made dangerous. Her interior monologue grieves not for Petra but for the version of herself that Petra's notes captured: the professor as authority, as someone worth transcribing. Does two things: establishes the Tartt-esque theme of intellectual seduction as corruption, and introduces the Christie mechanism of evidence that reframes meaning (the lecture notes as both faithful record and prosecutorial weapon).

**Scene 3: The Night in the Chapel (Central Set-Piece)**
Cross-examination forces Linnea to walk the jury through the night. Arriving at the old Congregationalist chapel. The fire inside. The four students (Jonah, Petra, and two others — Suki Traille and Devlin Amari). The chanting, which she recognized as a distorted version of the Orphic hymns she had taught. Petra on the ground. The blood. This scene is told in fragments, with the defense attorney's questions serving as structural breaks. The reader assembles the night from Linnea's partial answers — and realizes, by the end of the scene, that the fragments don't quite fit together. There is a moment Linnea skips: the minutes between when she arrived and when she called 911. What she did in those minutes is the central absence of the story. Does two things: delivers the mystery-thriller obligation of the crime's reconstruction, and creates the Christie-style puzzle — all pieces present, arrangement misleading.

**Scene 4: "Let Them Try" (Crisis)**
The handwritten note. Three words in Linnea's handwriting on a Post-it seized from her office desk. The prosecutor asks what she meant. The defense attorney objects. The judge overrules. Linnea's interior monologue, for the first time, offers no interpretation, no analysis, no elegant reframing. Just the memory of writing the note at her desk at 10 p.m. on a Tuesday, after Jonah had left her office hours, after he had described what the seminar group wanted to do, after she had said — what? She cannot remember what she said aloud. She remembers writing the note. She remembers the handwriting was steady. Does two things: delivers the maximum-pressure moment the legal thriller requires, and performs the Tartt insight that guilt is not about what you did but about the moment you knew what you were allowing.

**Scene 5: The Parking Lot (Closing)**
Linnea alone. The rain. The book. The discovered handwriting. No verdict, no resolution. The story ends on the recognition that the gap between testimony and truth is not a failure of the legal system but a permanent condition of consciousness — that Linnea will carry both versions of the night (the one she told the court, the one she tells herself) and that neither will settle into truth, and that this is, in some terrible sense, her life's work: the scholarly study of people who are ruined by the gap between what they said and what they knew. Does two things: refuses the tidy legal-thriller ending (no verdict, no slam of the gavel), and enacts the Tartt principle that confession changes nothing — the story you tell about yourself is the only story you will ever tell, and it is never the right one.

## 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Opening:** The reader feels taut, careful attention — a woman is preparing to do something difficult, and the precision of her preparation (reading *Hecuba*, choosing her clothes, rehearsing her composure) suggests this is someone who manages her life through control. The reader is curious about what she's controlling.

**Early testimony:** Growing unease. The reader begins to sense that Linnea is not simply a witness — she is implicated. The shift from sympathy ("a professor forced to testify") to suspicion ("a professor selecting her testimony") happens gradually, through accumulating details that don't quite align. The reader feels the ground shifting.

**Central set-piece:** Vertigo. The reconstruction of the night in the chapel is told in fragments that refuse to cohere. The reader is doing the jury's work — trying to piece together what happened — and discovering that each new fragment makes the picture less clear, not more. The emotional experience is the frustration and fascination of being inside an unreliable narration that is trying, with genuine effort, to be reliable.

**Crisis:** A cold drop in the stomach. "Let them try." Three words that might be innocent and might be damning, and the silence of Linnea's interior voice — the voice that has been analyzing and reframing everything — is more frightening than any confession. The reader feels the abyss of a person who cannot access their own truth.

**Closing:** Not devastation, not catharsis. A bleak, steady recognition — like looking at a landscape you've been driving toward for hours and realizing it's exactly as flat as it looked from a distance. The reader puts the story down feeling that something has been honestly rendered: the impossibility of ever fully accounting for what you let happen. Not "quietly devastated" but rather the feeling of having watched someone perform an autopsy on their own conscience and find the organ missing.

## 6. Formula Integration

### Donna Tartt (Author A) — Most visible in: Interior monologue throughout, especially the central chapel reconstruction
Tartt's DNA lives in the lush, hypnotic interior voice — the long sentences that carry the reader past confessions, the intellectual environment rendered as dangerously seductive. The professor's narration of her seminar, her relationship with Jonah, her love for Petra should feel like Tartt's Richard Papen narrating his obsession with the Greek class: the beauty of ideas as a drug, the confession that comes too late. Specific passage: Linnea's interior reflection on why she didn't stop the seminar from becoming something more than academic — the Tartt move of a character who committed a terrible act for a beautiful reason and who can articulate the beauty with devastating precision while remaining blind to the terror.

### Agatha Christie (Author B) — Most visible in: The courtroom cross-examination structure and the "Let Them Try" revelation
Christie's DNA lives in the plot mechanics: the testimony that functions as misdirection, the evidence that reframes when reconsidered, the public-setting revelation. The courtroom cross-examination should operate like a Christie investigation — each question calibrated to shift the picture, with the "twist" being not a single moment but a cumulative reframing. The "Let Them Try" note functions like the key piece of evidence in *Witness for the Prosecution*: a document that could mean two things, whose ambiguity IS the point. The specific Christie move: the audience (jury and reader) watching a performance without knowing it's a performance — and then, with the note, being forced to reconsider every performance they've watched.

### *The Secret History* (Work X) — Most visible in: The seminar group dynamics and the professor-student relationship
The small group bound by shared transgression, the classical education as moral corruption, the narrator who participated and must testify. The five-student seminar group — Jonah, Petra, Suki, Devlin, and the absent fifth (who left before the ritual and is not part of the trial) — maps onto Tartt's Greek class. The professor's position is a variation: she is not Richard (the participating outsider) but Julian (the teacher who cultivated the conditions). The structural influence: the story opens after the death, as *The Secret History* does, and the investigation *is* the narration, with every version of events implicating the teller.

### *Witness for the Prosecution* (Work Y) — Most visible in: The dual-testimony structure and the question of whose loyalty is real
The courtroom as theater, the witness whose testimony is performance, the inversion of victim and perpetrator. Linnea's testimony — technically truthful, emotionally misleading — mirrors Romaine Vole's devastating gambit: the lie that serves truth, the betrayal that IS loyalty. The specific thematic influence: the story asks whether Linnea is lying *for* Jonah (protecting her student) or *about* Jonah (protecting herself), and the answer turns out to be both, simultaneously, in a way that neither the jury nor the reader can cleanly separate. The ending refuses the *Witness for the Prosecution* resolution — there is no final reveal that clarifies the deception. The deception is the character.

## 7. Title

**Compelled Witness**

"Compelled" operates on three levels: (1) legal — a compelled witness is one subpoenaed to testify, who cannot refuse; (2) psychological — Linnea is compelled by forces she doesn't fully understand to narrate a version of events that protects a story she needs to survive; (3) intellectual — she has spent her life studying people who were compelled to bear witness (Hecuba, the chorus of Greek tragedy). The title does not start with "The." It functions as both a legal term and a description of a person who cannot stop performing the act of testimony, even (especially) when the testimony is addressed to herself.

## Technical Notes for the Writer

### Dual-track structure:
- **Courtroom sections**: Close third person, present tense. Dialogue between attorneys and Linnea. Clean, direct prose (Christie-influenced). The courtroom is rendered specifically — the wood of the witness box, the angle of overhead lights, the prosecution's table with its neat folders.
- **Interior sections**: Close third person, past and present tense interwoven. Linnea's memory, analysis, and self-narration. Lush, winding prose (Tartt-influenced). These sections should be longer and more hypnotic than the courtroom sections — the contrast between the two registers IS the story.
- **The gap**: The story's central technique. At key moments, the courtroom answer and the interior thought will diverge — not as dramatic irony (the reader knows the "real" truth) but as genuine uncertainty (neither version is the whole truth, and the reader must navigate between them).

### What NOT to do:
- Do NOT reveal what happened in the minutes between Linnea's arrival at the chapel and her 911 call. The absence is the point.
- Do NOT give a verdict. The story ends before the jury deliberates.
- Do NOT have Linnea achieve a moment of self-knowledge or catharsis. She is the same person at the end as at the beginning — just more publicly so.
- Do NOT resolve whether the ritual was sacred, theatrical, or staged. Linnea doesn't know. The reader shouldn't either.
- Do NOT use the names Marcus or Chen for any character.

### Specific details to include:
- The Bern banking crisis of 1720: integrated as content from Linnea's lecture/seminar, something Petra once quoted back to her. The detail of 500 creditors ruined by two trusted intermediaries.
- The Lopushna Monastery: integrated as part of the comparative unit on sacred spaces that become sites of destruction. The image of people running toward a place of refuge that burns them.
- Euripides' *Hecuba* as the text Linnea reads in the waiting room.
- The old Congregationalist chapel (1780s, later a cider barn) as the site of the ritual.
- The specific note: "The chapel. Walpurgis. Let them try."
- The kitchen ceiling leak — a detail that anchors Linnea's domestic life as something she has allowed to deteriorate while her intellectual life consumed her.
