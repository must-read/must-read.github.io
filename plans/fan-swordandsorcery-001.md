# Story Plan: fan-swordandsorcery-001

## Assignment

- **Genre**: Fantasy / Sword and Sorcery
- **Combination ID**: fan-swordandsorcery-001
- **Target word count**: 7,600 words (acceptable range: 7,300-7,900)
- **Reading time**: 30 minutes
- **Risk card**: REVERSE CHRONOLOGY — The story moves backward in time. Each scene occurs before the previous one. The reader gains understanding of causes only after experiencing consequences. The emotional arc runs counter to the chronological arc.

### Style Sources

**Author A — Joe Abercrombie**: Gritty, visceral, darkly funny grimdark. Morally grey characters rendered with cynical precision. Close-third POV that burrows into a character's head with unflinching honesty. Short, punchy sentences for action; longer, sardonic interior passages. Dialogue that bites. Violence described with clinical specificity rather than glorification. A world where idealism is a liability.

**Author B — Madeline Miller**: Classical, elevated prose with modern emotional intelligence. Mythological reimagining that takes ancient material seriously. Long, precise sentences with a cadence borrowed from oral tradition. First-person narration of deep interiority. Transformation as central metaphor. The sentence as vessel for accumulated feeling. Nature as both backdrop and mirror. Dignity even in suffering.

### Structural/Thematic Sources

**Work X — *The Blade Itself* (Joe Abercrombie)**: Rotating close-third POV chapters. Each character's arc moving toward inevitable collision. The ensemble cast where everyone believes they're the hero. Chapter titles that recontextualize. POV switches that create dramatic irony (the reader knows things the current POV character doesn't).

**Work Y — *Circe* (Madeline Miller)**: Power earned through suffering rather than birthright. The outcast who becomes formidable. The cost of transformation. Divinity as loneliness. Pharmakeia as patient, intimate knowledge of the world's materials. The long view of an immortal consciousness watching mortals come and go.

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Joe Abercrombie — *The Blade Itself*, Opening

**Source**: Author A / Work X — Opening of Chapter 1 ("The End")
**Origin**: *The Blade Itself*, Chapter 1
**Why representative**: Establishes Abercrombie's signature style — action delivered through visceral close-third, sardonic internal voice even in extremis, violence as physical comedy.

> Logen plunged through the trees, bare feet slipping and sliding on the wet earth, the slush, the wet pine needles, breath tearing at his lungs... "Shit," said Logen. He threw himself to one side, slipped and fell on his face, rolled away thrashing through the brush...

Abercrombie's opening drops the reader mid-crisis. No preamble. The very first word of the First Law trilogy is "Logen" — a name. The second sentence is a swear word. The prose is kinetic: short clauses, commas-as-heartbeats, a protagonist defined by his capacity to survive unglamorously. Logen is a legendary warrior introduced face-down in mud. That gap between reputation and reality is Abercrombie's home territory.

Key Abercrombie markers for the writer to internalize:
- Profanity as punctuation, never as shock
- Internal monologue that's self-deprecating and sharply funny
- Violence described with precise physical mechanics (joints, balance, weight)
- Reputation as a burden the character resents but cannot escape
- Short paragraphs, often a single sentence, for impact
- "Every man has his excuses, and the more vile the man becomes, the more touching the story has to be."
- "You have to have fear to have courage."
- "Once you've got a task to do, it's better to do it than live with the fear of it."

### Sample 2: Madeline Miller — *Circe*, Chapter 1

**Source**: Author B / Work Y — Opening of Chapter 1
**Origin**: *Circe*, Chapter 1
**Why representative**: Miller's signature first-person mythological voice — classical cadence, precise imagery, interiority that carries centuries of accumulated feeling.

> When I was born, the name for what I was did not exist. They called me nymph, assuming I would be like my mother and aunts and thousand cousins. Least of the lesser goddesses, our powers were so modest they could scarcely ensure our eternities. We spoke to fish and nurtured flowers, coaxed drops from the clouds or salt from the waves. That word, nymph, paced out the length and breadth of our futures. In our language, it means not just goddess, but bride.

Key Miller markers for the writer to internalize:
- First-person narration with the weight of retrospect (the narrator knows how the story ends)
- Classical vocabulary used naturally, not ostentatiously
- Sentences that build through accretion — clause added to clause — before landing
- Nature imagery that is both literal and metaphorical
- The body as a site of transformation and knowledge
- "You cannot know how frightened gods are of pain. There is nothing more foreign to them."
- "In a solitary life, there are rare moments when another soul dips near yours, as stars once a year brush the earth."
- "Like everyone, she knew the stories of Helios' temper when he was crossed. However gold he shines, do not forget his fire."

### Sample 3: Joe Abercrombie — Glokta's Interior Voice

**Source**: Author A — The First Law Trilogy, Glokta POV sections
**Origin**: *The Blade Itself* and sequels, Glokta chapters
**Why representative**: Abercrombie's most distinctive voice — a tortured, cynical ex-swordsman whose interior monologue is devastatingly funny and self-lacerating.

Glokta's chapters feature italicized internal commentary running alongside the narrative action. He observes his own pain, his own cruelty, and the absurdity of power with a detachment that is both defense mechanism and philosophical position. His body is ruined; his mind is razor-sharp. The gap between them is where the humor lives.

Key voice elements:
- Italicized internal questions that puncture the action ("Why do I do this?")
- Physical pain described with grim comedy (his ruined teeth, his cane, his lurching walk)
- Contempt for everyone, including himself, delivered as observation rather than rant
- A character who was beautiful and is now grotesque, and knows exactly what that costs

### Sample 4: Madeline Miller — Circe's Transformation Scenes

**Source**: Author B / Work Y — Circe's discovery of pharmakeia
**Origin**: *Circe*, mid-novel
**Why representative**: Miller's rendering of power as intimate knowledge — not flashy, not violent, but patient and transformative.

Circe's magic is rooted in knowing materials: the properties of moly, the behavior of roots, the patience of distillation. She transforms men into pigs not through spectacle but through an understanding of what they already are. Miller writes sorcery as craft — closer to gardening than to warfare. The power is in the attention.

> The spiral shell. Always another curve out of sight.

Key elements for the writer:
- Magic described through sensory detail (texture, smell, taste of ingredients)
- Power as knowledge, not force
- The long patience of mastery — years compressed into paragraphs
- Transformation that reveals essential nature rather than creating something new

### 2 Selected Concepts

**1. Neopilina galatheae — Living fossil mollusc**
A creature thought extinct for 380 million years, found alive at the bottom of the ocean in 1952. A monoplacophoran — one of the oldest body plans in the animal kingdom, a single-shelled mollusc that has survived every mass extinction by going deep, going quiet, going still.

**Integration**: The protagonist's sorcery will be based on a form of biological memory-craft — she works with organisms that remember, that carry ancient knowledge in their forms. Her signature material is a type of deep-cave limpet (monoplacophoran analog) that has survived in the world since before the gods arrived. The shell is ground into powder for her most potent transformations. The creature's survival strategy — descend, endure, outlast — mirrors her own. In the story's earliest chronological moment (the final chapter), she first discovers these creatures in a tidal cave, and the reader understands where everything began.

**2. Hugh Palliser — Admiral, colonial governor**
An 18th-century naval officer who was both ruthlessly effective and politically destructive — he commanded during the Seven Years' War, governed Newfoundland, and was court-martialed for abandoning his commander at the Battle of Ushant.

**Integration**: One of the secondary POV characters is a military commander named Palliser — a tactician who prides himself on never losing a battle but who, in the story's reverse chronology, is revealed to have abandoned an ally at a crucial moment. The name carries the weight of historical martial authority while containing within it the seed of betrayal. In the first chapter (last chronologically), Palliser is dead at the protagonist's feet. By the final chapter (first chronologically), he's her most trusted friend, still unbetrayable. The reader carries the knowledge of what he'll become.

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

A sorceress stands over the body of the last person she ever trusted, in a city that once exiled her. Told in reverse chronology — seven scenes moving backward through fifteen years — the story strips away the legend of Vasht the Unmerciful to reveal the exile, the student, the girl. Every display of power the reader witnesses has already been paid for by a loss they haven't yet seen. The question isn't "what happened?" but "was there a moment when it could have gone differently?" — and the story's architecture refuses to answer.

### 2. Protagonist

**Vasht**, 34 at the story's opening (which is the chronological end), 19 at the story's close (which is the chronological beginning).

- **Situation**: A sorceress-warrior who has just killed Palliser, the commander who raised her from exile into weaponry, after discovering he sold intelligence that led to the massacre of the Selthine — a nomadic people who sheltered her during her years of exile. She stands in the great hall of Keth-Amara, a city built on volcanic glass, wearing the legend she never wanted.
- **What she WANTS (conscious goal)**: To be left alone. At every stage of her life, she wants the world to stop requiring things of her. She doesn't seek power — she seeks the cessation of need.
- **What she NEEDS (unconscious truth)**: To accept that isolation is not peace. That she chose the solitude not because she was "above" connection but because connection requires a vulnerability she cannot control, and control is the only currency she trusts.
- **Flaw/blind spot**: Vasht believes she can learn the cost of everything in advance. Her sorcery is built on knowing materials, on understanding what a thing IS before she transforms it. She applies this to people — she thinks if she studies someone deeply enough, she can predict whether they'll betray her. This makes her a superb judge of character and a terrible friend, because friendship requires tolerating the unknown.

### 3. Structure — Reverse Chronology (7 Scenes)

The story is told in seven scenes, each moving backward in time. The primary POV is Vasht (close-third, approximately 75% of the text). Two secondary POV intrusions from Palliser and from Sila (a Selthine woman who was Vasht's closest companion during exile) provide external perspective. These intrusions are brief (300-600 words each), placed as counterpoint within Vasht's chapters, showing how others see her — the myth, not the woman.

**Scene 1 — "Godhood" (Year 15 / Present Day)**
~1,200 words. Vasht POV with Palliser intrusion.

Vasht stands in the hall of Keth-Amara over Palliser's body. The city's council watches from the gallery. She has just killed him with a transformation — not a blade, not fire, but a sorcery that turned his own blood against him, thickening it in his veins until his heart couldn't pump. The prose describes this with Abercrombie's clinical precision. Palliser's brief POV intrusion (italicized, past-tense, like a memory surfacing) reveals his last thought: not fear, not regret, but a weary recognition — *she was always going to be better than me at this*. Vasht walks out. Nobody stops her. The reader doesn't know why she killed him, only that the city that exiled her is now afraid of her, and that the fear looks exactly like respect.

**Scene 2 — "Reputation" (Year 12)**
~1,100 words. Vasht POV.

Three years earlier. Vasht is summoned back to Keth-Amara by Palliser, who now commands its armies. He needs her to break a siege — an enemy force has encircled the city using wardstones that nullify conventional magic. Vasht's sorcery, based on biological transformation rather than elemental force, is unaffected. She walks through the siege lines at dawn, alone, and what she does to the besieging army involves their own bodies turning wrong — bones softening, joints reversing, a hideous quiet metamorphosis that takes eleven minutes. She counts. The city opens its gates. Miller's elevated prose here, the sentence-rhythm of ancient chronicle, contrasts with the visceral Abercrombie body-horror of what she actually does. She's invited to stay. She refuses. The reader notes she's thinner than in Scene 1, harder, more precise. Something has been subtracted.

**Scene 3 — "Symposium" (Year 9)**
~1,200 words. Vasht POV with Sila intrusion.

Six years before the killing. Vasht lives in a cave system in the coastal mountains with the Selthine nomads. She has been with them for four years. She teaches the children rudimentary herb-craft. Sila — fierce, funny, impatient with Vasht's formality — drags her to a celebration. The Selthine play music on instruments made from the shells of deep-cave monoplacophorans, the ancient limpets that Vasht uses in her sorcery. The shells resonate at frequencies that make the listener feel as though time has slowed. Vasht, for the first and only time in the narrative, laughs. Sila's brief intrusion shows her perspective: *she watches like someone counting exits. Even when she laughs. Even when she forgets to count.* This scene is the emotional heart. Everything before it (chronologically later) is what Vasht paid for this warmth. Everything after it (chronologically earlier) is who she was before she could have it.

**Scene 4 — "Attrition" (Year 6)**
~1,100 words. Vasht POV.

Nine years before the killing. Vasht has been in exile for one year. She is starving, living in a tidal cave, slowly learning to identify which organisms can be transformed and which resist her. She discovers the monoplacophorans — the ancient limpets, older than the gods, clinging to rocks at the cave's lowest depth. She grinds one shell into powder and mixes it with sea-water and blood from a cut on her own palm. The resulting paste, applied to driftwood, transforms it into something that isn't quite wood and isn't quite bone — something new. This is the first real sorcery she has done. It costs her: a memory. She can no longer remember what her mother's hands looked like. The sorcery's price is always specific, always personal, always a piece of the self that doesn't grow back. Abercrombie's gritty survival prose dominates here — hunger, cold, the practical mechanics of staying alive when nobody wants you alive.

**Scene 5 — "Exile" (Year 5)**
~1,000 words. Vasht POV with Palliser intrusion.

Ten years before the killing. Vasht is expelled from Keth-Amara. The crime: she transformed a nobleman's hunting hound into a songbird during a banquet, publicly, to settle an argument about whether transformation was possible at all. The nobleman, humiliated, demands punishment. The city council — including a younger Palliser, then a mid-ranking officer — votes for exile. Palliser's intrusion reveals he voted against exile and argued for leniency, not because he cared about Vasht but because he recognized her potential as a weapon. *A blade you exile is a blade someone else picks up.* He was right. He just didn't know he'd be the one she cut.

**Scene 6 — "Apprenticeship" (Year 3)**
~1,000 words. Vasht POV.

Twelve years before the killing. Vasht is 22, studying under Revka, the city's only licensed transmuter — a half-blind old woman who works with molds and fermented compounds. Revka teaches her the principle that all sorcery in this world obeys: *you cannot create or destroy. You can only change the form.* And every change of form requires an equal change in the sorcerer. Mass is conserved; identity is not. Vasht is brilliant but impatient. She wants the spectacular transformations. Revka tells her: "The spectacular is easy. Any fool can turn a man into a pig. Turning a pig into something that remembers being a man — that takes a lifetime." Vasht doesn't understand. She will. Revka is the one character who never betrays Vasht, never disappoints her, and whose death (of old age, between scenes, unremarked) is the loss the reader feels most acutely by its absence.

**Scene 7 — "Roots" (Year 1)**
~1,000 words. Vasht POV.

Fourteen years before the killing. Vasht is 19, newly arrived in Keth-Amara from a coastal village where she was feared for an incident the story never fully explains — something she did to the fishing nets, or to the fish in them, that made the village elders send her away. She stands at the gates of the city built on volcanic glass and hears, for the first time, the low harmonic that the glass produces when wind passes through the city's architecture. She has no money, no connections, no name. She has a pouch of dried herbs she collected by instinct, not knowing what they do. She has the stubbornness that Miller described in Circe — the adaptive ruthlessness of someone who sees the weak point in whatever is trying to kill them. She asks a stranger where she can learn about transformation. The stranger points her toward Revka's workshop. Vasht walks. The city hums.

The final line of the story is also its chronological first moment. It does NOT mirror the opening. It does NOT resolve. It is a girl walking toward a door she doesn't yet know how to open, carrying herbs she doesn't yet know how to use, in a city that will exile her and fear her and need her, and the reader knows all of this and she knows none of it.

### 4. Key Scenes (5 Essential Moments)

1. **The killing in the great hall** (Scene 1): Vasht's sorcery shown at its most devastating — clinical, quiet, biological. The reader sees the end before the cause. Palliser dies not with a sword through him but with his own blood betraying him. This establishes the stakes and the horror of what Vasht has become, while Palliser's brief POV intrusion humanizes the victim.

2. **The siege-breaking** (Scene 2): The moment that transforms Vasht from exile to legend. She walks through an army and unmakes it. The prose must balance Miller's elevated, chronicle-like tone with Abercrombie's body-horror specificity. This is the scene where the reader understands what kind of power is in play — and begins to wonder what it cost.

3. **The celebration with the Selthine** (Scene 3): The emotional fulcrum. The only scene where Vasht is happy. Music, food, Sila's companionship. The monoplacophoran shells used as instruments. The reader knows these people are already dead (from Scene 2's reference to the Selthine massacre). Dramatic irony at its most devastating — borrowed directly from Greek tragedy, per Miller's tradition.

4. **The discovery in the tidal cave** (Scene 4): Vasht's hands bleeding, her stomach empty, grinding an ancient shell into powder. The first transformation. The first cost. This is where the sorcery system's rules become visceral — you pay in memories, in pieces of self. The monoplacophoran detail grounds the magic in something specific and strange.

5. **Vasht at the gates** (Scene 7): The ending that is a beginning. A 19-year-old with dried herbs and stubbornness. No legend, no fear, no power. The reader carries the weight of everything they know she'll become. The city hums. She walks toward a door.

### 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Opening (Scene 1)**: The reader feels dread and awe. A woman has killed someone and an entire city is afraid of her. The prose is cold, controlled, magnificent in its precision. The reader is impressed and disturbed.

**Middle (Scenes 2-4)**: As the story moves backward, the dread gives way to something more complicated — a growing tenderness toward a younger version of this terrifying woman, counterweighted by the knowledge of what she becomes. Scene 3 (the Selthine celebration) is where the reader's chest tightens, because joy in a story told backward is the most painful thing there is.

**Close (Scenes 5-7)**: By the final scenes, the reader is watching a young woman make the first steps toward everything they've already witnessed. The emotion is not catharsis, not sadness exactly, but something closer to the feeling of watching someone you love walk into a room where you know something terrible will happen, and you cannot warn them because the warning is the story you just read. It is the specific ache of knowing too much.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Joe Abercrombie (Author A)** — Most visible in: Scene 1 (the killing) and Scene 4 (survival in the tidal cave). Abercrombie's voice dominates when violence is happening, when the body is at stake, when the prose needs to be short, brutal, and darkly funny. Vasht's internal monologue throughout carries his cynicism — she sees the world clearly, expects the worst, and is rarely surprised. Key line style: "She'd killed men with less ceremony. But Palliser deserved ceremony. He'd earned at least that."

**Madeline Miller (Author B)** — Most visible in: Scene 3 (the Selthine celebration) and Scene 6 (apprenticeship with Revka). Miller's voice dominates when transformation is happening, when time stretches, when the prose needs to accumulate weight through cadence and image. The sorcery system — patient, material, rooted in knowledge of living things — is pure Circe. Key line style: "She learned the monoplacophoran the way Revka had taught her to learn everything: by holding it until her fingers knew its grain better than her own skin, until the creature's patience became indistinguishable from her own."

**The Blade Itself (Work X)** — Most visible in: The rotating POV structure. The primary Vasht close-third is interrupted by brief Palliser and Sila intrusions that show how she appears from outside. These intrusions create the dramatic irony that is Abercrombie's structural signature — the reader holds information that the current POV character lacks. The ensemble collision is compressed: these characters are converging (in chronological time) toward the killing in the great hall, and the backward telling makes every convergence feel like a divergence.

**Circe (Work Y)** — Most visible in: Scene 4 (discovery of the monoplacophorans and first sorcery) and the sorcery system throughout. Circe's themes — power earned through suffering, the outcast who becomes formidable, divinity as loneliness — are the story's thematic spine. Vasht IS a Circe figure: exiled, patient, transformed by her own transformations. The cost structure of the magic (you pay in memories, in pieces of self) comes directly from Miller's exploration of what transformation costs the transformer.

### 7. Title

**"Vasht Ascending"**

The title operates on two levels. Read in narrative order (reverse chronology), the story descends — from power to vulnerability, from goddess to girl. "Ascending" is ironic: the reader experiences a descent. But read in chronological order, Vasht IS ascending — from exile to mastery. The title names the chronological arc the reader never directly experiences but reconstructs from the backward telling. It also carries a deliberate echo of mythological apotheosis — a mortal becoming divine — while the story insists that the apotheosis was a diminishment, not an elevation.

Alternative titles considered and rejected:
- "Before the Blade" — too generic, too close to the source work title
- "Unmerciful" — single-word titles can work but this one announces the theme too loudly
- "Backward into Godhood" — used in the author meeting title, too on-the-nose
- "Shell and Sinew" — nice texture but too decorative

## Reverse Chronology — Architectural Notes for the Writer

The reverse chronology is not a gimmick. It is the story's meaning. Here's why:

1. **Every victory arrives pre-paid.** When the reader sees Vasht break the siege in Scene 2, they've already seen the end result in Scene 1 — and they know more cost is coming. The backward structure means the reader can never celebrate a triumph, because the price has already been shown.

2. **Connection becomes more precious as it approaches.** The Selthine appear first as an absence (referenced in Scene 2), then as a presence (Scene 3). Moving backward, the reader meets them AFTER knowing they're doomed. This is Greek dramatic irony: the audience knows what the characters don't.

3. **The protagonist's softening is the story's escalation.** In a forward-told story, the protagonist becoming harder and more powerful is the rising action. In reverse, the protagonist becoming softer and more vulnerable IS the rising action. The tension increases as the armor comes off.

4. **The ending refuses resolution.** The final scene is the chronological beginning — a girl walking toward a door. The reader knows everything that will happen. The story knows nothing. This asymmetry IS the story's meaning: you cannot go back and warn someone, even if you've read the whole book.

5. **Scene transitions should NOT announce the time-jump.** Don't open each scene with "Three years earlier..." Let the reader feel the temporal shift through details: Vasht's age, the condition of her hands, the presence or absence of specific scars, who is alive and who isn't. Trust the reader. They'll track it.

## Sorcery System Notes

- **Biological transformation**: Vasht's magic works by understanding what a thing IS at the cellular/material level and persuading it to become something else. Not elemental magic (fire, lightning). Not ritual magic (sigils, incantation). Intimate, material, hands-on.
- **Cost**: Every transformation costs the sorcerer something specific and personal. A memory. A sensory capacity. A piece of identity. The cost is always proportional to the scale of the transformation. Small changes cost small memories. Transforming an army costs... something the reader can infer from who Vasht has become by Scene 1.
- **Monoplacophoran shells**: The ancient limpets are the key reagent. Their survival across geological time has embedded something in their calcium carbonate — a memory of forms, a catalog of what life has been. Ground into powder, they serve as the medium through which transformation travels. Vasht calls this work "reading the shell."
- **No spoken spells, no glowing runes.** The magic looks like a woman grinding powder, mixing paste, pressing her hands against flesh. It looks like medicine. It looks like cooking. It looks like patience.

## Thread Left Open

The story never explains what Vasht did to the fishing nets (or the fish) in her home village. It's referenced twice — in Scene 5 (exile) and Scene 7 (arrival at Keth-Amara) — as the incident that made her village send her away. The reader can infer it was an early, uncontrolled transformation, but the specifics are never provided. This thread remains open: a wound before the first wound we see, a beginning before the beginning.
