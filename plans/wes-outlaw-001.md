# Story Plan: wes-outlaw-001

**Genre**: Western
**Subgenre**: Outlaw Narrative
**Combination ID**: wes-outlaw-001

**AuthorA**: Elmore Leonard — Snappy dialogue; lean prose; criminals with personality; the Western-to-crime pipeline
**AuthorB**: Annie Proulx — Brutal, compressed, landscape-saturated prose; "Brokeback Mountain"; the West as unforgiving
**WorkX**: No Country for Old Men by Cormac McCarthy — A pursuit narrative that gradually sidelines its protagonist; the antagonist as force of nature; the sheriff's monologue framing a story he cannot control
**WorkY**: Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid (screenplay by William Goldman) — The mythology of likeable outlaws; the awareness that the era is ending; charm as a survival strategy that eventually fails

**Risk Card**: THE PROTAGONIST IS WRONG — The protagonist's fundamental assumption, belief, or plan must be demonstrably incorrect, and the story must make this clear without having another character simply tell them so. The wrongness should be structural, not just a minor miscalculation.

**Target Word Count**: 5,600 words (acceptable range: 5,300-5,900)
**Reading Time**: 22 minutes

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Elmore Leonard (AuthorA)

**Source**: "Three-Ten to Yuma" (1953), opening and representative passages
**Origin**: Dime Western Magazine, March 1953; collected in *The Complete Western Stories of Elmore Leonard*
**Why representative**: This story shows Leonard's signature lean prose, tension built through dialogue rather than description, and criminals with personality — the outlaw Jim Kidd talks his way through a hostage situation while the deputy tries to maintain control.

Opening: "He had picked up his prisoner at Fort Huachuca shortly after midnight and now, in a silent early morning mist, they approached Contention."

Leonard's rules that define his voice:
- "Never use a verb other than 'said' to carry dialogue." The line of dialogue belongs to the character; the verb is the writer sticking his nose in.
- "Leave out the part that readers tend to skip." Thick paragraphs of prose with too many words in them. What the writer is doing is perpetrating "hooptedoodle."
- "If it sounds like writing, I rewrite it." Invisible prose. The reader should never feel the author's presence.
- Rule 7 on dialect: Leonard specifically praised Annie Proulx's restrained approach in *Close Range* as a model.

Leonard's dialogue technique: Characters reveal themselves through how they talk, not through authorial description. His criminals are smart, funny, and wrong. The mouth is the weapon. His Westerns read like crime fiction in period dress — the same interest in professional criminals, the same respect for competence, the same awareness that charm is a strategy with an expiration date.

### Sample 2: Annie Proulx (AuthorB)

**Source**: "The Half-Skinned Steer" and "Brokeback Mountain" from *Close Range: Wyoming Stories* (1999)
**Origin**: Atlantic Monthly, November 1997 (Half-Skinned Steer); The New Yorker, October 1997 (Brokeback Mountain)
**Why representative**: These stories show Proulx's compressed, landscape-saturated prose where the West is unforgiving geography, not romantic backdrop. The land is always the antagonist.

From "The Half-Skinned Steer": "The country poured open on each side, reduced the Cadillac to a finger-snap. Nothing had changed, not a goddamn thing..." An elderly man drives back to Wyoming for his brother's funeral, and the landscape diminishes him with every mile.

"He felt himself slip back, the calm of eighty-three years sheeted off him like water, replaced by a young man's scalding anger..."

From "Brokeback Mountain": "What Jack remembered and craved in a way he could neither help nor understand was the time that distant summer on Brokeback when Ennis had come up behind him and pulled him close, the silent embrace satisfying some shared and sexless hunger."

The passage ends: "Later, that dozy embrace solidified in his memory as the single moment of artless, charmed happiness in their separate and difficult lives."

Proulx's voice is muscular, blunt, primal. She does exhaustive research and wields precise, haunting details about place. The epigraph to *Close Range* — "Reality's never been of much use out here" — is the key. The sky gets as much attention as the landscape. Big skies making men feel small. The land is a mirror that shows you your actual size, which is smaller than you thought.

### Sample 3: No Country for Old Men (WorkX) — Cormac McCarthy

**Source**: *No Country for Old Men* (2005), Sheriff Bell's opening monologue and key quotes
**Origin**: Novel by Cormac McCarthy, Vintage International
**Why representative**: The structural DNA we're borrowing — a pursuit narrative that gradually sidelines its protagonist, framed by a peripheral narrator who can't make sense of the violence.

Sheriff Bell's opening monologue: "I sent one boy to the gas chamber at Huntsville. One and only one. My arrest and my testimony. I went up there and visited with him two or three times. Three times. The last time was the day of his execution... He'd killed a fourteen year old girl and I can tell you right now I never did have no great desire to visit with him let alone go to his execution but I done it... And he told me that he had been plannin to kill somebody for about as long as he could remember. Said that if they turned him out he'd do it again. Said he knew he was goin to hell. Told it to me out of his own mouth."

Key quotes that capture the novel's philosophy:
- "You never know what worse luck your bad luck has saved you from."
- "If the rule you followed brought you to this, of what use was the rule?"
- "This country will kill you in a heartbeat and still people love it."
- "How does a man decide in what order to abandon his life?"
- "Ever step you take is forever. You cant make it go away. None of it."

McCarthy's structural move: Llewelyn Moss, the protagonist you've been following for 200 pages, is killed offstage, between chapters, his death reported secondhand. The real subject was never the man being chased — it was the man watching the chase who couldn't make sense of it. The sheriff. The one who narrates a story he has no power to change.

### Sample 4: Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid (WorkY) — William Goldman

**Source**: *Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid* (1969), screenplay by William Goldman
**Origin**: Twentieth Century-Fox, final draft July 15, 1968
**Why representative**: The charm-as-survival-strategy dynamic, the recurring awareness that the era is ending, the buddy-outlaw mythology, and Goldman's signature wit in the face of doom.

Key dialogue exchanges:

BUTCH: "Kid, there's something I ought to tell you. I never shot anybody before."
SUNDANCE: "One hell of a time to tell me."

BUTCH: "What happened to the old bank? It was beautiful."
GUARD: "People kept robbing it."
BUTCH: "Small price to pay for beauty."

The recurring, increasingly desperate: "Who are those guys?"

BUTCH: "I couldn't do that. Could you do that? Why can they do it? Who are those guys?"

ETTA: "I'm 26, and I'm single, and a school teacher, and that's the bottom of the pit..."

SUNDANCE: "Did you say they were hired permanent?"
ETTA: "No, just until they kill you."

Goldman's structural insight: Butch survives on talk. The talking is the skill, not the gun — the mouth. The movie works because you believe the mouth will save him right up until the moment it doesn't. The freeze frame at the end is Goldman's lie — it says they died beautiful. The truth is nobody dies beautiful in that country. Goldman also understood that the buddy dynamic is a performance — two men performing for each other because the alternative is being alone, and being alone means confronting what you actually are.

### 2 Selected Concepts:

**1. The Bertillon System (Criminal Identification Anthropometry)**
Integration: The law closing in on the outlaw is not abstract — it is specifically the new Bertillon system of criminal identification spreading from the East. The Pinkerton agents pursuing our protagonist carry standardized cards with his measurements: skull width, foot length, cubit. The protagonist's charm and ability to talk his way out of situations worked when identification depended on eyewitness memory and rough sketches. Now his body has been reduced to numbers on a card. He has been measured and classified into one of 243 categories. This is the specific mechanism through which the era is ending — not metaphorically, but literally. The world has learned to see him, and seeing is different from recognizing. You can charm someone who is recognizing you. You cannot charm a number. This detail threads through the story: the protagonist's growing awareness that the system pursuing him has changed its nature, though he misreads what this means (he thinks he can outrun science; the truth is that science has already arrived where he's going).

**2. Desert Varnish**
Integration: The canyon walls that narrow around the protagonist in the final act are coated in desert varnish — that black-orange patina that takes 10,000 years to form on exposed rock. The partner (the Sundance figure) knows what it is because he once worked a season for a geological survey. He mentions it offhand: ten thousand years for the rock to grow that skin. The detail lands differently for the reader than for the protagonist. The protagonist hears it and doesn't register it — he's focused on the plan, the exit, the next bend in the canyon. But the reader hears it as the story's quiet verdict on human ambition measured against geological time. The varnish was forming before anyone in this story was born, before the idea of outlaws existed, before English was spoken on this continent. It will still be forming after the protagonist's bones are alkali dust. The landscape has been here for 10,000 years of patient chemical reaction, and these men have been here for three days. The varnish is the land's way of showing you your actual size.

## Step 2: Story Design

### 1. Premise

Two bank robbers — one a talker, one a watcher — ride into the drainage canyons of southern Wyoming in the autumn of 1899 with a plan to hit the last unprotected bank before the railroad completes its spur line. The talker believes they are Butch and Sundance: two charming outlaws staying one step ahead of a closing world. The watcher has known for months that the plan is broken, that the Pinkerton agents behind them carry a new system of identification that has already reduced both men to measurements on a card, and that the era the talker mourns as ending has in fact already ended. The story the protagonist is living in is not the story he is in.

### 2. Protagonist

**Name**: Gar Olmstead
**Age**: 37
**Situation**: A career bank robber in the southern Wyoming and Colorado territories who has survived fifteen years on charm, speed, and the distance between towns. He rides with his partner, Lyle Duff — a quiet, competent man ten years his junior who has been with him for four years.

**What he WANTS**: To hit the Rawlins Stockmen's Bank before the Union Pacific spur line brings Pinkerton agents and telegraph service to the last unconnected corridor in Wyoming. One last job, then south — maybe Mexico, maybe Arizona Territory. The plan.

**What he NEEDS**: To understand that the plan is the problem. That the plan has been the problem for years. That every plan he's made since about 1895 has been a story he tells himself to avoid sitting still long enough to hear what the country is telling him — that he is not good at this, has never been as good at this as he believes, and that the people around him (Lyle, the woman he left in Laramie, the lawmen who let him go twice because he wasn't worth the paperwork) have all seen the hole in him that he refuses to see.

**His flaw/blind spot**: Gar believes his charm works. He believes talking is a skill that saves people. He believes the banter between himself and Lyle is evidence of a partnership, when it is actually evidence of a performance — two men talking so they don't have to be quiet, because quiet is where the truth lives. His fundamental wrongness is structural: he thinks the era is ending on his watch, that he is riding the last wave. The era ended before him. He arrived after the story was already over. He is not Butch Cassidy at the closing of an age. He is a man doing an impression of Butch Cassidy in a world that has already moved past the original.

### 3. Structure

**Opening image/scene**: A Pinkerton agent named Harlan Scobie sits in the Rawlins telegraph office in October 1899, filling out a Bertillon identification card for Gar Olmstead. He has the measurements from a prior arrest in Denver — skull width, right ear length, left foot, left middle finger, left cubit, standing height. He is filing these into one of 243 categories. He writes the physical description: a description that matches Gar perfectly but could also match several hundred other men in the territory. Then he adds the notation that makes Gar identifiable: "Talks. Constant. Uses humor to manage situations. Subject believes himself charming." Scobie seals the card in an envelope and posts it to the railway express office. He has all winter. The spur line will be done by spring. He doesn't need to chase Gar. He just needs to wait for the world to get smaller.

Then CUT TO: Gar and Lyle, two days' ride from Rawlins, camped in a drainage wash, Gar talking. Talking about the plan. Talking about Mexico. Talking about the bank. Lyle listening, or appearing to listen, sharpening a knife on a whetstone, not looking up.

**The inciting disruption**: Gar and Lyle ride into the town of Separation, Wyoming (a real ghost town on the old Overland Trail), to buy supplies before the bank job. In the general store, Gar sees a posted Pinkerton circular — not a sketch, not a description, but a Bertillon card. His measurements. His category number. His physical dimensions reduced to numerals and fractions. He sees himself turned into arithmetic. He reads the notation about his talking and laughs — he thinks it's funny, thinks it proves the Pinkertons know him, which he takes as evidence of his significance. Lyle sees the same card and goes quiet. He understands what Gar does not: this is not fame. This is the end of anonymity. You cannot talk your way past a number.

**The escalating middle**: Gar proceeds with the plan. He scouts the bank, times the shift changes, identifies the route out through the canyon system south of town. He is good at this — the planning, the talking, the performance of competence. He charms a livery owner into boarding their horses for free. He buys drinks for a railroad surveyor and extracts the date the spur line will arrive (three months). He narrates the whole thing to Lyle like a story he's telling to an audience, because that's what it is.

Meanwhile, the story shows us what Gar doesn't see:
- Lyle has been corresponding with his sister in Nebraska. He has been talking about leaving. He carries the letters in his saddlebag, not hidden but not mentioned, the way you carry something you've already decided about.
- The livery owner didn't give them free boarding because of Gar's charm. He gave it because he recognized the Bertillon description and wants them to stay put until the sheriff returns from Cheyenne.
- The railroad surveyor Gar charmed has already mentioned two strangers to the express office manager, who has already wired the Pinkerton office in Denver.

The reader sees both movies: the comedy Gar is starring in (two charming outlaws, one last job, the banter, the plan) and the tragedy he is actually in (the world has already seen him, the partner is already leaving, the charm never worked the way he thought it did).

**The crisis point**: Gar and Lyle hit the bank. It goes wrong — not catastrophically, but revealingly. The bank teller doesn't flinch, doesn't comply with Gar's routine. The teller has seen a robbery before, or more precisely, has been trained for one — the Stockmen's Association has been drilling banks along the corridor since the Pinkerton report flagged the route. Gar has to actually threaten violence, which he has never been good at, because violence is silence and Gar is noise. They get the money — less than expected, because the bank was warned and moved its reserves — and ride south into the canyon system.

In the canyon, Lyle tells Gar he is leaving. Not after Mexico. Now. After they split the money. He is going to Nebraska. His sister has a farm. He is done.

Gar responds the way he always responds: he talks. He makes the case. He invokes the partnership, the plan, the South American dream. He performs Butch-to-Sundance. And for the first time in four years, Lyle doesn't play the role. He just looks at Gar and says nothing. The silence is enormous. It fills the canyon. And in the silence, Gar can hear the country — the wind, the gravel, the distance — but he doesn't listen to it. He starts talking again, louder, because the louder he talks the less he can hear the silence that is the truth.

**The ending**: They split the money in the narrowing canyon. The walls are coated in desert varnish — Lyle mentions it, ten thousand years for the rock to grow that skin. Gar doesn't hear it. Lyle rides north. Gar rides south, alone, into the drainage that runs forty miles and then stops.

The final section shifts perspective — not to Gar but to Scobie, the Pinkerton agent, writing his report in the Rawlins telegraph office weeks later. The bank job. The recovery of most of the money. The partner apprehended in Nebraska (Lyle, who went quietly). And the primary subject — Olmstead — still at large, heading south, into country that narrows. Scobie notes the measurements on the card. Notes the file number. Notes that every sheriff's office between Rawlins and the Arizona border has a copy. He does not need to chase Olmstead. He needs to wait. The spur line will be done by spring. The telegraph will reach Separation by January. The world is getting smaller at a rate of approximately four miles of wire per day.

The last paragraph is the landscape: the drainage running south, narrowing, the desert varnish on the walls, the sky becoming a ribbon, a thread. No person in it. Just country. The sound a place makes when it's done with you.

### 4. Key Scenes (5)

1. **The Bertillon Card in the General Store** (inciting disruption + character revelation): Gar reads his own measurements posted on the wall and laughs. He thinks it's fame. Lyle reads the same card and understands it's a death sentence — not a dramatic one, but a bureaucratic one. The scene does two things: advances the plot (they're identified) and reveals the core wrongness (Gar misreads every signal the world sends him). This is where the Leonard influence is strongest — two criminals reading the same document, one seeing comedy, one seeing tragedy, the whole scene carried in dialogue.

2. **Gar Charming the Livery Owner** (escalating middle + dramatic irony): A scene of pure Leonard dialogue — Gar working a mark, the patter fluid and funny, the reader enjoying the performance. But the scene has a second layer the reader catches and Gar doesn't: the livery owner is too agreeable. He's keeping them in town. The charm isn't working — it's being allowed to work, which is different and worse.

3. **The Bank Robbery** (crisis point): The teller doesn't flinch. The trained response reveals that the system has reached this far — not the Pinkertons themselves, but the Pinkerton method: standardized preparation, anticipated routes, drilled responses. Gar's improvisatory style hits a prepared surface for the first time. The robbery succeeds but the take is thin. This scene channels the Goldman/Butch Cassidy influence: "What happened to the old bank?" "People kept robbing it." The romantic outlaw meets institutional response.

4. **Lyle's Departure in the Canyon** (emotional climax): The partner says he's leaving. Gar tries to talk him out of it — the Butch-to-Sundance pitch. Lyle doesn't argue. He just stops performing. The silence is the story. This scene fuses Proulx (the landscape bearing down, the canyon walls narrowing, the desert varnish marking geological time) with the Goldman dynamic (the partnership was a performance, and when one actor leaves the stage, the other is exposed). Lyle's line about desert varnish — ten thousand years — is the story's quiet verdict on human ambition.

5. **Scobie's Report** (the frame, the McCarthy structure): The Pinkerton agent writes his report. Factual, bureaucratic, clinical. The protagonist has been reduced from a person to a file number. The story's emotional center — Gar riding south, alone, into narrowing country — is reported as a line item. The reader knows what it means. Scobie doesn't care what it means. The sheriff (equivalent) narrates a story he doesn't need to understand because understanding is no longer required. The system works without understanding. This is the No Country for Old Men structure: the protagonist sidelined, his story continuing offstage, the frame narrative cold and competent and missing the point, which is that the point was never the point.

### 5. The Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning**: Pleasure. The reader enjoys Gar the way they enjoy Butch Cassidy — he's funny, quick, competent-seeming. The banter with Lyle is warm. The plan sounds possible. The reader is in the comedy.

**Middle**: Growing unease. The reader starts catching the signals Gar misses. The livery owner's compliance. The Bertillon card's precision. Lyle's silences. The comedy is still playing but the tragedy is bleeding through at the edges, the way you can hear a second frequency underneath a note.

**Crisis**: The sick recognition of watching someone you like walk into a trap they built themselves. The bank robbery goes thin. Lyle leaves. Gar keeps talking into the silence. The reader wants him to stop talking and listen, but he can't, because listening would mean hearing the country, and hearing the country would mean hearing the truth about himself.

**Ending**: Not devastation. Something colder. The administrative indifference of Scobie's report. The landscape that was there before Gar and will be there after. The reader puts the story down feeling the specific emptiness of watching someone ride hard in the wrong direction and knowing that the wrong direction is the only direction they were ever capable of going. A sense that the world has moved past a man who never noticed it moving.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Elmore Leonard (AuthorA)** — Concentrated in the dialogue scenes: the Bertillon card scene in the general store, the charming of the livery owner, and every exchange between Gar and Lyle. Lean prose, "said" as the only dialogue tag, no interiority when characters are talking, the sense that people reveal themselves through what they say and especially through what they choose not to say. Gar's voice is a Leonard voice — snappy, self-amusing, performing for an audience that may not be there. The whole story should feel like Leonard wrote the people and Proulx wrote the ground they stand on.

**Annie Proulx (AuthorB)** — Concentrated in every passage where the landscape appears: the drainage wash at camp, the approach to Separation, the canyon system south of town, and the final image. Compressed, brutal, precise. The land as antagonist. The sky measured. The distance made physical. The specific detail — sage so thick it coats the inside of your throat, desert varnish that takes 10,000 years to form, the alkali flat at the end of the drainage where the water runs out. Every landscape passage should make the reader feel the West's indifference to human intention.

**No Country for Old Men (WorkX)** — Concentrated in the opening (Scobie filling out the Bertillon card, a cold institutional frame), the structure (the protagonist gradually losing control of his own story), and the ending (Scobie's report, the protagonist reduced to a line item, the frame narrator's clinical indifference to the emotional content of the story he's recording). Also in the philosophical subtext: "If the rule you followed brought you to this, of what use was the rule?" Gar followed the rule of charm, and it brought him to a narrowing canyon, alone.

**Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid (WorkY)** — Concentrated in the Gar/Lyle dynamic, the bank robbery, and the departure scene. The mythology of likeable outlaws. The awareness that the era is ending (Gar's version) vs. the awareness that the era has already ended (the story's truth). Charm as a survival strategy that eventually fails. The buddy performance as a way of avoiding being alone. "Who are those guys?" becomes the Bertillon system — the faceless, methodical, uncharmable pursuit. Gar's wrongness is Butch's wrongness magnified: Butch at least recognized the walls were closing in. Gar thinks the walls are closing in when in fact they closed years ago and he's been living inside them, calling the enclosure "the open road."

### 7. Title

**Riding the Backwash**

This title comes directly from the author meeting — Proulx's observation that the protagonist is "not riding the last wave. He's swimming in the backwash." It captures the story's central irony without announcing it. It suggests movement (riding) that is actually a consequence of movement that's already finished (backwash). It has a Western feel without being obviously Western. It does not start with "The."

Alternative titles considered and rejected:
- "Charm and Measurement" — too clever, announces the theme
- "Both Movies" — too meta, references the author meeting too directly
- "Annotation on the Silence" — beautiful but too literary for a Western
- "Four Miles of Wire Per Day" — specific but obscure
- "Separation" — too minimal, and could be any genre

## Risk Card Implementation: THE PROTAGONIST IS WRONG

Gar Olmstead's wrongness is not a single miscalculation but a structural misreading of his entire situation. The story demonstrates this through accumulating evidence that the reader can see and Gar cannot:

1. **He is wrong about his charm**: The livery owner complies not because Gar is charming but because he wants Gar to stay in town. The railroad surveyor he "works" immediately reports them. His charm has never been as effective as he believes — the two lawmen who "let him go" in previous years did so because he wasn't worth the paperwork, not because he talked his way out.

2. **He is wrong about the era**: He believes he is riding the last wave of a closing frontier. The frontier closed before he started. The Bertillon system was adopted in 1882. The Pinkertons have been using standardized identification for fifteen years. Gar is not witnessing the end of an era — he is performing a reenactment of it.

3. **He is wrong about his partnership**: He believes his dynamic with Lyle is Butch-and-Sundance, a partnership forged in mutual respect and shared danger. Lyle has been planning to leave for months. The letters to Nebraska are not hidden. Gar never looked because looking would mean seeing that the performance had an audience of one.

4. **He is wrong about the plan**: One last job, then south. But the bank was warned. The money was moved. The route south narrows into a drainage that runs forty miles and stops. There is no "south." South is a wall of alkali and evaporated ambition.

The story never has a character tell Gar he is wrong. Instead:
- The Bertillon card tells him, and he misreads it as fame.
- The livery owner tells him, and he misreads compliance as persuasion.
- The teller's trained non-response tells him, and he misreads it as one bad bank.
- Lyle's silence tells him, and he fills it with talk.
- The landscape tells him constantly — the narrowing canyon, the ten-thousand-year varnish, the drainage that stops — and he never looks.

The protagonist is demonstrably, structurally wrong about everything, and the story makes this clear through action, irony, and landscape rather than through any character's speech. The reader holds both movies: the comedy Gar is starring in, and the tragedy he is actually in.
