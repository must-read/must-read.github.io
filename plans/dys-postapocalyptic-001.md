# Story Plan: dys-postapocalyptic-001

## Assignment

- **Genre**: Dystopian / Post-Apocalyptic
- **Combination ID**: dys-postapocalyptic-001
- **AuthorA**: Octavia Butler — Visceral, unflinching prose; power dynamics; race, gender, and survival in extreme conditions
- **AuthorB**: P.D. James — Literary dystopia; elegiac, morally serious; the quiet end of humanity
- **WorkX (structure)**: *The Road* by Cormac McCarthy — Episodic, stripped-to-bone journey narrative; no chapters, just relentless forward motion through ruin; the father-child unit as last moral structure
- **WorkY (themes)**: *Station Eleven* by Emily St. John Mandel — Art as survival mechanism after collapse; the persistence of beauty in wreckage; memory of the old world as both sustenance and poison
- **Target word count**: 4,100 words (acceptable range: 3,800-4,400)
- **Risk card**: None
- **Formula summary**: "Butler's unflinching survival instinct meets James's elegiac moral gravity, driven by McCarthy's stripped-bone journey structure and Mandel's insistence that art outlasts collapse — a post-apocalyptic story about what people carry when they can only carry one thing."

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Octavia Butler — *Parable of the Sower* (AuthorA)

**Source**: *Parable of the Sower*, Chapter 1 (journal entry format)
**Why representative**: Demonstrates Butler's first-person immediacy, her matter-of-fact rendering of a violent, resource-scarce world, and her protagonist's unflinching awareness of power dynamics.

> Saturday, July 20, 2024
>
> A new neighborhood group has formed. I'm not sure what they plan to do, but it's good to see people getting together. All the homes on our street have walls and fences, and most have gates. We don't go out as much as we used to. When we do go out, we go in groups. That's the way things have been for years. Dad says things were different once. He and Mom came here from Los Angeles back when I was a baby. He says L.A. was already too dangerous. Lots of people were moving to small towns then or building walled communities.
>
> I think about that. I don't remember anything else. This is my world, the only one I've ever known. When Dad preaches about God's love, I think he's preaching about something he knew once. Something he remembers. Something that doesn't exist anymore—at least not out here.

Butler's prose style is spare and direct — diary entries that read like dispatches from a war zone. She never flinches. The violence is stated, not dramatized. The social structures of oppression are observed with an anthropologist's precision. Her protagonists carry knowledge of how power works in their bodies: hyperempathy in Lauren's case, the literal capacity to feel others' pain. The prose moves with purpose — no ornament, no sentimentality, but deep emotional intelligence underneath the stripped surface.

**Key Butler techniques for this story**:
- First-person or close-third with visceral body awareness
- Power dynamics rendered through concrete physical detail
- Survival as labor, not abstraction
- Race, gender, and class as structural forces, not themes to be announced
- Short, declarative sentences that accumulate force through repetition

### Sample 2: P.D. James — *The Children of Men* (AuthorB)

**Source**: *The Children of Men*, opening passage
**Why representative**: Shows James's elegiac, diary-entry approach to civilizational decline — morally serious, intellectually precise, suffused with a grief that never becomes melodramatic.

> Early this morning, 1 January 2021, three minutes after midnight, the last human being to be born on earth was killed in a pub brawl in a suburb of Buenos Aires, aged twenty-five years, two months and twelve days. If the first reports are to be believed, Joseph Ricardo died as he had lived. No one, certainly, will grieve for him. [...] I shall start my diary on this first day of a new century and of the new millennium—my fiftieth birthday.

James's later passage on the quietus (mass suicide of the elderly):

> The mud-grey sea heaved sluggishly under a sky the colour of thin milk, faintly luminous at the horizon, where there hung great bunches of darker-grey and black cloud, like a half-raised curtain.

And on the Rome pilgrimage:

> ...the rows of spluttering candles, the kneeling women, rich and poor, young and old, fixing their eyes on the Virgin's face with an intensity of longing almost too painful to witness. He remembered their outstretched arms, their palms pressed against the glass protective shield, the low continual mutter of their prayers as if this ceaseless anguished moan came from a single throat and carried to that unregarding marble the hopeless longing of all the world.

**Key James techniques for this story**:
- The elegiac sentence: long, cadenced, with subordinate clauses that build weight
- Moral seriousness without moralizing
- The quiet — not silence (dramatic) but quiet (the sound of purpose leaking out)
- Civilization's decay rendered through its lingering habits: lawn-mowing, shirt-ironing, prayer
- Hope as imposition, demanded by a world that no longer deserves it

### Sample 3: Cormac McCarthy — *The Road* (WorkX — Structure)

**Source**: *The Road*, opening pages
**Why representative**: The stripped-to-bone prose, absence of quotation marks, episodic structure of camps and encounters, the relentless forward motion.

> When he woke in the woods in the dark and the cold of the night he'd reach out to touch the child sleeping beside him. Nights dark beyond darkness and the days more gray each one than what had gone before. Like the onset of some cold glaucoma dimming away the world.
>
> [...] He pushed the cart and both he and the boy carried knapsacks. In the knapsacks were essential things in case they had to abandon the cart and run for their lives. Clamped to the handle of the cart was a chrome motorcycle mirror that he used to watch the road behind them. He shifted the pack higher on his shoulders and looked out over the wasted country. The road was empty. Below in the little valley the still gray serpent of a river. Motionless and precise. Along the shore a burden of dead reeds.
>
> [...] They set out along the blacktop in the gunmetal light, shuffling through the ash, each the other's world entire.

**Key McCarthy structural techniques for this story**:
- Episodic, camp-to-camp movement; no chapters, just section breaks
- Stripped prose — no quotation marks, minimal punctuation
- The journey as grammar of survival: each stop temporary, each meal possibly the last
- Encounters with other survivors as moral tests
- The dyad (father-son, here transformed to another configuration) as last moral structure
- Landscape descriptions that are simultaneously physical and spiritual

### Sample 4: Emily St. John Mandel — *Station Eleven* (WorkY — Themes)

**Source**: *Station Eleven*, the Travelling Symphony passages
**Why representative**: The persistence of art after collapse, the tension between survival and beauty, the traveling performers who insist that Shakespeare matters in a world without electricity.

> Twenty years after the collapse they were still in motion, travelling back and forth along the shores of Lakes Huron and Michigan. [...] The caravans had once been pickup trucks, but now they were pulled by teams of horses on wheels of steel and wood. All of the pieces rendered useless by the end of gasoline had been removed—the engine, the fuel-supply system, all the other components that no one under the age of twenty had ever seen in operation—and a bench had been installed on top of each cab for the drivers.

And the novel's central axiom, painted on the lead caravan: **"Survival is insufficient."**

> Jeevan's understanding of disaster preparedness was based entirely on action movies, but on the other hand, he'd seen a lot of action movies. He started with water, filled one of the oversized shopping carts with as many cases and bottles as he could fit. There was a moment of doubt on the way to the cash registers, straining against the weight of the cart—was he overreacting?

**Key Mandel thematic techniques for this story**:
- Art as survival mechanism, not luxury — but also not automatically virtuous
- Memory of the old world as both sustenance and poison
- The traveling performer/artist as protagonist
- The specific, material detail of post-collapse logistics: what works, what rusts, what gets repurposed
- "Survival is insufficient" as philosophy — staying alive is only half the project

### 2 Selected Concepts

**1. Fishing Branch / Ni'iinlii Njik (Yukon thermal springs)**
**Integration**: The protagonist's destination — a place she's heard rumor of — where geothermal springs keep water warm year-round, even in a world gone cold after the collapse. Not a paradise but a real place with a physical explanation: limestone aquifers, thermal upwelling, water that refuses to freeze. She doesn't know if the settlement there still exists, only that the water is warm and the salmon still run. The Gwitchin name — "the place where salmon spawn" — becomes a piece of knowledge she carries, told to her by someone who died. It gives the journey a direction without guaranteeing an arrival.

**2. Emilia / tassel flower (tropical medicinal plant)**
**Integration**: Along the road, the protagonist encounters a settlement where a woman has been cultivating emilia plants in a makeshift greenhouse — tiny red tassel flowers that serve as both food (edible leaves) and wound treatment (antimicrobial properties). The woman calls them "Flora's Paintbrush." The protagonist is offered a handful of dried emilia leaves to carry — they weigh almost nothing. She must decide whether to take the medicine-food or preserve space in her pack for the sheet music she's been protecting. This becomes one of the story's concrete choice-points: the practical versus the beautiful, compressed into an actual physical decision about what fits in a bag.

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

A cellist named Adaora walks north through the remains of the Pacific Northwest, five years after a climate-and-infrastructure collapse that killed the power grid and most of the population, carrying a cracked cello on her back and a bundle of handwritten sheet music in her pack. She has heard of a settlement at a place called Ni'iinlii Njik — thermal springs that keep a river warm even in winter, where people have gathered because the water never freezes. The question the story asks: what does it cost to carry beauty through a world that only rewards the practical — and is the cost the same as the price?

### 2. Protagonist

- **Name**: Adaora Obi, 31
- **Situation**: Former cello student at a Pacific Northwest conservatory who was three months from her graduate recital when the grid went down. Has been walking for two years, part of a loose column of migrants that has thinned to almost nothing. Travels alone now.
- **What she WANTS**: To reach Ni'iinlii Njik, where she's been told people still gather, where there is warm water and food, where she might play for an audience again.
- **What she NEEDS**: To understand that she carries the cello for herself — not for any audience, not to preserve culture, not to prove that art matters. The weight in her arms is the shape of her own refusal to become only a body that eats and walks.
- **Flaw/blind spot**: She believes carrying the cello is an act of service — that she's preserving something for others, for the future, for humanity's memory. This belief lets her avoid confronting the selfish truth: she carries it because without it her hands feel wrong, because the calluses on her fingertips are the last proof that she was once a person with a life she chose. The selfishness isn't a failure — it's the actual engine of survival — but she can't see it, and her inability to see it makes her sanctimonious about her burden in ways that alienate the people she meets.

### 3. Structure

**Opening image/scene**: Adaora tuning the cello at dawn in an abandoned gas station. The instrument is held together with pine resin and wire where the neck cracked in a fall three months ago. The tuning is wrong — the repairs changed the resonance — but she adjusts her fingering to compensate. She plays four bars of a Bach suite, and what comes out is Bach filtered through damage: recognizable but altered, the overtones strange and wooden. She stops. She doesn't know whether what she just played was music or a memory of music.

**Inciting disruption**: She encounters a settlement built in a decommissioned railway workshop — a small community of about forty people who have organized around repair: fixing tools, salvaging metal, keeping a diesel generator running two hours a day for their welding equipment. The settlement has food, water, a medic. They have no music. They offer her a place if she stays. The leader, a former civil engineer named Dov, tells her plainly: "We don't need a cellist. We need someone who can carry pipe." The disruption is not violence but an offer of safety that requires surrender of the instrument.

**Escalating middle**: Adaora stays three days. During this time: (a) She meets Hessa, a woman who tends a greenhouse of tassel flowers — emilia plants — that serve as food and wound medicine. Hessa offers her dried emilia leaves to carry, but Adaora's pack is full because of the sheet music. The choice crystallizes. (b) She plays for the settlement one evening. The response is complicated — a few people weep, a few walk away, one man asks her to stop because the sound reminds him of his daughter's piano lessons and he can't bear it. The music doesn't heal. It sits beside the pain, as James would have it. (c) She learns that Ni'iinlii Njik may not exist as she imagines it — another traveler passed through weeks ago saying the settlement there had been taken over by a group that charges admission in labor: three months' work for the right to stay. It may not be a refuge. It may be another economy with different currencies.

**Crisis point**: On the morning she plans to leave, Dov finds her packing the cello and tells her she's making a mistake. He doesn't say it cruelly. He says: "I had a boat once. A twenty-four-foot sailboat. I loved it the way you love that thing. I burned it for fuel the first winter. And here I am." Adaora has to choose: leave with the cello toward an uncertain destination, or stay in a functional settlement and become a pipe-carrier. But the real crisis is internal — Dov's honesty forces her to confront why she carries the instrument. Is it for others? For culture? For the future? Or is it for her own hands, her own identity, her own refusal to be reduced? And if it's for herself, is that enough?

**Ending**: Adaora leaves. She walks north. The ending does not show her arriving at Ni'iinlii Njik. Instead, we see her at the next camp, alone, at the edge of a river that is not warm, that is ordinary cold water. She takes out the cello. She plays — not Bach, not anything she learned. Something that comes from the cracked instrument and her stiffening fingers, something that is neither the old world nor the new one. The sound goes out over the water and nobody hears it. She plays until her fingers hurt and then she stops and sits in the silence, and the last image is not the music but the creak of the folding camp chair and her breath in the cold air and the river moving. She is still walking. She has not arrived. The thread of whether Ni'iinlii Njik is real, or worth reaching, or already ruined, remains open.

### 4. Key Scenes

- **The gas station tuning**: Opens the story. Establishes the cracked instrument, the altered sound, the physical reality of carrying a cello through ruin. Does double duty: hooks with immediate sensory detail and establishes the central metaphor (Bach filtered through damage = old culture transformed by catastrophe).

- **Dov's offer / the railway workshop settlement**: The functional community that has no room for art. Adaora sees what survival looks like when it's organized around usefulness alone: clean water, repaired tools, a generator schedule, assigned labor. It works. It's also missing something she can't name, though she tries, and her attempts to name it sound preachy even to her own ears.

- **Hessa's greenhouse / the emilia leaves**: The woman who grows tassel flowers. The dried leaves weigh almost nothing and could save Adaora's life if she gets an infected wound. The sheet music weighs more. Adaora takes some of the leaves — but she doesn't discard the sheet music to make room. She stuffs the leaves into the cello's body through the f-hole, where they rattle faintly when she walks. Medicine inside the instrument. This image — practical survival literally contained within the vessel of beauty — is the story's answer to its own question, though Adaora doesn't recognize it as such.

- **The evening performance**: Adaora plays for the settlement. The response is not triumph. One person finds it unbearable (the man whose daughter played piano). Others are moved but unsure what to do with the feeling. The music does not transform or heal. It occupies the same space as their grief without diminishing it. After she finishes, the loudest sound is the creak of her chair.

- **Departure and the cold river**: The final scene. Adaora playing for no one at the edge of ordinary water. The sound going out and being absorbed by the landscape. Not a concert, not a performance, not an act of cultural preservation. Just a woman playing an instrument because her arms would feel wrong without it.

### 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning**: The reader feels the weight — physical, literal weight. A woman carrying a cello through ash and ruin. There's something absurd about it, and something stubborn, and the reader doesn't yet know which feeling to trust.

**Middle**: The reader's sympathy complicates. Adaora is admirable but also a little self-righteous about her burden. When Dov offers her safety, the reader understands the offer's appeal and resents Adaora slightly for refusing it on principle. When the man asks her to stop playing, the reader feels the inadequacy of art — not its failure, but its inadequacy, which is different.

**End**: The reader feels the ache of something unresolved. Not hope exactly — not despair exactly. Something closer to the recognition that carrying a heavy, impractical thing through a ruined world is neither heroic nor foolish but simply what this particular person does, and that the doing of it — the labor of it, the practice of it — is its own justification, though no one will ever say so, least of all Adaora herself. The reader puts the story down and is aware of their own hands.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Butler (AuthorA) — most visible in**: The railway workshop settlement scenes. The power dynamics — who decides what's useful, who allocates labor, who eats first — rendered through concrete physical detail in Butler's unflinching style. Dov is not a villain; he's a pragmatist whose pragmatism has a cost he can't see. The hierarchy of the settlement is shown through body language, meal portions, who speaks first. Adaora's awareness of her own body — the calluses, the shoulder pain, the way the cello's weight has reshaped her spine — is Butler's visceral embodiment of survival.

**James (AuthorB) — most visible in**: The evening performance scene and the ending. The elegiac cadences, the long sentences that build weight through subordinate clauses, the moral seriousness that refuses to moralize. James's influence shapes the register when the story contemplates what has been lost — not with nostalgia but with the precision of an inventory. The quiet after the music stops. The world's refusal to be healed by beauty. Hope as labor rather than feeling.

**McCarthy / *The Road* (WorkX) — most visible in**: The overall structure. Episodic, stripped, forward-moving. Short sections separated by white space rather than chapters. The journey itself as grammar. Spare prose in the travel passages — landscape rendered in compound clauses without conjunctions, ash and road and sky. The encounters with other survivors as moral tests. The section where Adaora walks alone between settlements should read like McCarthy: declarative, rhythmic, the vocabulary of ruin.

**Mandel / *Station Eleven* (WorkY) — most visible in**: Hessa's greenhouse scene and the central thematic question. The specific, material detail of what survives collapse — the tassel flowers that are both food and medicine, the repurposed railway workshop, the cello held together with resin and wire. Mandel's insistence that survival is insufficient, that beauty persists not because it's useful but because some people are too stubborn or too selfish to let it go. The traveling artist as protagonist. The question of what you carry.

### 7. Title

**"Resonance and Ash"**

Structure: compound form using "and" — joins an abstract musical/emotional concept with a concrete post-apocalyptic image. Does not start with "The." The word "resonance" carries multiple meanings: the physical resonance of the cello (altered by its cracks and repairs), the emotional resonance of art in a world of ruin, and the question of whether beauty resonates at all when no one is listening. "Ash" grounds it in McCarthy's landscape and the physical world of the story.

## Notes for the Writer

- The prose should alternate between two registers: Butler's spare, visceral, body-aware sentences for the travel and survival passages, and James's longer, more cadenced, elegiac sentences for the moments of reflection and performance. The shift should feel organic, not programmatic — Butler when Adaora is moving and acting, James when she stops and the silence presses in.

- Do NOT let the ending be kind. Do not let the music heal anything. Do not let Adaora have an epiphany about why she carries the cello. She keeps carrying it. That's the statement. The reader can supply the meaning.

- The creak of the chair after the music stops (from the author meeting) is a crucial image. Use it at least twice: once after the settlement performance, once in the final scene. The second time, it should carry the accumulated weight of the whole story.

- Adaora's name is Nigerian (Igbo), meaning "the people's daughter." Her parents were first-generation immigrants. This is part of her background but should surface through specific detail (what her mother cooked, what language her grandmother spoke when she called), not through exposition.

- The cello is a physical character. Describe it the way McCarthy describes landscape: its grain, its cracks, its weight, the smell of the pine resin, the wire repairs catching light. The reader should feel the instrument in their own arms.

- Ni'iinlii Njik should remain at the edge of the story — mentioned, rumored, possibly real, never reached. The Gwitchin name should be spoken once by someone who knew what it meant, and Adaora should carry the words without being sure of the pronunciation.

- The emilia leaves inside the cello: this is the story's central image-as-argument. Medicine inside the instrument. The practical contained within the beautiful. Do NOT explain this. Let it be an action, a physical fact, and let the reader do the work.

- Target word count: 4,100 words (3,800-4,400 acceptable). This is enough for 4-5 episodes on the road with one extended settlement scene. Do not try to cover too much ground geographically — three locations maximum (gas station opening, railway workshop settlement, cold river ending).
