# Story Plan: cn-truecrime-001

## Metadata
- **Genre**: crime-noir
- **Subgenre**: true-crime-fiction
- **Slug**: cn-truecrime-001
- **Target Word Count**: 5,200 (range: 4,900-5,500)
- **Reading Time**: 21 minutes
- **Risk Card**: None

## Formula Elements
- **Author A (style)**: James Ellroy — staccato telegraphic prose, obsessive institutional corruption, documents as evidence, compound nouns, police-report rhythm
- **Author B (style)**: Megan Abbott — noir sensibility applied to female experience, sleek insinuating prose, body-aware, what it costs women to navigate male systems
- **Work X (structure)**: An American Tragedy (Dreiser) — crushing institutional power determining who gets justice; outcome decided before the process begins
- **Work Y (themes)**: A Time to Kill (Grisham) — courtroom as moral arena; the gap between law and justice; legal professional confronting system failure

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1 — James Ellroy (Author A)
**Source**: *White Jazz* (1992), opening prologue
**Origin**: Chapter 1, Dave Klein narration

> All I have is the will to remember. Time revoked/fever dreams — I wake up reaching, afraid I'll forget. Pictures keep the woman young. L.A., fall 1958. Newsprint: link the dots. Names, events — so brutal they beg to be connected. Years down — the story stays dispersed. The names are dead or too guilty to tell. I'm old, afraid I'll forget: I killed innocent men. I betrayed sacred oaths. I reaped profit from horror. Fever — that time burning. I want to go with the music — spin, fall with it.

**Why representative**: This is Ellroy's most compressed first-person voice — fragments as sentences, dashes replacing conjunctions, the rhythm of confession rendered as machine-gun staccato. The narrator reconstructs events through documents and memory. Every sentence punches. No connective tissue. The reader must assemble meaning from shrapnel.

### Sample 2 — James Ellroy (Author A)
**Source**: *L.A. Confidential* (1990), political bash scene
**Origin**: Jack Vincennes sequence

> He hit the bash at full swing: Ellis Loew, a suite packed with Republican bigshots. Women in cocktail gowns; men in dark suits. The Big V chinos, a sport shirt sprayed with dog blood.

And later:

> "Cocksucker, I shit where you breathe. I put Bill McPherson in the tank for you, I cold-coked him and put him in bed with that colored girl, I fucking deserve to know who you want me to put the screws to next."

**Why representative**: Demonstrates the telegraphic scene-setting — compound images, semicolons as connective tissue, profanity as rhythm. Dialogue functions as institutional betrayal made audible. The language of corruption is clipped, transactional, percussive.

### Sample 3 — Megan Abbott (Author B)
**Source**: *Bury Me Deep* (2009), descriptive passage
**Origin**: Early chapters

> Thrill parties every night over on Hussel Street. That tiny house, why, it's 600 square feet of percolating, Wurlitzering sin. Those girls with their young skin, tight and glamorous, their rimy lungs and scratchy voices, one cheek flush and c'mon boys and the other, so accommodating, even with lil' wrists and ankles stripped to pearly bone by sickness. They lay there on their daybed, men all standing over round, fingering pocket chains and hands curled about gin bottle necks.

**Why representative**: Abbott's body-aware prose — skin, lungs, wrists, ankles, bone. The female body as site of both pleasure and exploitation. The prose insinuates rather than declares. Rhythm is sinuous, not staccato — long sentences that accumulate physical detail until the reader feels claustrophobic. The narration occupies the male gaze while simultaneously critiquing it.

### Sample 4 — Megan Abbott (Author B)
**Source**: *The Turnout* (2021), opening / Interview (Hazlitt, 2021)
**Origin**: Chapter 1 + author's own description of her method

> They were dancers. Their whole lives, nearly. They were dancers who taught dance and taught it well, as their mother had.

Abbott on her own process: "I tend to write about bodies a lot, maybe because I've never had the ability to be artful or successful with my body." And: "I can only really conceive of a story if it's around a crime!" And: "As a very lapsed Catholic, those are the only two things [I'm] interested in anyway" — referring to sex and death as story engines.

**Why representative**: The opening — deceptively simple, rhythmic, incantatory — establishes Abbott's method: repetition rather than elaboration for emphasis. Her prose is "noir-inflected," built on "furtive, squirrelly sensations" that get under the skin. She "writes precisely without making a fetish of precision."

### Sample 5 — Theodore Dreiser (Work X: Structure)
**Source**: *An American Tragedy* (1925), Book III trial sections
**Origin**: Analysis and critical excerpts

The trial in *An American Tragedy* features prosecutor Orville Mason described as "a fox hound within the last leap of its kill" — a term-limited DA who needs a big conviction to position himself for a judgeship. Mason manipulates the courtroom through:
- Exploiting public sympathy by having Roberta's letters read aloud in court and published in newspapers
- Fabricating the strength of circumstantial evidence
- Coaching witnesses to maximize emotional impact
- Defense attorneys who dress and coach Clyde to appear innocent, creating a counter-theater

The system itself is the antagonist. Clyde's lawyers "style Clyde and give him pointers on how to behave as if he is innocent when he is not." The prosecution "manipulates and fabricates evidence to make their circumstantial case appear stronger than it actually is." The outcome is predetermined by political ambition, public opinion, and class prejudice.

> Indeed, his effect on her was electric — thrilling — arousing a sense of what it was to want and not to have.

Dreiser's signature: long, nested clauses building institutional weight. The prose accumulates bureaucratic detail until the individual is buried beneath the system's paperwork.

**Why representative**: Structure is the weapon. Dreiser builds a complete picture of the trial — courtroom theater and backstage manipulation running simultaneously. The reader sees justice as performance, outcome as foregone conclusion. This structural duality — what happens in the room vs. what happens behind the scenes — is the engine for our story.

### Sample 6 — John Grisham (Work Y: Themes)
**Source**: *A Time to Kill* (1989), courtroom and thematic passages
**Origin**: Goodreads collected quotes

> The system reflects society. It's not always fair, but it's as fair as biased, emotional humans can make it.

> And until we can see each other as equals, justice is never going to be even-handed.

> Make friends with fear, Lucien always said, because it will not go away, and it will destroy you.

> With murder, the victim is gone... But rape is much worse. The victim has a lifetime of coping...

**Why representative**: Grisham frames the courtroom as a moral arena where the gap between law and justice becomes visible. His lawyer-protagonists are not cynics — they believe in the system enough to be devastated when it fails. The tension is between legal procedure and moral truth: what is lawful vs. what is right. Jake Brigance's climactic jury address asks jurors to reverse racial roles — to feel justice rather than apply statute. This is the thematic engine: a legal professional who discovers that excellence within the system cannot overcome the system's willingness to protect power.

### 2 Selected Concepts

**Selection 1: Fillet (redaction)** — The 18th-century practice of censoring names by cutting out central letters and replacing them with dashes (e.g., W——m P——t for William Pitt). Used in publications to communicate information while avoiding libel suits. The word "fillet" itself means to remove the bones — to strip the skeleton from the flesh.

**Integration**: The protagonist, assembling her record of what happened after she leaves the state attorney's office, uses a version of this technique in her private reconstruction. She cannot name the state attorney directly in what she writes — she doesn't know yet if this is a legal document, a letter, a confession, or a weapon. So she refers to him in her notes by title only, or by initials, or by dash-marks: the filleted name. This becomes a structural motif — the man who destroyed her case is present throughout the text but never fully named, the way his sabotage was present throughout the grand jury proceeding but never fully visible. The reader knows who he is, just as 18th-century readers knew who W——m P——t was. The redaction is a fiction that preserves the form of discretion while communicating everything.

**Selection 2: Graphisurus despectus (salt-and-pepper long-horned beetle)** — A beetle that mines just beneath the bark of hickory trees, feeding on dead wood from the trunk and main branches. It works below the surface, invisible, consuming the structure from inside without breaking the exterior.

**Integration**: This becomes a metaphor that the protagonist uses once — sparingly — in a moment when she's describing what the state attorney did to her case. He didn't attack it from outside. He didn't refuse to file it. He took it to the grand jury and hollowed it from within, the way a beetle mines beneath bark: the tree looks intact from the outside, but the structure is consumed. She knows this image because she saw it on a nature documentary with her daughter, or because she drove past a dead hickory tree that looked healthy until it fell across a road. The detail is grounded and personal, not literary. It's the kind of image a person uses when they're trying to explain something that language built for courtrooms cannot hold.

## Author Meeting Key Extractions

### Central Tensions
1. **Ellroy vs. Abbott on what the story is about**: Ellroy insists it's about the paper — the documents, the filing cabinet, the material record. Abbott insists it's about the woman — her body in that office for fourteen months, translating screaming into syntax. Resolution: the story must hold both simultaneously.

2. **The hallway image**: Abbott's defining image — the prosecutor standing in the hallway outside the grand jury room, listening through the wall as her boss presents her case wrong. She can hear the tone of voices but not the words. She hears when a girl testifies because the voice goes higher, thinner, younger. This is the story's central scene.

3. **Omission as violence**: Ellroy's insight — the state attorney doesn't need to revise the evidence. He simply doesn't introduce it. The receipts exist in a drawer four floors below the grand jury. "Not revision. Omission."

4. **The girls reclassified**: Abbott — the system "solicits their pain and then reclassifies it." The state attorney reframes victims as participants, harm as transaction, coercion as consent. Each link in the logic chain is a lie.

5. **Badge-in timestamps as narrative**: Ellroy — the institutional record of her devotion. Fourteen months of 6:47 AM arrivals and 11:15 PM departures. The system tracked her while she built a case it had already decided would fail.

6. **Does she take the documents?**: Ellroy's question the story must answer. Abbott's counter: whether taking them changes anything, because "proof was never the problem."

7. **The delusion of excellence**: Abbott — "Is that integrity or is that the particular delusion of women who believe that excellence will protect them?" The belief that the quality of the work matters is "the thing the system exploits most efficiently."

8. **The state attorney is a man**: Ellroy — "People always want to say the system did this. The system didn't do anything. A man walked into a room and asked a sixteen-year-old girl whether she was paid for her time. A man. With a mouth. Making a choice."

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

A female assistant state attorney in northeast Florida spent fourteen months building a 60-count indictment against a trafficking predator — interviewing underage victims, compiling financial records, tracing motel receipts along I-95. Her boss, the elected state attorney, took her case to the grand jury and deliberately sabotaged it: calling the victims prostitutes, declining to introduce financial evidence, presenting fourteen months of work as if the state had nothing. Three months after the no-bill, she walks out of the office. Now she sits in a rented room with a banker's box of copied documents, writing down what happened — not sure yet whether she's building a federal referral, a press leak, or her own private autopsy of a case the system murdered from inside.

### 2. Protagonist

**Name**: Leigh Dorlan
**Age**: 34
**Situation**: Former assistant state attorney, Fourth Judicial Circuit (Jacksonville area), now unemployed, three months out from quitting. Living in a rented efficiency apartment. Has a seven-year-old daughter (shared custody with ex-husband). Drives a 2019 Honda Civic with 127,000 miles on it. Currently writing in a room she pays for weekly, surrounded by copied case files she took from the office.

**What she WANTS**: To make the documents matter — to find some venue, some authority, some reader who will see what she built and understand that the no-bill was manufactured. She wants the case to exist somewhere outside the filing cabinet where it was buried.

**What she NEEDS**: To accept that the documents were never going to save those girls, and that her fourteen months of work served the system's theater of due diligence more than it served justice — and then to act anyway, not from faith in the process but from something harder and less clean than faith.

**Her FLAW**: She believes in the evidentiary standard. Not abstractly — bodily. She built her career on the premise that if the evidence is strong enough, the system will follow the evidence. This belief kept her at her desk until 11 PM for fourteen months. It kept her sitting across from traumatized teenagers asking them to trust a process that had already classified them as expendable. Her flaw is not naivete; it's that her competence makes her the system's most efficient instrument. She is so good at the work that the work becomes the trap.

**Her WOUND (specific)**: During the grand jury week, she stood in the fourth-floor hallway and listened through the wall while her boss questioned a sixteen-year-old named Amber Sutcliffe. She heard Amber's voice — thin, defensive, the pitch of a girl trying to sound older than she is — and she heard her boss ask whether Amber was "compensated for her time." She had spent four sessions with Amber over three months. She had promised Amber, in words she now cannot un-say, that the state would act on what Amber told her. She cannot hear the word "compensated" without tasting bile.

### 3. Structure

**Opening Image** (~800 words): Leigh in the rented room. The banker's box on the table. She's sorting documents — not reading them, sorting. Organizing by type: phone logs, motel receipts, financial transfers, witness statements. The room is described in Ellroy's institutional-decay register: fluorescent light, particleboard desk, carpet that smells like carpet cleaner which smells worse than what it's covering. She has a legal pad. She's writing headings. This is not a narrative yet — it's taxonomy. She is still operating in the mode the office trained her in: organize, categorize, present. The Ellroy voice dominates here — staccato, document-focused, the rhythm of case-file assembly.

**Inciting Disruption** (~1,000 words): She finds a document she doesn't remember copying — or maybe she does remember, but the memory is different now. It's page 47 of the original indictment, the count addressing Amber Sutcliffe specifically. She reads her own language from fourteen months ago: clinical, precise, legally structured. And she realizes she can hear two voices in the document — the voice she used to write it (detached, prosecutorial, third-person) and the voice Amber used to tell her what happened (present-tense, broken, the syntax of a girl describing things that happened to her body). The Abbott voice enters here — body-aware, insinuating, the gap between institutional language and lived experience. Leigh begins writing not as a lawyer but as a witness. She starts to reconstruct the grand jury week.

**Escalating Middle** (~2,000 words): Alternating between the documents and Leigh's reconstruction of the grand jury week. We see the case she built — in document language, Ellroy-staccato, financial records and phone logs and timestamps — and we see the case her boss presented, reconstructed from what she heard through the hallway wall and what she pieced together afterward from the grand jurors' body language when they filed out. The filleted name technique: she refers to the state attorney as "the SA" or "D——" throughout her written account. We learn the political context: the defendant's family has connections to the county's development community; the state attorney's next campaign depends on that community's support. The middle escalates through the specific victims — not all thirty-one, but three: Amber (16, the one Leigh is haunted by), a girl identified only by case number who recanted before the grand jury, and a girl who was never called to testify because the SA decided her prior drug arrest made her "non-credible." The middle section uses the beetle metaphor once — Leigh trying to explain to herself how a case can be destroyed without anyone visibly attacking it. The bark looks intact. The tree falls.

**Crisis Point** (~800 words): Leigh finishes her reconstruction and reads it back. She has fifty-three pages of handwritten legal pad. She has organized the evidence. She has documented the sabotage. And she realizes that what she's written is not a federal referral — it's too personal, too angry, too full of the hallway and the voices through the wall. It's also not a press leak — it names victims, it would expose the girls to public scrutiny the system already subjected them to. And it's not a confession — she didn't do anything wrong. She built the case right. The case was right. She sits with the knowledge that the document she's created is a thing that has no venue. There is no office to badge into, no grand jury to present to, no judge to file with. She has assembled proof of something the system is designed to make unprovable: not that a crime was committed, but that the mechanism designed to address the crime was operated in reverse.

**Ending** (~600 words): Leigh calls Amber Sutcliffe's cell phone. Not from the legal pad, not as a former prosecutor. The conversation is brief and does not go well. Amber does not want to hear from her. Amber says something that Leigh will not write down. Leigh hangs up. She looks at the banker's box. She looks at the legal pad. She picks up a motel receipt — a Days Inn on I-95, room paid in cash, November 14th — and she puts it in her wallet. Not the whole box. One receipt. Then she calls a reporter she knew from her days working drug cases. She doesn't tell the reporter what she has. She asks whether the reporter would be interested in meeting. The reporter says yes. The story ends with Leigh driving — no destination named, the receipt in her wallet, the box still on the table in the rented room. The ending does NOT resolve: we don't know if the reporter will take it, if the feds will act, if the documents will matter. What we know is that Leigh has stopped organizing and started moving. One receipt. A phone number. The action is small, incomplete, and real.

### 4. Key Scenes (each does at least two things)

1. **Leigh sorting documents in the rented room** (Opening): Establishes the Ellroy voice AND reveals character — she is still doing the job even after leaving the office. Sorting is her coping mechanism and her prison. The room's institutional decay mirrors the system she left.

2. **Reading the Amber Sutcliffe count and hearing two voices in it** (Inciting Disruption): Introduces the Abbott voice (body, female experience, the cost of translation) AND cracks open the narrative form — Leigh shifts from legal taxonomy to personal reconstruction, which is the story's structural pivot.

3. **The hallway scene — listening through the wall during Amber's grand jury testimony** (Escalating Middle, central set piece): Delivers the story's most visceral sequence AND dramatizes the Dreiser structure — the predetermined outcome, the theater of justice, the individual crushed by the institution. Leigh hears her boss say "compensated." She hears Amber's voice go thin. She cannot intervene. The wall is literal and institutional.

4. **Leigh reading her own reconstruction and realizing it has no venue** (Crisis): Forces the protagonist to confront the Abbott theme — excellence within the system does not override the system — AND dramatizes the Grisham gap between law and justice. The document is proof. Proof was never the problem.

5. **The phone call to Amber, then the single receipt in the wallet** (Ending): Leigh acts, and the action costs her (Amber's rejection) AND refuses to resolve — the Grisham theme of a legal professional confronting system failure becomes concrete. She doesn't take the whole box. She takes one receipt. The gap between what she has and what she can do with it is the story's final image.

### 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Reader starts**: Clinical detachment — watching a competent woman do competent work. The documents feel authoritative, organized, safe. The reader trusts Leigh's process the way the grand jurors should have trusted the evidence.

**Reader moves through**: Growing dread — as the middle section reveals the sabotage, the reader experiences the same sickening realization Leigh had: the system was not failing; the system was working as designed. The hallway scene is the point of maximum visceral discomfort — the reader is trapped with Leigh, hearing muffled voices, unable to intervene.

**Reader arrives at**: Something between rage and grief that has no clean name. Leigh's reconstruction is both a feat of documentation and a futile gesture. The phone call to Amber — Amber's refusal to engage — lands like a slap. The single receipt in the wallet is not triumph, not defeat. It's a woman making the smallest possible move in the direction of action because the alternative is to keep sorting papers in a rented room forever.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Ellroy (Author A) concentrates in**: The opening section and all document-facing passages. The staccato rhythm, compound nouns, telegraphic syntax. Particularly in: the timestamp sequences (badge-in 6:47, badge-out 11:15), the financial records (wire transfers, motel receipts, phone logs), and Leigh's internal monologue when she's in case-assembly mode. Ellroy owns the institutional voice — the system's own language, turned against it. The filleted name of the state attorney (D——) is an Ellroy device: the redacted document as evidence of what the system hides.

**Abbott (Author B) concentrates in**: Every passage involving the girls' bodies and Leigh's body in institutional space. The moment she reads her own indictment and hears Amber's voice underneath the legal syntax. The hallway scene — listening through the wall, the physical sensation of helplessness. Abbott owns the passages where institutional language fails and the body's knowledge takes over: Leigh tasting bile at the word "compensated," the way her hand shakes when she writes Amber's name, the girls' skin and voices and the particular posture of teenagers testifying about things done to their bodies. Abbott also owns Leigh's self-interrogation: is this integrity or delusion?

**Dreiser / An American Tragedy (Work X) concentrates in**: The structure of the middle section — the dual narrative of the case Leigh built vs. the case the SA presented. Two stories occupying the same space, the same evidence, the same grand jury room. The predetermined outcome: the SA read the indictment, understood what prosecution would cost him politically, and the case was dead from that moment. Everything after — the interviews, the evidence collection, the grand jury presentation — was theater. Leigh was the only person in the building who didn't know she was in a play. This structural irony — the mechanism of justice operated in reverse — is Dreiser's contribution.

**Grisham / A Time to Kill (Work Y) concentrates in**: The thematic spine — the gap between law and justice. Leigh is Jake Brigance without the jury, without the closing argument, without the verdict. She built the case right. The evidence was right. The system declined to use it correctly. The moral arena of the courtroom became a stage for institutional self-protection. The Grisham theme surfaces most sharply at the crisis point: Leigh's reconstruction is a brief she cannot file, an argument she cannot make, addressed to a court that does not exist. And in the ending: the reporter, the phone call, the attempt to find some venue — any venue — where evidence can mean what evidence means.

### 7. Title

**Sixty Counts**

Rationale: Direct, numeric, institutional — Ellroy's influence in the title form. Refers to the number of counts in the original indictment that the state attorney sabotaged. Does not start with "The." Does not duplicate existing title structures (no "of" construction, no compound-with-and, no gerund). The number is specific and concrete — it sounds like a legal document, which is what the story is about. It also carries the ghost of a different meaning: sixty accusations the state chose not to speak, sixty charges that were written and then silenced, sixty times the word "guilty" was warranted and withheld.

## Summary for Writer Agent

Write a 5,200-word crime-noir story in fictionalized true-crime mode. Protagonist Leigh Dorlan, 34, former assistant state attorney, sits in a rented room reconstructing how her boss sabotaged a 60-count trafficking indictment she built over fourteen months. Structure alternates between document-focused passages (Ellroy staccato) and body-aware reconstruction (Abbott insinuation). The grand jury hallway scene — Leigh listening through the wall while her boss calls underage victims prostitutes — is the story's center of gravity. The ending is one receipt in a wallet, one phone call to a reporter, no resolution. The story's question is not whether justice will be done — it won't — but whether a woman who believed in the evidentiary standard can act after that belief has been demolished.
