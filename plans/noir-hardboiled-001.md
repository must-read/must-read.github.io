## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Walter Mosley — *Devil in a Blue Dress* (AuthorB)
**Origin**: Opening chapter, published 1990
**Why representative**: Establishes Mosley's first-person noir voice — conversational, warm, observant of racial dynamics, grounded in the specific textures of Black LA life.

> I was surprised to see a white man walk into Joppy's bar. It's not just that he was white but he wore an off-white linen suit and shirt with a Panama straw hat and bone shoes over flashing white silk socks. His skin was smooth and pale with just a few freckles. One lick of strawberry-blond hair escaped the band of his hat. He stopped in the doorway, filling it with his large frame, and surveyed the room with pale eyes; not a color I'd ever seen in a man's eyes. When he looked at me I felt a thrill of fear, but that went away quickly because I was used to white people by 1948.
>
> I had spent five years with white men, and women, from Africa to Italy, through Paris, and into the Fatherland itself. I ate with them and slept with them, and I killed enough blue-eyed young men to know that they were just as afraid to die as I was.

Key stylistic features: First-person past tense, conversational but precise, racial awareness woven into observation, physical detail used to establish power dynamics, war as backstory that shapes worldview, warmth and wariness coexisting.

### Sample 2: Dashiell Hammett — *The Maltese Falcon* (WorkX)
**Origin**: Opening paragraph, Chapter 1 "Spade & Archer"
**Why representative**: Hammett's objective, camera-eye style — all surface, no interiority, the detective described as a physical object rather than a consciousness.

> Samuel Spade's jaw was long and bony, his chin a jutting V under the more flexible V of his mouth. His nostrils curved back to make another, smaller, v. His yellow-grey eyes were horizontal. The V motif was picked up again by thickish brows rising outward from twin creases above a hooked nose, and his pale brown hair grew down from high flat temples in a point on his forehead. He looked rather pleasantly like a blond Satan.

Key stylistic features: External description only, geometric/angular language, no emotional interiority, clinical precision, the detective as mask. The entire novel works this way — Spade is known only through action and dialogue, never thought. The "client is never who they seem" structure: Miss Wonderly arrives, lies about her name, her sister, her situation. Every client in the novel is performing a role.

### Sample 3: Raymond Chandler — *The Long Goodbye* (WorkY)
**Origin**: Opening lines, Chapter 1
**Why representative**: Chandler's lyrical first-person, the detective as observer of beauty and ruin, the initial meeting with Terry Lennox that seeds the entire novel's emotional architecture.

> The first time I laid eyes on Terry Lennox he was drunk in a Rolls-Royce Silver Wraith outside the terrace of The Dancers. The parking lot attendant had brought the car out and he was still holding the door open because Terry Lennox's left foot was still dangling outside, as if he had forgotten he had one. He had a young-looking face but his hair was bone white. You could tell by his eyes that he was plastered to the hairline, but otherwise he looked like any other nice young guy in a dinner jacket who had been spending too much money in a joint that exists for that purpose and for no other. There was a girl beside him. Her hair was a lovely shade of dark red and she had a distant smile on her lips and over her shoulders she had a blue mink that almost made the Rolls-Royce look like just another automobile.

Key stylistic features: Long, rhythmic sentences that accumulate detail; the detective's eye drawn to beauty even in degradation; wealth as setting; the friend introduced as already broken; LA as a place where money and loneliness coexist; melancholy laced through every observation.

### Sample 4: Jim Thompson — Prose Style (AuthorA)
**Origin**: Composite description based on *The Killer Inside Me*, *Pop. 1280*, and critical analysis
**Why representative**: Thompson's narrators are cheerful monsters — conversational, folksy, confiding in the reader while concealing the knife. The first person is intimate and poisoned.

Thompson's signature voice works through several techniques:
- **Folksy confession**: The narrator speaks to you like a friend, using colloquial language, small-town wisdom, cliches that feel deliberate. Lou Ford in *The Killer Inside Me* sounds like a pleasant deputy sheriff dispensing homespun advice — until the violence erupts and the voice doesn't change register at all. That flatness is the horror.
- **Cheerful nihilism**: His characters observe their own moral collapse with something approaching amusement. Nick Corey in *Pop. 1280* watches himself commit murder and muses on it the way you'd muse on a bad meal. The grin Thompson's characters wear is the most honest thing about them — it acknowledges that the distance between what people say and what they do is the funniest joke in the world.
- **The swerve**: Thompson builds a comfortable, almost cozy narrative voice and then detonates it. The violence is sudden, graphic, and delivered in the same conversational tone as the setup. No shift in register. No dramatic music. Just the same friendly voice telling you something terrible.
- **Interior rot**: Unlike Hammett's exteriors-only approach, Thompson lives inside his narrators' skulls. You see every justification, every self-deception, every moment where the narrator knows he's lying to himself and does it anyway, and the reader is complicit because the voice is so warm.

**For the writer**: The Thompson influence in this story should manifest as moments where our narrator's self-knowledge curdles — where he sees something about himself clearly and then narrates past it with a smoothness that should make the reader uneasy. Not a psychopathic narrator, but a man who occasionally glimpses the psychopath's logic and finds it... recognizable.

### Selected Concepts (2):

**1. Kastle-Meyer Test (phenolphthalein blood detection)**
Integration: Our detective, Nate Garvey, is a former crime scene technician before becoming a PI. He still carries a small kit with phenolphthalein reagent and hydrogen peroxide — a habit from his LAPD days. In a key scene, he uses this test on a stain in his friend's car, and the swab turns bright pink. The specificity of the test — how it works, what the pink means, the fact that it can't distinguish human blood from animal blood — becomes a metaphor for the story's central problem: presumptive evidence. Everything points to guilt, but nothing confirms it absolutely. The test gives you pink, not proof. And Nate has to decide what to do with pink.

**2. Watts Towers (Simon Rodia's 33-year construction project)**
Integration: The Watts Towers serve as a recurring image and touchstone. Nate and his old friend Emmett used to walk past them in the early days of Rodia's construction — two young men watching an old Italian immigrant build something impossible out of scrap metal, bottle caps, and broken tiles. The Towers become a metaphor for what Emmett was trying to build (a legitimate life, a business, something that would last) and what Nate was trying to build (a reputation, a name, something clean). By the time of the story, Rodia has finished the Towers and walked away, leaving them to the neighborhood. Emmett's construction has also reached its completion — but what he built is not what Nate thought it was.

## 1. Premise

A Black private investigator in 1957 Los Angeles takes a case from his oldest friend — a man who says his business partner has disappeared with money they both need. But the friend is lying about what the money was for, and the missing partner isn't missing at all. As Nate Garvey peels back the layers, he discovers that the friend he's known for twenty years has been running a predatory lending operation that preys on Black families trying to buy homes in newly desegregated neighborhoods — and the "missing" partner is the one person who tried to stop it.

The engine: What do you owe a friend who turns out to be the thing you've spent your career fighting?

---

## 2. Protagonist

**Nathaniel "Nate" Garvey**, 42, Black private investigator working out of a two-room office on South Broadway, between a barbershop and a tailor who's been closing for three years. Korean War veteran (served with the 24th Infantry Regiment, one of the last segregated units). Former LAPD crime scene technician — quit in 1953 after watching the department bury the investigation of a beating that left a man blind. Kept his forensic kit. Got his PI license.

- **What he WANTS**: To find Emmett's missing partner and the money, to help his friend, to repay a debt of loyalty that goes back to 1935 when Emmett's mother fed him during a winter when his own mother was too sick to work.
- **What he NEEDS**: To accept that loyalty to a person is not the same as loyalty to who that person used to be. The Emmett he loves existed twenty years ago. The Emmett sitting in his office is someone else wearing the same face.
- **His flaw/blind spot**: Nate believes that knowing someone a long time means knowing them. He reads strangers with perfect clarity — it's his job — but with Emmett, he substitutes memory for observation. He sees the teenager who shared his last orange, not the man who's been feeding families into a machine.

## 3. Structure

### Opening Image/Scene (The Hook)
Nate is in his office at 7 PM, cleaning his phenolphthalein kit — the small glass vials, the hydrogen peroxide, the cotton swabs. A ritual from his LAPD days. He's methodical, calm. The phone rings. It's Emmett Rawlings, and his voice is wrong. Not scared exactly — Nate knows Emmett's scared voice — but controlled in a way that means he's already decided what to say and is working to keep the other words locked inside. Emmett asks to meet. Not at the office. At Rodia's place — the Watts Towers.

### The Inciting Disruption
Emmett tells Nate his business partner, a man named Calvin Oates, has disappeared with $14,000 from their real estate development company. Emmett is terrified — not of the money, he says, but because people are counting on that money to close on houses. He's been helping Black families buy homes in Compton, newly opened after the restrictive covenants fell. If the money's gone, six families lose their deposits, their credit, their chance. Emmett needs Nate to find Calvin, and he needs Nate — not the police — because Calvin has a record and the police won't look for a Black man with a record. They'll assume he ran and close the file.

Nate takes the case. Not because the facts add up, but because it's Emmett. Because the alternative is saying: I see through you, and I'm choosing not to help.

### The Escalating Middle
Nate's investigation reveals layers of deception:

**First layer**: Calvin Oates didn't disappear. He's been staying at a rooming house in Pacoima, and he's not hiding from Emmett — he's hiding from the people Emmett introduced him to. Calvin is bruised, jumpy, and when Nate finds him, his first words are: "He sent you? God damn it, he sent you."

**Second layer**: The $14,000 wasn't for closing costs. Emmett's "development company" is a front for a predatory lending scheme. He's been buying distressed properties in transitioning neighborhoods, selling them to Black families on installment contracts at inflated prices with balloon payments. When the families can't make the balloon, Emmett forecloses, keeps the down payment, and resells the property. It's legal. It's the same thing white land speculators have been doing for decades. Emmett is just doing it to his own people.

**Third layer**: Calvin discovered the scheme, threatened to go to the authorities, and was beaten by two men Emmett hired. The $14,000 is real — but it's the accumulated down payments from families who are about to lose everything.

Through these discoveries, Nate interviews tenants, visits county records, examines documents Calvin has saved. The world of South LA opens up — the oil refineries visible from Compton backyards, the new freeways carving through neighborhoods, the hope and the vultures circling the hope.

### The Crisis Point
Nate goes to Emmett's house. A nice house, nicer than Nate expected — in a neighborhood that Nate now understands was paid for by the scheme. They sit across from each other. Emmett knows that Nate knows. And the conversation is the one from the author meeting — not a confession, not a negotiation, but a goodbye that neither man wants to say.

Emmett's defense: "Every white bank in this city turned them down. I gave them a chance to own something. Yes, the terms are hard. The terms are always hard. You think the banks that redlined these people for thirty years gave them better terms? I'm the only one who said yes."

The terrible thing is that Emmett is partially right. The system is rigged, and Emmett exploited the rigging from the inside. He became the machine because the machine was already eating people, and at least this way, he got to eat too.

Nate has to decide: protect his friend (who is also his past, his youth, the person who kept him alive), or protect the families Emmett is devouring.

### The Ending
Nate gives Calvin's evidence to a lawyer he knows — not the police, because the police don't prosecute Black-on-Black financial crime, and not the newspapers, because the story would be used to discredit Black homeownership itself. A quiet legal action. The families get their money back. Emmett's scheme collapses.

But Nate doesn't tell Emmett it was him. He lets Emmett believe Calvin went to the lawyer on his own. This is the cowardice at the heart of Nate's decency — he does the right thing, but he can't face being the one who did it. He can't be the friend who destroyed a friend.

Final image: Nate drives past the Watts Towers at dusk. Rodia finished them three years ago and walked away — gave the property to a neighbor and left for Martinez, California. Never came back. The Towers stand there, 99 feet of scrap metal and broken tile, built by a man who wanted to do something big and did it and then left. Nate thinks about Emmett. About the house Emmett built with other people's deposits. About the Towers, built with salvage. About what lasts. He doesn't have an answer. He drives home.

**What's left open**: Whether Emmett will figure out it was Nate. Whether the friendship is over or just wounded. Whether Nate's choice to hide his role was mercy or cowardice or both.

## 4. Key Scenes

1. **The Watts Towers meeting** (opening act): Nate and Emmett stand in the shadow of the Towers at twilight. Emmett delivers his lie about Calvin and the money. The Towers loom — built from nothing, built from salvage, built over 33 years by a man who just wanted to make something. This scene establishes the friendship (they used to watch Rodia work as teenagers), the case, and the central image. It does double duty: advances plot (Nate takes the case) and reveals character (Nate sees the lie but takes the case anyway because of who's asking).

2. **Finding Calvin in Pacoima** (midpoint): Nate locates Calvin in a rooming house on a dirt street with no sidewalks. Calvin is bruised, drinking, and furious. "He sent you? God damn it, he sent you." This scene flips the case — the missing man isn't missing, the money isn't what Nate thought, and Emmett's story collapses. Calvin shows Nate the installment contracts. The scene does triple duty: plot reversal, character revelation (Calvin as moral center), and world-building (Pacoima's substandard housing, the specificity of predatory lending documents).

3. **The phenolphthalein test** (investigation sequence): Nate examines Emmett's car while Emmett is at work. He finds a dark stain on the trunk lining. He runs the Kastle-Meyer test — swab, reagent, hydrogen peroxide. The cotton turns bright pink. Presumptive positive for blood. Calvin's blood, almost certainly, from when Emmett's hired men beat him. But the test can't confirm it's human blood, let alone whose. Presumptive. Not proof. Everything in this case is presumptive. This scene advances plot (evidence of violence) and serves as metaphor (the whole story is about presumptive knowledge — what Nate presumes about Emmett vs. what he can prove).

4. **The confrontation at Emmett's house** (crisis): The conversation that the whole story has been building toward. Nate and Emmett, sitting in Emmett's living room with its good furniture, knowing the truth but dancing around it. Emmett's justification — that he gave people a chance the banks wouldn't, that the terms are hard because the world is hard. Nate's inability to argue because Emmett is partially right, and partially right is the most dangerous kind of wrong. This scene is character-as-action: Nate's silence, his failure to accuse, his quiet decision to act through intermediaries rather than directly — all of it reveals the flaw in his loyalty.

5. **Driving past the Towers** (closing image): Nate alone in his car. The Towers in fading light. Rodia is gone — finished his work and walked away. Nate wonders if that's strength or abandonment. He wonders if what he did to Emmett was either of those things. The car keeps moving. The scene closes the emotional arc without resolving it.

---

## 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning**: The reader feels warmth and competence. Nate is good at his job, steady, the kind of man you'd trust with a problem. There's comfort in his ritual with the forensic kit, his orderly office, his relationship with the city. The reader settles in with a professional.

**Middle**: The warmth curdles. As the case unfolds, the reader feels the same discomfort Nate feels — the growing certainty that the friend is rotten, overlaid with the desperate wish that it's not true. The reader wants Nate to be wrong. The Pacoima scene — Calvin's bruises, his fear, the stacked installment contracts — produces something like nausea. Not at the violence, but at the banality of the scheme. Emmett isn't a monster. He's an entrepreneur. That's worse.

**Crisis**: The reader feels the weight of an impossible choice. Emmett's argument is real — the system is rigged, and his exploitation is a mirror of what white institutions have done for decades. The reader should feel the gravitational pull of Emmett's logic even while recognizing its poison.

**End**: The reader should feel something like the ache Mosley described in the author meeting — not grief exactly, but the dull recognition that doing the right thing and doing the brave thing were not the same thing, and Nate did the right thing and failed at the brave thing, and he'll have to live with that. The final image should leave the reader with a sense of endurance rather than resolution — the city goes on, the Towers stand, the car keeps moving.

## 6. Formula Integration

### AuthorA — Jim Thompson (Psychopathic first-person narrators; cheerful nihilism)
**Key passage**: The confrontation at Emmett's house. When Emmett makes his argument — "I gave them a chance" — Nate's interior narration should slip into Thompson territory. For a moment, Nate sees Emmett's logic with perfect, terrible clarity. He almost admires it. The system eats people, and Emmett chose to be the teeth instead of the meat. There's a cold rationality to that, and Nate — who left the LAPD because he couldn't stomach the violence — recognizes in himself a flicker of the same logic. He could have stayed. Could have been the teeth. He chose not to, but the choice wasn't as clean as he tells himself. This is Thompson's influence: the moment when the narrator glimpses the psychopath's reasoning and finds it... recognizable. Not sympathetic. Recognizable. The grin behind the good man's face.

### AuthorB — Walter Mosley (Easy Rawlins voice; Black LA; humanistic noir)
**Key passage**: The opening scene at the Watts Towers and throughout the narrative voice. The entire first-person narration should carry Mosley's rhythms — conversational, warm, observant of racial dynamics without being didactic. Nate notices who's in which neighborhood and why. He reads white people the way Easy reads them — with the practiced clarity of a man who learned early that understanding the powerful is a survival skill. The warmth is in the details: the barbershop next to his office, the way he describes food, the affection for the city even as he catalogues its cruelties. LA should feel like a place someone lives, not a metaphor.

### WorkX — *The Maltese Falcon* by Dashiell Hammett (The client is never who they seem)
**Key passage**: The entire case structure. Like Brigid O'Shaughnessy walking into Spade's office with a false name and a false story, Emmett walks into Nate's life with a false case. The "missing partner" isn't missing. The "stolen money" was never what Emmett said it was. Each layer of investigation peels back another lie. The Hammett structure is the skeleton: detective takes case, case is a lie, real case emerges underneath, detective must decide what to do with the truth. The difference from Hammett: Spade could hand the Falcon over to the police because he didn't love Brigid. Nate can't hand Emmett over because he does love Emmett, or loves who Emmett was.

### WorkY — *The Long Goodbye* by Raymond Chandler (Male friendship and betrayal; loneliness; LA as character)
**Key passage**: The final confrontation and the ending. The entire emotional architecture is Chandler's: a detective who takes damage for a friend, discovers the friend is smaller than he needed him to be, and walks away changed. Marlowe and Lennox's last meeting — "You're a moral defeatist" — echoes in Nate and Emmett's living room conversation. But where Marlowe walks away clean (as Mosley noted in the author meeting), Nate walks away dirty. He did the right thing through a back door, and he knows it. The loneliness at the end is Chandler's loneliness — the detective in his car, the city around him, nobody to go home to who would understand what just happened. LA as character: the freeways cutting through neighborhoods, the oil smell from the refineries, the heat that makes the steering wheel too hot to grip.

## 7. Title

**"Presumptive Pink"**

Reasoning: Avoids starting with "The" (contributing to getting the platform below 30%). The title references the Kastle-Meyer test — the phenolphthalein reagent that turns pink in the presumptive presence of blood. "Presumptive" carries the story's central problem: everything in Nate's investigation is presumptive. He presumes he knows Emmett. He presumes the case is what Emmett says. The test tells him there's blood, but not whose, not why. The whole story is about what you do with evidence that's almost proof but isn't. "Pink" is unexpected in a noir title — a color associated with softness, with flesh, with the raw underside of things. It's also the color of the cotton swab after the test. A small, specific, disturbing detail that carries more weight than it should.

## Key Researched Details for the Writer

1. **The Kastle-Meyer Test**: Phenolphthalein reagent + hydrogen peroxide on a cotton swab. If hemoglobin is present, the swab turns bright pink within 15 seconds. First described 1903. Cannot distinguish human from animal blood. The test is "presumptive" — it suggests blood, but isn't proof. A positive result means further testing is needed.

2. **Watts Towers**: Built by Simon "Sam" Rodia, an Italian immigrant tile mason, from 1921 to 1954 — 33 years. Seventeen interconnected structures, tallest is 99.5 feet. Built from scrap steel, wire mesh, mortar, broken tiles, bottle caps, shells, pottery shards. Neighborhood children brought him scraps. When asked why, Rodia said: "I wanted to do something big, and I did." He deeded the property to a neighbor in 1954 and moved to Martinez, CA. Never returned.

3. **Central Avenue, Los Angeles**: The heart of Black LA from 1920-1955. Jazz clubs — Club Alabam, the Dunbar Hotel, the Lincoln Theater. Dexter Gordon, Charles Mingus, Charlie Parker. Decline began when restrictive covenants were struck down (1948 Shelley v. Kraemer) and Black residents could live elsewhere. By 1957 (story's setting), Central Avenue was fading but still present.

4. **Compton's Demographic Shift**: After Shelley v. Kraemer (1948), Black families began buying homes in previously restricted neighborhoods. Compton changed "almost overnight" in 1952. By 1957, it was a major destination for Black homeownership. This is the context for Emmett's scheme — he's exploiting the transition, selling on installment contracts to families who can't get conventional mortgages.

5. **Installment Contracts (Contract for Deed)**: A predatory real estate practice where the "buyer" makes payments but the seller retains the deed. If the buyer misses a single payment, the seller can evict them and keep all previous payments. Widely used against Black buyers who couldn't get conventional mortgages. Legal, devastating, and widespread in the 1950s.

6. **Pacoima in the 1950s**: San Fernando Valley neighborhood. Dirt roads, no sidewalks, substandard housing. Many African Americans settled there during the Great Migration. A mix of older homes and blue-collar industry. Easily a place where a man like Calvin might go to ground — cheap, overlooked, off the beaten path.

7. **LAPD in the 1950s**: Under Chief William Parker — supposedly "professional" but brutal. Bloody Christmas 1951 (beating of prisoners). Known for ignoring Black-on-Black crime while policing Black neighborhoods aggressively. This is why Emmett tells Nate not to go to the police — and why Nate knows he's right about that, even if he's lying about everything else.

8. **Oil Refineries**: Visible from much of South LA. The El Segundo refinery (Standard Oil) processed 276,000 barrels daily. The smell of petroleum hung over neighborhoods, a constant reminder that industry's needs trumped residents' comfort. Good atmospheric detail — Nate can smell the refineries from Compton.

9. **Freeway Construction**: By 1957, the LA freeway system was expanding rapidly — 150+ miles per year. Communities were being bisected. The Harbor Freeway (110) was under construction through the 1950s, carving through Black neighborhoods. Good metaphor for institutional destruction that parallels Emmett's personal predation.
