# Story Plan: Fifteen Years of Noise

## Assignment

- **Genre**: creative-nonfiction
- **Subgenre**: nature-writing
- **Slug**: fifteen-years-of-noise
- **Target Word Count**: 5,400 words (acceptable range: 5,100-5,700)
- **Reading Time**: 22 minutes
- **Risk Card**: None assigned

### Style Sources

**Author A — Annie Dillard**: Mystical, nature-inflected prose; *Pilgrim at Tinker Creek*; attention as spiritual practice. Dillard's ability to render sustained attention to the natural world as a spiritual practice. Her prose makes patience feel like a form of intelligence.

**Author B — Denis Johnson**: Raw, hallucinatory prose; fractured consciousness; spiritual yearning amid degradation. Johnson's spare, hallucinatory prose and his gift for rendering altered states of consciousness. Fifteen years of listening to cosmic noise produces its own altered state.

**Work X — Pilgrim at Tinker Creek by Annie Dillard**: A meditation structured around a single place and sustained observation, where the subject is not the thing observed but the quality of attention itself. The radio telescope is the creek; the pulsars are the muskrats. Contemplative, recursive, building meaning through accumulation.

**Work Y — Contact by Carl Sagan**: The theme of listening for a signal from the cosmos and what the act of listening means for the listener. But where Sagan's signal is dramatic and transformative, THIS signal is statistical and ambiguous. The gravitational wave background is not a message — it is evidence of distant collisions. The universe is not speaking to us.

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Annie Dillard — *Pilgrim at Tinker Creek*, Chapter 2: "Seeing"

**Source**: Author A / Work X (Annie Dillard / *Pilgrim at Tinker Creek*)
**Origin**: Chapter 2: "Seeing" (HarperPerennial, 1974)
**Why representative**: This passage demonstrates Dillard's signature movement from precise physical observation to mystical apprehension — the way she renders seeing itself as the subject, not the thing seen. The penny metaphor establishes attention as a kind of grace.

> When I was six or seven years old, growing up in Pittsburgh, I used to take a precious penny of my own and hide it for someone else to find... I would take a piece of chalk, and, starting at either end of the block, draw huge arrows leading up to the penny from both directions. After I learned to write I labeled the arrows: SURPRISE AHEAD or MONEY THIS WAY. I was greatly excited, during all this arrow-drawing, at the thought of the first lucky passer-by who would receive in this way, regardless of merit, a free gift from the universe.

> It is possible, in deep space, to sail on solar wind. Light, be it particle or wave, has force: you rig a giant sail and go. The secret of seeing is to sail on solar wind. Hone and spread your spirit till you yourself are a sail, whetted, translucent, broadside to the merest puff.

> I saw the backyard cedar where the mourning doves roost charged and transfigured, each cell buzzing with flame. I stood on the grass with the lights in it, grass that was wholly fire, utterly focused and utterly dreamed. It was less like seeing than like being for the first time seen, knocked breathless by a powerful glance... I had been my whole life a bell, and never knew it until at that moment I was lifted and struck.

### Sample 2: Annie Dillard — "Total Eclipse"

**Source**: Author A (Annie Dillard)
**Origin**: "Total Eclipse," from *Teaching a Stone to Talk* (1982)
**Why representative**: Shows Dillard's ability to render extreme perceptual experience — the moment when observed reality becomes alien and consciousness strains at its edges. Critical model for writing the moment the signal crosses threshold.

> All those things for which we have no words are lost. The mind — the culture — has two little tools, grammar and lexicon: a decorated sand bucket and a matching shovel.

### Sample 3: Annie Dillard — "Living Like Weasels"

**Source**: Author A (Annie Dillard)
**Origin**: "Living Like Weasels," from *Teaching a Stone to Talk* (1982)

### Sample 4: Denis Johnson — "Car Crash While Hitchhiking" (*Jesus' Son*)

**Source**: Author B (Denis Johnson)
**Origin**: "Car Crash While Hitchhiking," from *Jesus' Son* (Farrar, Straus & Giroux, 1992)
**Why representative**: Johnson's fractured consciousness, the way intoxication and trauma produce a kind of hyper-clarity rather than confusion. His sentences alternate between crystalline observation and disorientation. Critical for rendering the altered state of fifteen years of listening.

> I rose up sopping wet from sleeping under the pouring rain, and something less than conscious, thanks to the first three of the people I've already named — the salesman and the Indian and the student — all of whom had given me drugs. At the head of the entrance ramp I waited without hope of a ride. What was the point, even, of rolling up my sleeping bag when I was too wet to be let into anybody's car?

> My thoughts zoomed pitifully. The traveling salesman had fed me pills that made the linings of my veins feel scraped out. My jaw ached. I knew every raindrop by its name.

> He couldn't tell me what he was dreaming, and I couldn't tell him what was real.

> She was glorious, burning. She didn't know yet that her husband was dead. We knew. That's what gave her such power over us.

> My secret was that in this short while I had gone from being the president of this tragedy to being a faceless onlooker at a gory wreck.

### Sample 5: Denis Johnson — "Emergency" (*Jesus' Son*)

**Source**: Author B (Denis Johnson)
**Origin**: "Emergency," from *Jesus' Son* (Farrar, Straus & Giroux, 1992)
**Why representative**: Shows Johnson's ability to move between mundane institutional reality (the hospital) and hallucinatory perception. The emergency room is the model for the control room — institutional spaces where consciousness warps under repetition and chemical (or temporal) alteration.

> I'd been working in the emergency room for about three weeks, I guess. This was in 1973, before the summer ended. With nothing to do on the overnight shift but batch the insurance reports from the daytime shifts, I just started wandering around, over to the coronary-care unit, down to the cafeteria, et cetera, looking for Georgie, the orderly, a pretty good friend of mine.

> "What am I crying for?" he said. "Jesus. Wow, oh boy, perfect."

### Sample 6: Carl Sagan — *Contact* (themes and structure)

**Source**: Work Y (Carl Sagan / *Contact*)
**Origin**: *Contact* (Simon & Schuster, 1985) — summary and thematic analysis
**Why representative**: The signal-detection narrative and its emotional infrastructure. Sagan's Ellie Arroway listens to the cosmos and receives a clear, dramatic signal — prime numbers from Vega. This story INVERTS that: the signal is not a message, not dramatic, not clear. It is a statistical wobble that takes fifteen years to distinguish from noise. The listening is the same; the meaning is opposite.

Ellie Arroway, a radio astronomer working in Socorro, New Mexico, discovers a signal from the distant star Vega — a repeating sequence of the first 261 prime numbers, which could only come from an intelligent civilization. The signal is at 4.4623 gigahertz (the atomic weight of Hydrogen multiplied by Pi). Examination of the harmonics reveals a TV-like signal containing an image of Adolf Hitler — Earth's first strong broadcast reflected back. The message contains a blueprint for an advanced machine.

**Key inversion for this story**: Sagan's signal is designed, intentional, addressed to humanity. The NANOGrav signal is a side effect — supermassive black holes spiraling toward each other, spacetime shivering, pulsars wobbling. None of it is for us. None of it is about us. The protagonist stands in the path of something enormous and impersonal. This inversion is the thematic core.

### Sample 7: NANOGrav Technical Details (factual grounding)

**Source**: Scientific context for authenticity
**Origin**: NANOGrav 15-year data release, June 29, 2023
**Why representative**: The actual science and cautious language of the announcement — essential for the story's tone.

NANOGrav employed 68 pulsars in the Milky Way, tracking exact arrival times of radio pulses over a 15-year period. The technique detects gravitational waves through their effect on spacetime, which imprints timing variations on pulsar signals as waves pass through. Einstein's general relativity predicts gravitational-wave backgrounds should produce a distinctive pattern of correlated timing variations — the "Hellings-Downs" correlation pattern. Both Bayesian and frequentist statistical analyses confirmed this pattern was "preferred over other correlation patterns, which could be caused by systematic effects."

The source is inspiraling supermassive black hole binaries in the mass range of ~100 million to ~10 billion Solar masses. The announcement used the phrase "evidence consistent with" — not "we have detected." The error bars shrank over fifteen years until the bend in the data was distinguishable from noise.

Data collected using the Arecibo Observatory (collapsed 2020), the Green Bank Telescope, and the Very Large Array. Most sources observed approximately monthly. The National Radio Quiet Zone in Green Bank, West Virginia — 13,000 square miles where electromagnetic emissions are strictly regulated. No cell phones, no WiFi, no microwave ovens (or they sit in metal Faraday cages). Vehicles servicing the telescope are 1950s/1960s diesels because spark plugs in gas engines create interference.

### 2 Selected Concepts

**1. Paludinella minima (minute salt marsh snail)**
**Integration**: The protagonist keeps a collection of micromollusk shells on her desk at Green Bank — tiny, spiraling gastropod shells she finds on research trips to the coast, some smaller than a grain of rice. The *Paludinella minima* becomes a recurring physical detail: she holds one between thumb and forefinger while waiting for data to load, rolls it across the desk during long observation shifts. The logarithmic spiral of the shell mirrors the spiraling orbits of the supermassive black holes she is trying to detect. She once tried to explain to her ex-husband why the shells mattered and failed. The connection is not metaphorical — it is structural. The spiral is the shape of things falling toward each other across vast distances, whether across a tidal flat or across a galaxy cluster. This detail should feel like something only a real astronomer would have on her desk — so specific it must be true.

**2. Gayton Windmill, Norfolk (truncated tower mill)**
**Integration**: The protagonist spent a sabbatical year at Jodrell Bank in England early in her career, and during that year visited Gayton Windmill in Norfolk — an eight-storey tower mill that had been truncated to four storeys and converted to holiday accommodation. She returns to this image when thinking about what fifteen years has done to her: the mill was built to turn, to face the wind, to grind. Now it's a place people sleep for a weekend. Its machinery was removed, its cap was gone, but the walls were two feet thick and the thing still stood. She thinks of herself as a truncated tower — the top half removed, the essential purpose altered, but the walls thick enough that the structure held. This becomes a quiet, recurring image in sections about her personal life — the marriage that ended, the career ambitions that narrowed into a single project. The windmill never becomes a heavy-handed symbol; it's a place she went once, that she thinks about sometimes, that happens to be the right shape for what she's feeling.

## 1. Premise

A radio astronomer named Ruth Galloway has spent fifteen years observing millisecond pulsars from the Green Bank Telescope in West Virginia as part of the NANOGrav collaboration, measuring infinitesimal timing wobbles that might indicate gravitational waves permeating the galaxy. On the day the accumulated data finally crosses the statistical threshold — "evidence consistent with a gravitational-wave background" — she discovers that the moment she waited her entire career for feels like almost nothing at all. The essay asks: what does it cost to attend to something that will never attend to you, and is the cost distinguishable from the reward?

## 2. Protagonist

**Ruth Galloway**, 49 years old. Associate professor of physics at West Virginia University, stationed at the Green Bank Observatory. She joined the NANOGrav collaboration in 2008, fresh from a postdoc at Jodrell Bank in England. Divorced since 2017 — her husband, a high school biology teacher named Paul, didn't leave because of the work itself but because of what the work had done to her sense of time. ("You talk about next year the way other people talk about tomorrow," he said once, and she didn't understand why that was a complaint.) No children. She has a twelve-year-old Australian shepherd named Keppler with a bad hip.

- **What she WANTS**: Professional vindication. The signal to cross the threshold. To have been right to spend her career this way.
- **What she NEEDS**: To understand that the vindication she craved was consumed by the act of waiting for it — that the listening *was* the thing, not preparation for the thing.
- **Her flaw/blind spot**: Ruth has confused patience with feeling. She has trained herself so thoroughly to defer gratification that she can no longer access it when it arrives. She experiences emotion on a delay, weeks or months after the triggering event, and she has mistaken this numbness for discipline. She thinks she is calm. She is actually dissociated.

## 3. Structure

The essay is structured as a **contemplative meditation** in the mode of *Pilgrim at Tinker Creek* — organized not by chronological narrative but by **layers of attention**, moving between the cosmic and the intimate, the technical and the personal. It uses recurring motifs (the fan, the snail shells, the windmill, the dog) that accrue weight through repetition with variation, the way Dillard builds meaning through returning to the creek.

**However**, the essay is punctuated by **Johnson-style fragments** — short, disconnected sections (sometimes only a paragraph) that break the contemplative flow with raw, disorienting honesty about what the waiting has cost. These fragments are in a different register: spare, declarative, unsettling. They are the cracks in the meditative surface.

### Section Architecture (approximate):

**Opening (~800 words)**: The fan. The control room at Green Bank. Ruth at 3 AM on an observation night, the data scrolling, the HVAC drone. Describe the room with forensic precision — the fluorescent lights, the cheap office chairs, the particular staleness of recirculated air. Introduce the pulsars not as science but as *sound*: the ticking, the regularity, the way they mark time for nobody. End with the fact that she has been sitting in some version of this chair for fifteen years.

**The Quiet Zone (~700 words)**: The drive into Green Bank. The National Radio Quiet Zone — 13,000 square miles of electromagnetic silence. What it does to your phone (nothing, because it doesn't work). The 1960s diesel trucks. The way the town feels like a power outage with the lights on. This is Dillard-mode: precise, attentive, finding the sacred in the restricted. Ruth's relationship with the silence — she chose to live in it, and now silence is the medium of her consciousness.

**What She's Listening For (~900 words)**: The science, rendered as nature writing. Pulsars as cosmic lighthouses — dead stellar cores spinning hundreds of times per second, sweeping radio beams across the galaxy. The timing array: dozens of pulsars, observed monthly, their signals arriving with metronomic precision. Except when they don't. Except when something — something vast, something passing through the fabric of space itself — makes every pulsar in the sky wobble, very slightly, in a correlated way. Gravitational waves: the shivering of spacetime as supermassive black holes spiral toward each other across billions of years. The Hellings-Downs curve — the mathematical signature that distinguishes a real signal from noise. Introduce the Paludinella minima shell on her desk — the logarithmic spiral.

**Fragment: The Marriage (~300 words)**: Johnson-mode. Spare, declarative. Paul and Ruth in 2016, a year before the divorce. He asks if the signal came today, what would she do. She says she'd call her collaborators. He asks: then what. She doesn't know. This is the problem.

**The Nature of the Signal (~600 words)**: The inversion of Contact. Sagan's signal was a message — prime numbers, a blueprint, an intelligence reaching out. The NANOGrav signal is the opposite: it is evidence of distant violence. Supermassive black holes, each containing billions of solar masses, locked in decaying orbits, their approach warping spacetime in ripples that propagate at the speed of light. The universe is not speaking to her. It is making sounds in its sleep, like Johnson's man in the VA hospital. The gravitational-wave background is not a signal; it is a consequence. Not addressed to anyone. Ruth attends to it anyway.

**Fragment: The Windmill (~350 words)**: The sabbatical at Jodrell Bank. A weekend in Norfolk. The Gayton Windmill — eight storeys truncated to four, its cap and sails removed, thick walls remaining. She stood inside it and thought: this was built to face the wind, to turn, to grind. Now it's a place people sleep. She thinks about this more than she should.

**The Accumulation (~700 words)**: The years of data rendered as a kind of geological process. Dillard's controlled repetition: describe Ruth checking the data in 2009, 2012, 2015, 2018 — the same act, the same chair, the same fan, the same scrolling numbers. Small variations. A different mug. A different season outside the window. The error bars slowly, slowly shrinking. The bend in the data becoming — not clearer, exactly. Less obscured. Like Dillard's mountain emerging from the windshield: she didn't detect the signal. She just stopped being unable to detect it.

**Fragment: Keppler (~250 words)**: The dog. Twelve years old, bad hip, still follows her to the car every observation night, still waits by the door. Ruth carries him up the porch steps now. The dog has been waiting as long as the data. Neither of them knows what for.

**The Day (~600 words)**: June 2023. The line crosses the threshold. The Hellings-Downs correlation achieves statistical significance. Evidence consistent with a gravitational-wave background. Ruth looks at the graph on her screen. She has imagined this moment for fifteen years. She feels — Johnson's broken pipes. The water comes back on but there's nothing to carry it. She goes to the vending machine. She buys a coffee she doesn't drink. She calls her collaborator in Oregon. They are cautious. They use the word "consistent." Nobody says "detected." The universe became slightly less ambiguous today.

**The Creek and the Telescope (~500 words)**: The final meditation. Return to Dillard's mode — the telescope as the creek, the pulsars as the water striders. Ruth at the Green Bank telescope at night, after the announcement. The dish moving, tracking something invisible. She has been a bell her whole life and never knew it. Except — that's not quite right either. She knew. She always knew. She just couldn't hear the tone until the striking stopped.

**Ending (~200 words)**: Ruth drives home. The road through the Quiet Zone. No radio. No phone signal. Just the headlights and the dark and the mountains that have been there for four hundred million years, not caring whether anyone looks at them. Keppler is waiting by the door. She carries him up the steps. She doesn't feel vindicated. She doesn't feel empty. She feels the particular weight of a thing she's been holding so long she'd forgotten it had weight. She puts the dog down, and he circles three times on his bed, and she stands in her kitchen in the radio silence and does not know what to do next.

## 4. Key Scenes

1. **The Control Room at 3 AM**: Ruth alone with the data and the fan. This scene establishes the physical reality of the work — the anti-romantic, institutional, unglamorous fact of scientific observation. The pulsars ticking. The fluorescent lights. The scene must make the reader feel the weight of routine that has ossified into identity. (Advances: world-building, character interiority. Reveals: how deeply Ruth has merged with the act of waiting.)

2. **The Marriage Fragment**: Paul asking "then what?" The brevity is essential — Johnson's mode. Three hundred words that contain the entire trajectory of a relationship dissolved by a mismatch in temporal scale. Paul experienced time normally; Ruth experienced time in fifteen-year increments. The scene does not explain or moralize. It reports. (Advances: personal stakes. Reveals: Ruth's inability to inhabit the present tense of her own life.)

3. **The Accumulation Montage**: The same chair, the same data, different years. Controlled repetition with small variations — Dillard's method. This is not a narrative passage; it is a rhythmic passage, building the felt weight of duration. The reader should begin to feel trapped in the sameness, and then the shift: the error bars shrinking, the bend emerging. Not a revelation but a recognition. (Advances: the central arc of detection. Reveals: that the signal was always there; only the noise was in the way.)

4. **The Day the Signal Crosses**: The anti-climax. Ruth looks at the graph, feels the broken-pipe emptiness Johnson described. The vending machine coffee. The cautious phone call. This scene must resist every impulse toward drama. The universe did not announce itself. It became slightly less ambiguous. Ruth goes to the vending machine not because she wants coffee but because she needs to do something with her body. (Advances: climax. Reveals: Ruth's dissociation from her own emotional life — the cost of the patience.)

5. **The Ending — Keppler and the Kitchen**: Ruth carries the dog up the steps. Stands in the kitchen. Does not know what to do next. The radio silence of the Quiet Zone as metaphor — but also as literal fact. There is no signal here. There is no signal anywhere. There is only the willingness to listen. The ending is open: Ruth holding a weight she'd forgotten had weight, in a silence she chose and now cannot leave. (Advances: resolution-without-resolution. Reveals: the question the essay asks cannot be answered, only lived.)

## 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Opening**: The reader feels the hum of institutional tedium — the fluorescent, recirculated-air familiarity of a workplace that has become an extension of the worker. Mild claustrophobia. Interest in the science, curiosity about the woman.

**Middle**: As the years accumulate through repetition, the reader begins to feel the weight of duration — first as intellectual understanding, then as a somatic sensation of being stuck. The fragments about the marriage and the windmill introduce a sharper emotion: something between grief and bewilderment. The reader starts to wonder whether Ruth has been brave or broken.

**The Day**: The reader expects catharsis and receives its absence. The anti-climax should produce a feeling of vertigo — Ruth feels nothing, and the reader, who has been building toward this moment alongside her, shares the disorientation. The broken-pipes image should land hard.

**Ending**: The reader should feel the particular ache of recognizing that an unanswerable question has been asked with total sincerity. Not devastation. Not uplift. Something closer to the feeling you get when you realize you've been hearing a sound for a long time without noticing it, and now that you notice it, you can't tell whether it's beautiful or just persistent. The dog circling three times on his bed. The radio silence. The woman standing in her kitchen with fifteen years of patience in her hands and no instructions for what comes after.

## 6. Formula Integration

**Annie Dillard (Author A)**: Most visible in the **Accumulation** section and the **Creek and the Telescope** meditation. Dillard's prose style — precise observation rendered with mystical intensity, the quality of attention itself as subject — should govern the contemplative passages. The controlled repetition of checking the data in different years mirrors Dillard's method of returning to the creek across seasons. The specific Dillard passage to emulate: "I had been my whole life a bell, and never knew it until at that moment I was lifted and struck." Ruth's version of this is inverted — she always knew; she just couldn't hear the tone until the striking stopped.

**Denis Johnson (Author B)**: Most visible in the **fragments** (the marriage, Keppler, the windmill) and the **Day** section. Johnson's spare, declarative sentences, his gift for rendering consciousness that has been altered by duration rather than drugs. The broken-pipes image from the meeting is pure Johnson and should anchor the Day section. The fragments should feel like dispatches from a different essay — disconnected, raw, tonally distinct from the contemplative passages. The Johnson sentence to emulate: "I knew every raindrop by its name." Ruth should know every pulsar by its name — and this knowledge should feel like both mastery and damage.

**Pilgrim at Tinker Creek (Work X)**: The structural model. The essay is organized around a single place (Green Bank) and sustained observation (the pulsars), where the true subject is the quality of attention itself. The telescope is the creek; the pulsars are the muskrats. Build meaning through accumulation, not narrative progression. The recursive structure — returning to the same images with deepening understanding — is Dillard's architecture. The essay should feel like a meditation that circles, not a story that progresses.

**Contact (Work Y)**: The thematic inversion. *Contact*'s signal is intentional, designed, a message from intelligence. The NANOGrav signal is accidental, a side effect of physics, addressed to no one. This inversion should be explicitly addressed in the "Nature of the Signal" section — the essay should name Sagan (or his fictional analogue) and say: that is not what happened here. Nobody called. Nobody was trying to reach us. The universe is making sounds in its sleep. The choice to listen anyway — that is the essay's argument.

## 7. Title

**"Fifteen Years of Noise"** — Retaining the working title. It does not start with "The." It is direct, unadorned, and slightly ambiguous: "noise" refers to the radio noise of the pulsars, the gravitational-wave signal hidden within noise, and the existential noise of a life spent waiting. The word "fifteen" gives it a documentary specificity that makes it feel true. It echoes Johnson's sensibility (spare, declarative) while containing Dillard's subject (sustained attention). It does not duplicate any existing title on the platform.

## Notes for the Writer

1. **Register shifts are intentional.** The contemplative Dillard sections should flow; the Johnson fragments should interrupt. The reader should feel slightly jarred by the transitions. This is not a smooth essay — it is a meditation punctuated by honesty.

2. **The science must be precise.** Get the NANOGrav details right: 68 pulsars, 15 years, Hellings-Downs correlation, the phrase "evidence consistent with," the Arecibo collapse in 2020. The science IS the nature writing — render it with the same attention Dillard gives to water striders.

3. **The anti-climax is the climax.** Do not let the Day section become dramatic. The moment the signal crosses the threshold should feel like the least significant moment in the essay — because the essay has been arguing, through its structure, that the significant moments were all the ones that came before.

4. **Keppler the dog is essential.** The dog is the only character who waits alongside Ruth without needing a reason. The dog waits because Ruth is gone and then Ruth is home. This is the purest form of the attention the essay is about.

5. **The Paludinella minima shell**: Use this as a recurring physical detail — Ruth holds it, rolls it, sets it on the desk. Never explain the connection to the spiraling black holes. Let the reader find it or not.

6. **The windmill**: Use sparingly. One section, plus perhaps a glancing reference in the ending. It is a private image, not a public symbol.

7. **Do NOT resolve Ruth's emotional state at the end.** She does not have a revelation. She does not cry. She does not understand. She stands in her kitchen and does not know what to do next. The essay ends there. The reader must sit with that.

8. **Banned names**: Never use "Marcus" or "Chen."
