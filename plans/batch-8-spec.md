## Work 1: sf-timetravel-001

- **Genre**: science-fiction / time-travel
- **AuthorA**: Ted Chiang — Precise, philosophical short fiction; each story a thought experiment rendered with crystalline clarity
- **AuthorB**: Ray Bradbury — Poetic, nostalgic, emotionally warm prose; lyrical even when apocalyptic; Midwestern sensibility
- **WorkX** (structure): *Slaughterhouse-Five* by Kurt Vonnegut — Structure: unstuck in time; nonlinear jumps between past, present, and future experienced simultaneously; the war novel as shattered chronology
- **WorkY** (themes): *The Time Traveler's Wife* by Audrey Niffenegger — Themes: love constrained by temporal displacement; the relationship where one person always knows what's coming; grief embedded in every meeting
- **Target word count**: 3700 words (±300) / 15 min read
- **Risk card**: none
- **Formula summary**: "Chiang's crystalline thought-experiment precision meets Bradbury's nostalgic lyricism, structured through Vonnegut's shattered chronology and suffused with Niffenegger's temporal grief — a time travel story where knowing the future is the cruelest form of love."

## Work 2: hor-weirdfiction-001

- **Genre**: horror / weird-fiction
- **AuthorA**: Robert Aickman — "Strange stories" that resist explanation; deeply unsettling ambiguity; the uncanny as art form
- **AuthorB**: Carmen Maria Machado — Surreal, queer, formally inventive horror; the body as site of terror and desire; fairy-tale structures
- **WorkX** (structure): *House of Leaves* by Mark Z. Danielewski — Structure: nested narratives within narratives; documentary evidence that contradicts itself; the text as labyrinth that the reader must physically navigate
- **WorkY** (themes): *The Lottery* by Shirley Jackson — Themes: communal violence accepted as routine; the horror of what people agree not to question; ordinary settings made monstrous through social consensus
- **Target word count**: 4800 words (±300) / 19 min read
- **Risk card**: MULTIPLE VOICES — The story uses 2-3 distinct narrators whose accounts overlap, contradict, or illuminate each other. Each voice is formally distinguishable. The truth lives in the gaps between their versions.
- **Formula summary**: "Aickman's inexplicable dread fused with Machado's queer-bodied surrealism, built on Danielewski's nested-document architecture and Jackson's horror of social consensus — a weird fiction told through contradicting voices about something a community refuses to name."

## Work 3: wes-literary-001

- **Genre**: western / literary-western
- **AuthorA**: Annie Proulx — Brutal, compressed, landscape-saturated prose; the West as unforgiving
- **AuthorB**: Larry McMurtry — Elegiac, character-rich storytelling; the death of the Old West; warmth and loss
- **WorkX** (structure): *Plainsong* by Kent Haruf — Structure: spare, chapter-alternating parallel lives in a small town; no quotation marks; the architecture of community built sentence by patient sentence
- **WorkY** (themes): *Housekeeping* by Marilynne Robinson — Themes: transience vs. rootedness; the house as failed anchor; women choosing drift over domesticity; what it means to stay and what it means to go
- **Target word count**: 5800 words (±300) / 23 min read
- **Risk card**: none
- **Formula summary**: "Proulx's brutal landscape compression meets McMurtry's elegiac warmth, structured through Haruf's patient parallel-lives architecture and haunted by Robinson's meditation on rootedness and drift — a literary western about what holds people to land that gives nothing back."

## Work 4: dys-postapocalyptic-001

- **Genre**: dystopian / post-apocalyptic
- **AuthorA**: Octavia Butler — Visceral, unflinching prose; power dynamics; race, gender, and survival in extreme conditions
- **AuthorB**: P.D. James — Literary dystopia; elegiac, morally serious; the quiet end of humanity
- **WorkX** (structure): *The Road* by Cormac McCarthy — Structure: episodic, stripped-to-bone journey narrative; no chapters, just relentless forward motion through ruin; the father-child unit as last moral structure
- **WorkY** (themes): *Station Eleven* by Emily St. John Mandel — Themes: art as survival mechanism after collapse; the persistence of beauty in wreckage; memory of the old world as both sustenance and poison
- **Target word count**: 4100 words (±300) / 16 min read
- **Risk card**: none
- **Formula summary**: "Butler's unflinching survival instinct meets James's elegiac moral gravity, driven by McCarthy's stripped-bone journey structure and Mandel's insistence that art outlasts collapse — a post-apocalyptic story about what people carry when they can only carry one thing."

## Work 5: fan-swordandsorcery-001

- **Genre**: fantasy / sword-and-sorcery
- **AuthorA**: Joe Abercrombie — Gritty, visceral, darkly funny; grimdark realism; morally grey characters rendered with cynical precision
- **AuthorB**: Madeline Miller — Classical, elevated prose; mythological reimagining with modern emotional intelligence
- **WorkX** (structure): *The Blade Itself* by Joe Abercrombie — Structure: rotating close-third POV chapters; each character's arc moving toward inevitable collision; the ensemble cast where everyone believes they're the hero
- **WorkY** (themes): *Circe* by Madeline Miller — Themes: power earned through suffering rather than birthright; the outcast who becomes formidable; the cost of transformation; divinity as loneliness
- **Target word count**: 7600 words (±300) / 30 min read
- **Risk card**: REVERSE CHRONOLOGY — The story moves backward in time. Each scene occurs before the previous one. The reader gains understanding of causes only after experiencing consequences. The emotional arc runs counter to the chronological arc.
- **Formula summary**: "Abercrombie's cynical grimdark grit meets Miller's classical emotional elevation, built on Abercrombie's rotating-POV collision structure and Miller's themes of power earned through suffering — a sword-and-sorcery epic told in reverse, where every victory is recontextualized as loss."
