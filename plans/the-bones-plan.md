- Characters who believe they are one thing and discover they are another

### Selected Concepts for Fusion

**Concept 1: Cecidosidae (Gall Moths)**
Moths that pierce plant tissue to lay eggs inside it. The plant responds by growing a gall — an abnormal structure that houses the parasite. The plant absorbs the foreign body and builds around it. This is a perfect metaphor for what the camp does to the grandmother: she arrives as a foreign body, and the camp doesn't reject her — it simply grows around her. She is absorbed. The gall is not the plant and not the moth — it's a third thing, created by the encounter. The cook could know about gall moths (or more likely, galls on oak trees, which are common in the South — oak galls, oak apples). He could mention them in passing, describing the trees near the camp, the knotted growths on the branches overhead. The grandmother wouldn't understand what he's describing, but the reader would.

**Concept 2: Hinton Parva / Rommersheim — Villages Absorbed or Renamed**
Hinton Parva: a village whose population declined until the parish was absorbed into a larger one. Rommersheim: a village renamed after an abandoned nearby village — the living place taking the name of the dead one. Both are about communities that cease to exist as independent entities. The camp under the overpass is this kind of place — it has no official name, no parish boundaries, but it persists. And the grandmother's previous life (her apartment, her address, her neighborhood) is the Hinton Parva — the place that was absorbed, that ceased to exist as a distinct thing. Her eviction is the moment her parish was dissolved. She just hasn't accepted it yet.

## 1. Premise

A former restaurant cook named Tolliver lives in a tent camp beneath an overpass in South Memphis. He is not broken — he is competent, precise, and unsentimental about his situation. One evening, while gnawing a bone he retrieved from behind a barbecue joint and diagnosing with professional exactitude everything wrong with how it was prepared, a recently evicted grandmother named Earline arrives at the camp with a suitcase, a jar of muscadine preserves, and the absolute conviction that she is staying one night. Over the course of that night, their conversation becomes a contest between his ruthless knowledge of how things are and her desperate insistence that her situation is temporary — and by dawn, the camp has simply absorbed her, the way a tree absorbs a gall, without argument or ceremony.

## 2. Protagonist

**Name**: Tolliver (no first name given in the text — just Tolliver)
**Age**: 54
**Situation**: Former line cook at a mid-tier restaurant in Midtown Memphis (not a chef — a cook, he'd correct you), lost his apartment when the restaurant closed, chose the camp over the shelter because shelters have rules about check-in times and he can't abide the infantilization. Has been in the camp four months.

**What he WANTS**: To be left alone. Specifically, he wants the grandmother to stop talking to him, stop explaining her situation, stop insisting her daughter-in-law will come get her tomorrow. He wants to eat his bone in peace.

**What he NEEDS**: To acknowledge that his competence — his ability to identify wood smoke and seasoning ratios, to know where the dry ground is, to survive with precision — is not the same as being okay. His expertise has become a wall. He names everything because naming things is the only control he has left, and it keeps him from having to feel anything about his situation.

**His flaw/blind spot**: He believes that because he understands his situation clearly, he has mastered it. He thinks clarity IS the victory. But clarity without feeling is just a more articulate form of the grandmother's denial — he's performing competence the way she's performing temporary-ness. Neither of them is actually present to what's happening to them.

## 3. Structure

### Opening Image/Scene (~800 words)
Tolliver sits under the overpass at dusk, working a rib bone he pulled from behind Neely's BBQ on Winchester. The prose is pure McCarthy: the bone described with forensic precision — hickory smoke, over-applied paprika rub, meat pulled twenty minutes past optimal (the collagen has broken down past the point of clean separation, so the fibers come away in wet strings rather than clean sheets). He knows all this because he used to work with pork shoulders every day, knows the feel of properly rendered fat against the teeth versus fat that's been heated too fast. The camp is described around him: the overpass overhead, the sound of traffic changing pitch as the evening thickens, the geography of tarps and pallets and the clay drainage line you learn to avoid. Other people in the camp move around him. He is alone but not isolated. The tone is Suttree-esque: the grandeur and specificity of poverty, rendered without pity.

### Inciting Disruption (~1,000 words)
Earline arrives. She is 71, wearing a blouse with a Peter Pan collar and carrying a hard-sided suitcase from the 1990s and a paper grocery bag. She has been directed here by someone at the bus station after the shelter turned her away (she arrived after the check-in cutoff). She does NOT introduce herself as homeless — she introduces herself as someone whose daughter-in-law is coming to get her tomorrow. This is key. Her entire identity in this first section is organized around the word "tomorrow." She asks Tolliver where she should put her things, and the question itself — "put her things" — reveals everything about the distance between her self-image and her reality. You don't "put your things" in a tent camp. You find a spot. Tolliver tells her where the ground is dry. She thanks him with the formality of someone checking into a hotel.

Here is the O'Connor engine: Earline is the grandmother from "A Good Man Is Hard to Find," transplanted. She is performing gentility against the evidence. But unlike O'Connor's grandmother, whose performance is comic because it's transparent, Earline's performance has a desperation that makes it gothic — she is holding her self-image together through sheer will, and the cracks are visible only in the details. She brought the muscadine preserves. She brought a nightgown. She is dressed for a visit, not for survival.

### Escalating Middle (~2,500 words)
The night unfolds in roughly episodic sections — like Suttree, each encounter is a small complete world:

**Episode 1 — The Camp's Social Geography (~600 words)**
Tolliver, reluctantly drawn into conversation, explains the camp to Earline. Not charitably — factually. Who sleeps where. Which church van comes on Tuesday (First Baptist, decent sandwiches, no prayer requirement), which on Thursday (Living Word Fellowship, better food but you have to sit through fifteen minutes of testimony first, and the volunteers look at you the way you'd look at a dog you were considering adopting). The woman in the blue tent, Deedee, who has a generator and charges phones for a dollar. The man everyone calls Bishop who was actually a deacon at a church in Whitehaven before something happened that nobody asks about. Tolliver knows all this. He catalogs it the way he once cataloged mise en place.

**Episode 2 — The Preserves (~600 words)**
Earline opens the muscadine preserves. The jar is from her own kitchen — she made them last September, back when she still had a kitchen. She offers Tolliver some. He smells them before he tastes them and identifies the grape variety, the sugar ratio (too high — she used the full measure when she should have cut it by a quarter because muscadines are sweeter than Concords), the processing temperature (a shade too hot, the pectin's slightly scorched). He tells her all this. She stares at him. This is NOT a bonding moment — it's an intrusion. He has taken something she made with her own hands and autopsied it. But he can't help it. He names things. He identifies. That's his curse and his competence.

She asks what he did before. He says he cooked. She asks where. He names the restaurant (a real-sounding Memphis restaurant name — Bardo's, on Cooper Avenue). She asks if he liked it. He says he was good at it, which isn't the same thing.

**Episode 3 — The Oak Galls (~500 words)**
There is a live oak near the camp's edge — unusual for this part of Memphis, probably planted fifty years ago when the neighborhood was residential. Its branches extend over several of the tarp setups. Tolliver points out the galls on its branches — knotted, woody growths the size of golf balls. He explains what they are: a wasp lays an egg inside the branch, and the tree doesn't reject it — it grows around it, builds a structure to house the thing that's invaded it. The gall is not the tree and not the wasp. It's a third thing.

Earline says that's awful. Tolliver says it's not awful, it's just what happens. The tree doesn't decide. It doesn't fight. It grows.

This is the thematic heart of the story, delivered as natural history, not metaphor. Neither character draws the parallel explicitly. The reader does.

**Episode 4 — Earline's Story (~800 words)**
Over the course of the conversation, Earline's story emerges — not confessed, but leaked. She owned her apartment for nineteen years. Her landlord sold the building. New owners raised the rent. She couldn't pay. Her son lives in Jackson but his wife — the daughter-in-law — "has opinions about space." Earline spent two weeks in a motel on Lamar before the money ran out. She called her son. He said they'd figure something out. That was four days ago. She is absolutely certain he will call tomorrow.

Tolliver listens to all this without comment. Not out of kindness — out of exhaustion. He has heard variations of this story from everyone in the camp. The details change. The architecture doesn't. Someone had something. The thing was taken or slipped away. The person is certain the situation is temporary. The temporariness extends. The person is still certain. This is the narrative structure of the camp itself — everyone living inside the word "until." Until I get back on my feet. Until my disability comes through. Until my cousin in Little Rock calls back.

He doesn't tell her this. There's no point.

### Crisis Point (~1,000 words)
Past midnight. The camp is mostly asleep. Traffic overhead has thinned to individual trucks — you can hear each one coming and going, a sound like breath. Earline is lying on a folded blanket on a pallet Deedee gave her, her suitcase beside her like a bedside table.

She folds her hands. She prays. Same prayer as every night — the words automatic, worn smooth by repetition. But for the first time, the prayer does not meet a ceiling. It goes up into the concrete underside of the overpass and the sound of trucks and the cold air above that and the sky beyond that. The words are the same. The container is different. And she knows it's different. She doesn't stop praying, but something in the prayer shifts — the words no longer reach where they used to reach, and the new distance is enormous.

This is the O'Connor moment — "not grace, but adjacent." Something in her recognition of where she actually is, not where she tells herself she is. It is not a conversion. It is not an epiphany. It is the prayer going up into nothing instead of a ceiling, and the nothing being real.

Tolliver is awake, though she doesn't know it. He hears her murmuring. He doesn't hear the words. He hears the sound of someone talking to a room that isn't there.

### Ending (~800 words)
Dawn. The light changes on the underside of the overpass — the concrete goes from black to gray to the color of old paper. The traffic resumes its daytime hiss. Someone in the camp has a radio going, gospel music from a station that plays gospel until seven and then switches to talk.

Earline is still there. She has not been retrieved. Her phone has not rung. She is sitting on the pallet with her suitcase still beside her, and her posture is the same posture she arrived with — upright, arranged, a woman who is going somewhere — but her hands are different. Her hands are in her lap, palms up, not folded, not holding anything, and she is looking at the oak tree with the galls on its branches.

Tolliver is making coffee over a Sterno can — instant coffee in a tin cup, water heated to not quite boiling (he knows the exact temperature where instant coffee stops dissolving and starts burning — 185 degrees, and he can feel the difference in the metal). He brings her a cup without being asked. She takes it with both hands.

She says: My daughter-in-law will call today.

He says: All right.

He doesn't correct her. The camp doesn't correct. The camp absorbs.

The last image: the oak galls overhead, lit by the morning sun, looking like something that grew there on purpose. The bone from last night, stripped clean, lying in the packed clay near the edge of the drainage line. The empty jar of muscadine preserves between them, still smelling of dark grapes and too much sugar.

**What is left open**: Whether the daughter-in-law will call. Whether Earline will still be there tomorrow night. Whether Tolliver's precision — his curse and competence — will ever be anything other than a way to avoid feeling. The story ends mid-morning, mid-situation, mid-life. The camp continues.

## 4. Key Scenes

1. **The Bone** (opening): Tolliver eating the rib bone with professional knowledge that makes every bite a forensic report on someone else's failure. The sensory detail must be McCarthy-level — the reader has to taste it before they understand what it costs.

2. **Earline's Arrival**: Her Peter Pan collar, her suitcase, her question about where to "put her things." The comic-gothic gap between her self-presentation and her situation, in the O'Connor register.

3. **The Preserves Autopsy**: Tolliver smelling and identifying everything about the muscadine preserves, turning her homemade offering into a clinical assessment. This scene crystallizes both his competence and his inability to simply receive something.

4. **The Oak Galls**: Natural history as covert metaphor. The tree that grows around what invades it. Neither character comments on the parallel. The reader carries it.

5. **The Prayer Without a Ceiling**: Earline's nightly prayer going up into open air instead of a bedroom ceiling. The moment where the container changes and the words feel different. "Not grace, but adjacent."

6. **The Morning Coffee**: Tolliver bringing her a cup without being asked. The smallest act of absorption. She says her daughter-in-law will call. He says: All right.

## 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Reader's arc**: Moves from fascination (the bone, the expertise) to dark humor (Earline's arrival, her insistence on temporariness) to growing discomfort (the camp's social ecology, the leaked details of Earline's dispossession) to a deep, quiet grief at the prayer scene — and then the ending, which refuses to resolve into either hope or despair, leaving the reader in the same suspended state as the characters. The camp continues. Life absorbs what invades it.

**Tolliver's arc**: He begins wanting to be left alone and ends having given her coffee. That's it. No transformation. No recognition. But the act itself — heating water to exactly the right temperature, carrying the cup — is the first thing he's done for someone else in the story. His competence, for one moment, serves another person rather than defending himself.

**Earline's arc**: She arrives performing temporary-ness and ends still performing it ("my daughter-in-law will call today"). But her hands have changed position. The prayer was different. Something has shifted, even if she cannot name it and would deny it if asked.

## 6. Formula Integration

### From Author A — Cormac McCarthy (Style)
- **No quotation marks** around dialogue. Dialogue embedded in narrative prose, distinguished by rhythm.
- **Biblical cadence** in descriptions of mundane poverty — the camp described with the same elemental grandeur McCarthy gives to rivers and deserts.
- **Physical precision**: the bone described with forensic specificity (collagen, rendering temperature, fiber separation). Coffee temperature. The feel of packed clay versus mud.
- **Suttree's episodic structure**: the night broken into encounters, each one a self-contained world.
- **The river replaced by the overpass**: McCarthy's river in Suttree becomes the overpass — the thing that runs over and past the characters, never stopping, marking time by sound rather than sight.

### From Author B — Flannery O'Connor (Style)
- **The comedy of self-deception**: Earline performing gentility in a tent camp, asking where to "put her things."
- **Grotesque specificity**: the camp's characters (Bishop the maybe-deacon, Deedee with her generator) drawn with O'Connor's eye for the particular detail that reveals the whole person.
- **The prayer as near-grace**: the moment where Earline's practiced prayer meets open sky, echoing the grandmother's final reach toward the Misfit — not a conversion, but a crack in the performance.
- **Dialogue that reveals self-deception**: Earline's repeated insistence on "tomorrow," "one night," "my daughter-in-law" — each repetition tightening the screw.

### From Work X — Suttree (Structure)
- **Episodic night structure**: like Suttree's chapters, the night is organized as a sequence of encounters — the bone, the arrival, the camp tour, the preserves, the oak galls, the stories, the prayer, the dawn.
- **A man living outside society by choice**: Tolliver chose the camp over the shelter, as Suttree chose the river over his family's wealth.
- **Relationships with the marginal**: the camp's residents are Suttree's ragpickers and drunks — not sentimentalized, not degraded, just present.
- **The overpass as river**: a constant presence, marking time by sound (the pitch of traffic changes with weather, with hour), shaping the geography of the camp the way the Tennessee River shapes Suttree's world.

### From Work Y — "A Good Man Is Hard to Find" (Themes)
- **The collision between self-image and reality**: Earline's conviction that she is temporary vs. the camp's permanence.
- **The grandmother figure**: Earline is the grandmother transplanted — performing respectability, clutching her identity, undone not by a gunman but by economic reality.
- **Violence as revelation**: here the violence is not physical but economic — eviction, the daughter-in-law's "opinions about space," the motel money running out. The violence that strips pretense.
- **"She would of been a good woman..."**: The Misfit's line echoes in Tolliver's refusal to correct Earline. He doesn't tell her she's not temporary. He just says "All right." His refusal to challenge her fiction is its own kind of mercy — or cruelty. The story doesn't decide.

### Wikipedia Concept Integration

**Cecidosidae / Oak Galls**: The gall wasp that lays eggs in tree tissue, and the tree's response — growing around the foreign body, creating a structure that is neither tree nor wasp but a third thing. Appears literally as the oak galls Tolliver points out to Earline. Operates as the story's covert structural metaphor: the camp absorbs newcomers without argument or decision. Earline is the egg. The camp is the tree. What grows between them is the gall — a third thing, neither her old life nor what she imagines her new one to be.

**Hinton Parva / villages absorbed**: The concept of a place that ceases to exist as an independent entity — its parish dissolved, its population absorbed into a larger structure — echoes in Earline's situation. Her address, her apartment, her nineteen years of tenancy: all dissolved. She is Hinton Parva, the parish that was absorbed, insisting she still has her own boundaries.

## 7. Title

**Title: Hickory, Not Oak**

Rationale: This comes from Tolliver's first assessment of the bone — he knows the wood used to smoke it was hickory, not oak. It captures his precision, his curse-competence, his inability to simply eat something without identifying it. It also works as an oblique reference to the oak tree with its galls — the story's other tree, the one that absorbs rather than flavors. The title doesn't start with "The" (avoiding the pattern). It's specific, strange, and suggestive without explaining itself.

Alternative titles considered:
- "Pulled Twenty Minutes Late" (too long, too quirky)
- "Adjacent" (too abstract)
- "What the Seasoning Knows" (already used for the meeting title)
- "Oak Gall" (too on-the-nose)
- "Earline, After" (reveals too much)
- "Overnight" (too generic)

## Technical Notes for Writer

### Prose Voice
The dominant voice should be McCarthy's — sparse, unpunctuated dialogue, long sensory sentences, biblical cadence. But the O'Connor influence should surface in the comic-grotesque details of the camp's residents and especially in Earline's dialogue and self-presentation. The two voices should not alternate — they should be woven. The camp described in McCarthy's register; the characters' self-deceptions revealed in O'Connor's.

### POV
Third person limited, tracking Tolliver. We are inside his expertise, his assessments, his cataloging. But we can see Earline clearly because Tolliver sees clearly — what he can't see is himself.

### Dialogue Formatting
No quotation marks. McCarthy convention. Dialogue identified by rhythm and attribution. Example:

*He told her the ground was dry on the east side if she kept to the right of the drainage line. She asked what happened if it rained. He said it rained sometimes. She said she meant what happened to the ground. He said it got wet.*

### Pacing
Slow burn. The story's tension is not external (nothing "happens" in the plot sense) but comes from the growing gap between Earline's narrative about herself and the reality we can see. The reader's discomfort should build gradually, like the change from evening to night to dawn.

### What to Avoid
- Do NOT have Tolliver explicitly pity Earline or reflect on the irony of his situation. He doesn't think in those terms.
- Do NOT have Earline "break down" or "finally admit" anything. Her performance holds. The cracks are visible only in details — the hands, the prayer, the posture.
- Do NOT resolve whether the daughter-in-law will call.
- Do NOT make the oak gall metaphor explicit. Let it sit.
- Do NOT end with a neat image that ties everything together. End mid-morning, mid-situation.
