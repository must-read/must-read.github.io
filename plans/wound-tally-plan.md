# Story Plan: Wound Tally

## Assignment
- **Genre**: crime-noir
- **Subgenre**: hardboiled
- **Combo ID**: noir-hardboiled-002

- **Target Word Count**: 3,800 words (range: 3,500-4,100)

## Formula Sources
- **Author A (Chester Himes)**: absurdist violence erupting without warning, dark humor as survival mechanism, rage channeled through picaresque energy, the city as chaotic organism
- **Author B (Ernest Hemingway)**: terse declarative prose, emotional restraint masking devastation, dialogue circling what cannot be said, wounded masculinity performing composure
- **Work X (A Rage in Harlem)**: a con spiraling through increasingly absurd and dangerous layers, love as the vulnerability that gets you killed, Harlem as a complete world with its own physics

## Research Samples Collected

### Chester Himes (A Rage in Harlem)
- Opening aerial prose: "Looking eastward from the towers of Riverside Church... waves of grey rooftops distort the perspective like the surface of the sea. Below the surface, in the murky waters of fetid tenements, a city of black people who are convulsed in desperate living, like the voracious churning of millions of hungry cannibal fish. Blind mouths eating their own guts. Stick in a hand and draw back a nub. That is Harlem."
- Police action: "Red-eyed patrol cars darted about like angry bugs, screaming to a stop, cops hitting flatfooted on the pavement, picking up every suspicious-looking character for the lineup."
- Physical description: Jackson as "a short, black, fat man with purple-red gums and pearly white teeth made for laughing."
- Imabelle's seduction: "She held him at arms' length... The man drowned. When he came up, he stared back, passion cocked, his whole black being on a live-wire edge... Solid ready to cut throats, crack skulls, dodge police, steal hearses, drink muddy water, live in a hollow log."
- **Style notes**: Prose swings between lyric description and blunt physical comedy. Harlem itself is a character — a devouring organism. Violence is absurd and sudden. Love makes men stupid in specific, comic, devastating ways.

### Ernest Hemingway (The Sun Also Rises / Short Stories)
- "A Clean, Well-Lighted Place" — the nada prayer: "Our nada who art in nada, nada be thy name thy kingdom nada thy will be nada in nada as it is in nada."
- "What did he fear? It was not fear or dread. It was a nothing that he knew too well. It was all a nothing and a man was nothing too."
- "He would lie in the bed and finally, with daylight, he would go to sleep. After all, he said to himself, it is probably only insomnia."
- Sun Also Rises opening: Robert Cohn introduced through what other people think of him, facts delivered flat, emotional content carried entirely by what is left out.
- **Style notes**: Short declarative sentences. Emotional devastation conveyed through physical description and restraint. Dialogue does the work of narration. What is NOT said is the architecture. The "iceberg theory" — 7/8 below the waterline.

## Wikipedia Random Concept Mining (20 Searches)

1. **Tally stick** — Medieval accounting device: notched hazelwood sticks split in half, each party keeps one piece as proof of debt. Stock (creditor) and foil (debtor). Natural irregularities in the split make counterfeiting impossible.
2. **Numbers game** — Illegal lottery in Harlem. Runners carry money and slips to the "bank." In the 1950s, billions circulated. Runners were charismatic neighborhood fixtures and prodigious mathematicians.
3. **Korean War African American veterans** — 100,000 Black troops at start, 600,000 by end. The 24th Infantry Regiment, all-Black, won the first victory at Yechon. Desegregation happened during the war. Veterans returned and energized the civil rights movement.
4. **Phantom limb syndrome** — 80-100% of amputees experience sensation in absent limb. The brain's body model doesn't update. Mirror box therapy creates illusion of the missing limb moving. Pain without presence.
5. **Float glass** — Molten glass poured onto molten tin to create perfectly flat sheets. Invented 1953-1957 (same era as story). The glass floats, finds its own level, emerges "fire-polished."
6. **Rent party** — 1920s-50s Harlem: tenants charged admission to apartment parties with live music to raise rent money. Stride piano. Harlem Stride. A communal survival mechanism disguised as entertainment.
7. **Wound ballistics / terminal ballistics** — The study of what a projectile does when it hits a body. Entry characteristics, exit characteristics, distance and direction of fire. "Vitality of the wound."
8. **Battle of Chosin Reservoir** — Nov-Dec 1950. 30,000 UN troops vs. 120,000 Chinese in lethal cold. 17-day battle. Marines fought their way out of encirclement. Massive casualties from both combat and frostbite.
9. **Numbers racket structure** — "Wheel" or banker at top. By 1925, thirty Black policy banks in Harlem. Runners were the public face. Stephanie St. Clair, "Queen of the Policy Rackets."
10. **Coanda effect** — A fluid jet stays attached to a curved surface it passes near. Deflection through entrainment. Used in aircraft lift. The jet bends to follow the wall.
11. **Lacuna** — In anatomy: tiny cavity in bone housing an osteocyte. In textual scholarship: a gap in a manuscript where something is missing. Plural: lacunae. "A space where something has been omitted or has come out."
12. **Sugar Hill, Harlem** — The "sweet life" neighborhood. Professional and creative elite. Thurgood Marshall, Willie Mays, Frankie Lymon in the 1950s. Mary Lou Williams's jazz salon.
13. **Debridement** — Surgical removal of dead, damaged, or infected tissue to improve healing. Autolytic debridement uses the body's own enzymes. Must remove the dead to let the living heal.
14. **Three-card monte** — A confidence game. The mark believes they can find the money card. Shills pretend to help the mark while actually helping the dealer. The mark's confidence is the mechanism of their loss. "The psychology of the con is to increase the mark's confidence."
15. **Vitiligo** — Autoimmune condition causing skin depigmentation. Patches of skin lose their color. The body attacking its own pigment.
16. **Lenox Avenue** — Harlem's heartbeat (Langston Hughes). IRT subway line underneath. Savoy Ballroom. Co-named Malcolm X Boulevard in 1987.
17. **Keloid scarring** — The body produces 20x more collagen than needed at a wound site. The scar grows beyond the original wound boundary. Healing that becomes its own pathology.
18. **Hot comb** — Hair straightening tool central to African American beauty culture. Madam C.J. Walker. A contested symbol of assimilation vs. self-care. The sizzle, the smell.
19. **Standing eight count** — Boxing: referee gives a fighter who is overwhelmed but not yet knocked down an 8-second respite. Abolished in professional boxing in 1998. A pause to assess whether you can continue. Treated as a knockdown for scoring.
20. **Soil liquefaction** — Under stress (earthquake), solid ground behaves like liquid. The apparently firm suddenly cannot bear weight. "Sand boils" erupt at the surface. Lateral spreading on slopes of only 1-2 degrees.

### Selected Concepts (2):
1. **Standing eight count** — The con's climax is structured like a standing eight count: the protagonist is overwhelmed but not yet down, and the pause that should save him is what exposes him. Also maps onto the war — the veteran has been receiving a standing eight count since Korea, a pause that isn't rest but assessment.
2. **Keloid scarring** — The wound tally of the title. The protagonist's scars (both literal from Korea and figurative from the con) heal in excess — they grow beyond the original wound. His performance of normalcy is a keloid: a survival response that has become its own pathology, visible, raised, marking him as damaged even as it proves he survived.

## Premise

**Harlem, August 1953.** Darnell Greer, twenty-six, has been back from Korea for eleven months. He runs a small property con on the blocks between Lenox Avenue and Seventh — selling "shares" in a building he doesn't own to working people who want an address they can pass to their children. The con is tight, small, only a few hundred dollars at a time, and sustainable because Darnell is careful and because the marks never compare notes.

The story covers one day — a Thursday in August — when three things converge: his biggest mark, a church deacon named Purnell Ames, is ready to pay $400 for a "share" that doesn't exist; a woman named Claudine Rowe, who runs a hair salon two blocks from the building Darnell pretends to own, has figured out the con (because one of Darnell's marks was her client and described the building to her, and Claudine knows the building's real owner); and the heat — 98 degrees, the kind that puts everyone on the stoop, turns the block into an audience.

The con spirals. Darnell must close Purnell before Claudine exposes him, but Claudine is not interested in exposing him — she wants a cut. And as the negotiation between Darnell and Claudine intensifies (in the back room of her salon, under the smell of hot combs and pressing oil), the war begins to leak through Darnell's performance. Not as flashback. As a stain appearing in the wrong room.

## Protagonist

**Darnell Greer**, 26. Born in Aiken, South Carolina, moved to Harlem at 14 with his mother. Drafted in 1951, served in Korea with the 24th Infantry Regiment (one of the last all-Black units before integration). Was at Chosin in spirit though not literally — his unit saw heavy action in the spring of '51 near the Imjin River. Came back with a Purple Heart, a keloid scar on his left forearm from shrapnel, and a sound in his ears that isn't tinnitus but isn't silence either.

**Specific flaw**: Darnell believes his own performance. He has told his polished war stories so many times — the sergeant who couldn't read a map, the rations three years expired — that the real memories have been walled behind them. He thinks the wall is solid. It isn't. The performance of composure IS his composure, and when the performance cracks, there is nothing underneath but the thing he walled in. He cannot distinguish between being okay and appearing okay, and this inability is what makes the con possible (he's convincing because he's convinced) and what will break him (when the convincing fails, he doesn't have a self to fall back on).

**Physical**: Lean, not tall, moves with an economy that reads as either grace or tension depending on who's watching. Keloid scar on left forearm that he covers with long sleeves even in August. Pearly teeth he shows often — laughter is his primary social instrument. Hands that are always doing something: straightening a collar, adjusting a hat brim, folding a newspaper. The hands betray the energy the face won't show.

## Structure (5 Beats)

### Beat 1: THE BUILDING (Opening — ~700 words)
**Thursday, 7:30 AM.** Darnell walks his route. Third-person close, Hemingway-terse. Short declarative sentences establishing the block, the heat already building, the bodies already out. He walks past the building — 412 West 133rd — and looks at it the way a man looks at something he owns, which is to say with irritation and familiarity, not desire. He doesn't own it. The building belongs to a man named Floyd Pinckney who lives in Mount Vernon and collects rent through an agent Darnell has studied. Darnell knows the building's dimensions, its flaws, its tenants' names. He knows it better than Floyd Pinckney does. This is the heart of the con and the heart of the delusion: knowing a thing so well you forget it isn't yours.

Introduce the numbers runner, **Tick** — a fixture, an organism of the block — who serves as comic relief and Greek chorus. Tick sees everything, comments on everything, and is the one person on the block who knows Darnell's con is a con (but doesn't care, because everyone's running something). Tick's dialogue should carry the Himes picaresque energy: fast, funny, profane, observant.

War leak #1 (small): Darnell pauses at the corner where a fire hydrant has been opened, water flooding the gutter. The water catches the light. He doesn't flinch or freeze, but his route adjusts — he walks on the dry side without thinking about it, without knowing he's doing it. The reader might not catch this the first time.

### Beat 2: THE MARK (Disruption — ~800 words)
**10:00 AM.** Darnell meets **Purnell Ames** in a diner on Lenox. Purnell is a church deacon, 52, a man who has spent thirty years being trustworthy and now wants to do something shrewd. He has $400 in a church envelope — money he can't afford to lose but pretends he can. The scene plays as a Hemingway dialogue set-piece: two men talking around the actual subject, never naming the building directly, never saying "share" or "investment," using euphemisms that both understand. Darnell is smooth, warm, precise. He tells one of his war stories — the sergeant and the map — and Purnell laughs. The laughter seals the deal.

But the deal doesn't close. Just as Purnell reaches for the envelope, a woman enters the diner — not Claudine, but a woman who reminds Darnell of someone. A smell, a way of moving. For two seconds, he is not in the diner. When he comes back, his hand is gripping the table edge, and Purnell has noticed. Darnell recovers — laughs it off, blames the heat — but the moment has introduced doubt. Not in Purnell's mind about the con, but in Darnell's body about his own control.

Purnell says he'll bring the money at 3:00 PM. He wants to see the building first. Darnell agrees, because saying no would be worse.

### Beat 3: THE WITNESS (Escalation — ~1,000 words)
**11:30 AM.** Darnell goes to Claudine Rowe's salon. He knows she knows. He learned this yesterday when Tick mentioned, casually, the way Tick mentions everything, that "that woman on 131st been asking about 412." Darnell's plan was to charm Claudine, deny everything, make her doubt what she knows. What he didn't plan for is Claudine.

**Claudine Rowe**, 30. Runs the salon, owns the lease, has a daughter she's raising alone. She is not interested in justice or morality. She's interested in money. She figured out the con because she pays attention — one of her clients, a woman named Delphine, described the building she was "buying into" and Claudine recognized the address. She went to Floyd Pinckney's rent agent and confirmed: no shares, no investment plan, no Darnell Greer on any paperwork.

The scene in the salon is the story's fulcrum. Claudine doesn't threaten. She offers. She wants 20% of whatever Darnell takes from Purnell and from any future marks. In exchange, she doesn't mention what she knows to anyone. The negotiation takes place in the back room while a client is under a dryer in the front, while the smell of hot combs and bergamot saturates the air.

This is where the Himes energy explodes into the Hemingway structure. The dialogue is terse but the situation is picaresque — two hustlers negotiating terms in a hair salon while a woman reads Jet magazine ten feet away. Claudine is not a love interest and not a victim and not a salve. She's a businesswoman recognizing an opportunity. She sees Darnell clearly — sees the performance, sees the sleeves in August, sees the hands.

War leak #2 (medium): During the negotiation, Claudine says something about the building's basement — "the pipes down there have been leaking since winter, the whole sub-floor is rotted." And Darnell, for no reason that makes sense in the conversation, says: "You can smell it when the ground is wrong. Before it goes. You can smell it." Claudine stops. This is not a sentence about plumbing. This is a sentence from somewhere else. Darnell doesn't explain it. He moves on. But Claudine has heard the wrong room.

She takes the deal — 20%.

### Beat 4: THE SHOW (Crisis — ~900 words)
**3:00 PM.** The building. Purnell arrives with the money. Darnell walks him through — they can't go inside, but Darnell knows every exterior detail, every window, every tenant's laundry on the fire escape. He narrates the building as if reading a ledger of what it's worth, and the narration is convincing because it's true. He knows this building. He just doesn't own it.

The block is an audience. It's 98 degrees. Kids on the hydrant. Tick on the corner, watching. Two old men on a stoop playing dominoes. A woman selling snow cones from a cart. The entire scene is staged — not by Darnell, but by August, by Harlem, by the physics of a block that turns every transaction into a performance.

Then: Claudine appears. She isn't supposed to be here. The deal was 20% after, not during. But Claudine has come because she wants to see — not to interfere, just to watch the show. She stands across the street with her arms folded.

Darnell sees her. Purnell sees Darnell see her. The moment is a standing eight count: the referee has stopped the fight, and Darnell has eight seconds to prove he can continue.

He cannot. Not because Claudine does anything — she just stands there. But her presence, the fact of a witness who knows, changes the physics. Under this pressure, the solid ground of Darnell's performance liquefies.

War leak #3 (full): Darnell begins describing the building's value to Purnell, and the description shifts. The building becomes another building. The numbers become other numbers. He starts talking about load-bearing walls, about what happens when you remove a wall that's holding the weight, about structures that look sound from the outside. He is not talking about 412 West 133rd anymore. He's talking about a position his unit held near the Imjin River, a farmhouse they occupied for three days that looked solid until artillery removed the south wall and everything above it came down on four men who were sleeping. He doesn't say this. The reader hears it in the architecture of the description — the suddenly technical language, the specificity about weight distribution, the way his voice goes flat and precise.

Purnell steps back. He puts the envelope in his inside pocket.

"I think I need to talk to my wife," Purnell says.

The con is dead.

### Beat 5: THE TALLY (Ending — ~400 words)
**4:30 PM.** Darnell sits on the stoop of 412 West 133rd. Not his building. Not anyone's building — it's just a building. Tick comes by, says something funny, gets no response, moves on. The hydrant is still open down the block. The water in the gutter catches the late-afternoon sun.

Claudine comes. She sits on the stoop two steps below him. She doesn't mention the con, the 20%, the failure. She asks him about the scar on his arm — the keloid is visible because he's rolled up his sleeves; it's too hot to keep performing that particular concealment.

"Korea," he says.

"It healed big," she says.

"That's what it does. Heals more than it needs to."

She looks at the street. He looks at the building. Neither of them says anything for a while, and this silence is not Hemingway's luxury silence or the performed silence of composure. It's the silence of two people sitting on a stoop in August because neither has anywhere better to be, and the silence has a weight that neither of them signed up for but neither of them moves away from.

**Open thread**: Claudine is still there. She still knows the con. She still wants money. What she does with what she's seen — whether she becomes an ally, a partner, a different kind of threat — is unresolved. The story ends with her on the stoop, and the question is not whether Darnell will con again (he will; the rent is due) but whether the next performance will be possible now that someone has seen the stain.

The last image: the keloid on his forearm, raised and shining in the heat. A wound that healed more than it needed to. The tally of what the body does with damage when the body is left to its own accounting.

## Key Scenes

1. **Darnell walks the block (opening)** — Establishes voice, setting, the building, and Tick. Hemingway's physical specificity married to Himes's block-as-organism. First small war leak through the fire hydrant avoidance.

2. **The diner with Purnell** — A dialogue set-piece where two men never name what they're doing. Hemingway's circling dialogue. The war story as social currency. The flash of disruption — the woman in the diner who triggers a two-second absence.

3. **The salon with Claudine** — The story's center. Himes's absurdist energy (negotiating a con's cut in a hair salon under the dryer noise) inside Hemingway's emotional restraint. Claudine as a character with her own economy. War leak #2: "You can smell it when the ground is wrong."

4. **The show at the building** — All threads converge in public, in heat, on the block. The con's collapse is not dramatic — it's structural. Darnell's language shifts and the performance fails. Purnell walks away not because he's been warned but because something in Darnell's voice changed rooms.

5. **The stoop (ending)** — Claudine and Darnell in a silence that is neither performed nor earned. The keloid as final image. The open thread of what Claudine will do. No resolution, no epiphany, no redemption arc.

## Emotional Trajectory

**Opening**: Competence, control, the pleasure of a well-maintained performance. Darnell is good at this. The reader should enjoy watching him work, the way you enjoy watching a mechanic who knows the engine.

**Mid-point**: Cracks appearing — not moral doubt but physical failure. The body refuses to maintain the wall. Each war leak is involuntary, not a choice to "open up" or "be vulnerable." The body simply fails to hold the partition.

**Crisis**: The full leak. The performance collapses not through confrontation but through the presence of a witness. Claudine's watching changes the pressure, and under pressure the apparently solid liquefies (soil liquefaction — the ground that seemed firm behaves like water).

**Ending**: Not despair, not hope. An exhausted, heat-stunned honesty that might be the beginning of something or might just be August. The wound tally is the body's accounting: what was taken, what healed wrong, what grew beyond the boundary of the original injury.

## Formula Integration Plan

### From Chester Himes (Author A):
- **Harlem as organism**: The block has its own physics, its own economy, its own audience. Every scene has bystanders. Nothing is private.
- **Absurdist energy**: The con negotiation in the hair salon. Tick as comic chorus. The escalating layers — the con, the cut, the witness, the building tour as theater.
- **Dark humor as survival**: Darnell's war stories are funny. Tick is funny. The situation of two hustlers dividing profits from a con that hasn't happened yet is funny. The humor doesn't mitigate the darkness; it IS the darkness wearing a better suit.
- **Identifiable passages**: The block descriptions, Tick's dialogue, the salon scene, the picaresque energy of the building tour with its stoop audience.

### From Ernest Hemingway (Author B):
- **Terse declarative prose**: Short sentences. Physical description carrying emotional weight. "He sat down. The coffee was hot. He drank it."
- **Dialogue circling the unsaid**: The diner scene with Purnell. The stoop scene with Claudine. What is NOT said is the story's real content.
- **Wounded masculinity performing composure**: Darnell's entire existence is this. The war stories as performance. The sleeves in August. The hands that won't stop moving.
- **Identifiable passages**: The opening walk, the diner dialogue, the final stoop silence, all the moments where restraint does the emotional work.

### From A Rage in Harlem (Work X):
- **Con spiraling through absurd layers**: The property scheme is simple; the complications (Claudine's discovery, Purnell's naivety, the public venue) make it picaresque.
- **Love as vulnerability**: Not romantic love, but the mark's trust — Purnell loves the idea of owning something, and that love is what Darnell exploits. Also: Claudine's presence introduces a non-romantic intimacy that is its own vulnerability.
- **Harlem as complete world**: The block between Lenox and Seventh has its own characters, economy, weather, audience. It doesn't need the rest of New York.

### From The Sun Also Rises (Work Y):
- **Post-traumatic drift**: Darnell is Jake Barnes in Harlem — functioning, sociable, carrying a wound that cannot be named directly. The armistice was months ago but the war is present tense.
- **Generation unmade by violence**: Every man on the block came back from somewhere with something wrong. Darnell is not unusual. He's just the one whose performance cracks today.
- **Expatriate displacement**: Darnell is an expatriate from his own previous self. Pre-Korea Darnell is gone. The man who came back wears his clothes and lives in his room but is not him.

## Voice and Style Notes for Writer

- **POV**: Third-person close on Darnell. Free indirect discourse. The narrator sounds like Darnell's internal voice but flattened, controlled — Hemingway restraint filtering Himes observation.
- **Sentence rhythm**: Default is short and declarative. Longer sentences only when describing the block or Claudine — the Himes energy needs room to sprawl in those moments.
- **Dialogue**: Sparse tags. Characters identified by speech pattern. Tick talks in streams. Purnell talks in careful complete sentences. Claudine talks in short questions and flat statements. Darnell modulates — warm and flowing with Purnell, clipped and careful with Claudine.
- **War leaks**: These are NOT flashbacks, NOT italicized memories, NOT scene breaks. They are sentences that don't belong in the conversation. The writer must make them feel like a wrong note in an otherwise controlled piece of music — not announced, not explained, just present. The reader should feel the ground shift.
- **Heat**: The heat is constant, physical, mentioned through bodies and surfaces, not through statement. Sweat on a glass. The weight of long sleeves. The smell of the salon. The open hydrant.
- **Banned**: No "Marcus," no "Chen." No flashback sequences. No moment where Darnell "realizes" or "understands" his trauma. No tidy ending. No symmetrical bookend with the opening walk.

## Title Confirmation

**"Wound Tally"** — Does not start with "The." Diverges from existing crime-noir titles (Presumptive Pink, Dragging the Channel, Sixty Counts, Narrow Filaments). The tally is: what was taken in Korea, what grew wrong in the healing, what the body keeps counting after the official count is over. Also resonates with the numbers game (tally as count), the property con (tally as ledger), and the standing eight count (tally as score). The title earns its meaning through accumulation, not announcement.
