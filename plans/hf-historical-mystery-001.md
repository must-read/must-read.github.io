## Research: Writing Samples

### Hilary Mantel — Wolf Hall

**Opening passage** (present-tense immersion, physical consciousness):

> "So now get up." Felled, dazed, silent, he has fallen; knocked full length on the cobbles of the yard. His head turns sideways; his eyes are turned towards the gate, as if someone might arrive to help him out.

> If he squints sideways, with his right eye he can see that the stitching of his father's boot is unravelling.

**Cromwell's abilities** (the self-made man rendered as catalog of competencies):

> He can draft a contract, train a falcon, draw a map, stop a street fight, furnish a house and fix a jury. He will quote you a nice point in the old authors, from Plato to Plautus and back again. He knows new poetry, and can say it in Italian. He works all hours, first up and last to bed.

**Political dialogue as lethal game** (every conversation a move):

> "But the king, alone, has no son. Whose fault is that?"
> "God's?"
> "Nearer than God?"
> "The queen?"

**Mantel on atmosphere and consciousness** (from literary criticism):

Mantel's narrative uses third-person limited-omniscient in present tense, creating the effect of inhabiting a calculating mind in real time. The constant "he" pronoun — almost always referring to Cromwell himself — makes the reader do the work of distinguishing subject from object. Critics describe her prose as having "a studied wildness of image and metaphor, like a falcon hooded and trained for the pageant field." She fought her publisher to keep the present tense, changing it back repeatedly through proofs.

**Key technique**: The past rendered not as nostalgia or distance but as a living, breathing, politically dangerous present. History is not known — it is happening. Mantel writes the material before the metaphor: the crack, the plaster, the horsehair binding. The meaning arrives unbidden.

**Quotes that capture her sensibility**:

> "It is the absence of facts that frightens people: the gap you open, into which they pour their fears, fantasies, desires."

> "Some of these things are true and some of them lies. But they are all good stories."

> "Fortitude. ... It means fixity of purpose. It means endurance. It means having the strength to live with what constrains you."

### Sarah Waters — The Little Stranger

**Opening passage** (the outsider's first view of the great house):

> "I first saw Hundreds Hall when I was ten years old. It was the summer after the war, and the Ayreses still had most of their money then, were still big people in the district. The event was an Empire Day fete: I stood with a line of other village children making a Boy Scout salute while Mrs Ayres and the Colonel went past us, handing out commemorative medals; afterwards we sat to tea with our parents at long tables on what I suppose was the south lawn."

**The house as ruin** (architecture encoding decline):

> "The passage made another ninety-degree turn — to the left, this time — and here at last a door stood properly ajar, letting out a blurred wedge of sunlight. It led to the room, Caroline told me, in which the family spent most of their time, and which had been known for years and years as 'the little parlour'. Of course 'little', as I'd already realised, was a relative term at Hundreds Hall. The room was about thirty feet deep and twenty wide, and it was decorated in a rather hectic manner, with more moulded detail on its ceiling and walls, and an imposing marble fireplace. As in the passage, however, much of the detail was chipped or cracked, or had been lost completely. The floorboards, humped and creaking, were covered with overlapping threadbare rugs. A sagging sofa was half hidden by tartan blankets. Two worn velvet wing-backed chairs stood close to the hearth, and sitting on the floor beside one of them was a florid Victorian chamber-pot, filled with water for the dog."

**Key techniques from literary criticism**:

Waters's novels use atmospheric dread building beneath period surfaces. Her queer subtexts emerge through apparently conventional narratives — desire coded into glances, gestures, the arrangement of domestic space. Her fiction "silently inserts her depiction of nineteenth-century female homosexuality into our cultural memory of Victorian fiction." The house is always a character — architecture encoding social hierarchies, desire, and repression. Fingersmith's unreliable narration "poses epistemological questions about the reliability of what we think we know, with reading revealed as an unreliable means of attaining authentic knowledge."

**The Little Stranger's central ambiguity**: Dr. Faraday's class resentment, his longing for the house, his desire for the daughter — is itself the haunting. The supernatural is a screen for the social. The house is not being destroyed by a poltergeist. It is being destroyed by history.

### Wolf Hall — Structural/Thematic Source

- The court as puzzle: every conversation is a move in a game with lethal stakes
- The self-made man navigating among the born-to-rule
- History known to the reader but not to the protagonist: dramatic irony as engine
- Power exercised through silence, patience, strategic withholding
- The material fact before the metaphor

### The Little Stranger — Thematic Source

- A declining country house and the class anxieties it embodies
- The possibly supernatural as metaphor for social disintegration
- The outsider admitted to the family who may be destroying it
- The gap between what is felt and what remains
- Architecture as emotional record

## Wikipedia Random Concept Mining

**20 concepts encountered** (from random/serendipitous searching):

1. **Pteridomania** — the Victorian fern craze (1850-1890), with Wardian cases in drawing rooms
2. **Ornamental hermits** — real humans paid to live in garden grottoes on aristocratic estates
3. **Window tax** (1696-1851) — homes with bricked-up windows to avoid taxation; architectural scars still visible
4. **Spite houses** — buildings constructed to annoy neighbors; architecture as revenge
5. **L'Inconnue de la Seine** — the death mask of an unknown drowned woman, reproduced across Paris parlors
6. **Deodand** — English common law (11th c. to 1846): any object that caused a person's death was forfeited to the Crown; a coroner's jury decided which chattel was responsible
7. **Calke Abbey** — a country house where rooms were untouched since the 1880s, preserved in decay by the National Trust; "the un-stately home"
8. **Brodsworth Hall** — traces of economizing: reduced fireplace openings, gaps on walls where sold-off paintings hung, threadbare carpets, curtains cut and turned
9. **Probate inventories** — legally required 1529-1782: room-by-room lists of a dead person's possessions, compiled by neighbors within days of death
10. **British country house contents auctions** — Mentmore Towers (1977), Stowe House (1848), Hamilton Palace (1882); high-quality catalogs with provenance and estimated prices
11. **Victorian coroner's inquests** — held in pubs because they had tables large enough for a body and room for a jury of twelve; the coroner and jurors required to "view" the body
12. **Daguerreotype** — first practical photographic process (1839); a single, unreproducible image on silvered copper
13. **Wardian case** — sealed glass terrarium; a "little world within a world" in the polluted Victorian drawing room
14. **Antimacassar** — cloth placed on chair backs to protect from macassar oil; a small act of domestic preservation
15. **Hallmarks on silver** — assay marks, date letters, maker's marks; each piece of silver carries its complete biography in stamped symbols
16. **Ha-ha** — a hidden boundary ditch in estate gardens; landscape that conceals its own borders
17. **Quincunx** — five-point planting pattern; Sir Thomas Browne's 1658 essay found the pattern everywhere in nature as evidence of divine order
18. **Herbarium** — Victorian women's pressed-flower albums; a respectable way to "examine the natural world" while restricted by sex
19. **Foxing** — age-related brown spots on paper, named for the color of a fox; the visible evidence of time's chemical work on documents
20. **Method of loci** — the ancient memory technique of storing information in rooms of an imagined building; the house as archive of consciousness

### Selected Concepts for Integration

**Concept 1: Deodand** (object forfeited because it caused death)

This concept will be architecturally central to the mystery. In the 1889 timeline, the death in the house triggers an inquiry not just into who is responsible but into which *object* — which piece of the house itself — was the instrument of death. The coroner's jury must determine the deodand. This transforms the house from setting into suspect. The cataloguer in 1947, listing objects for sale, is unknowingly handling items that were once legally implicated in a death. One object that should be in the inventory is missing — the deodand, forfeited to the Crown decades ago. Its absence is the first clue.

**Concept 2: Probate inventory / room-by-room listing**

The 1947 timeline is structured around the act of cataloguing — a woman moving room by room through a dying house, listing what remains. This mirrors the probate inventory tradition: a legal documentation of the dead's possessions, compiled by neighbors within days of death. The cataloguer's work becomes a probate inventory for the house itself, which is dying. Each room she enters becomes a chapter, and the gaps in the inventory (objects missing, spaces where things were) become the negative space where the mystery lives.

## Story Design

### Title

**Inventory of What the House Removed**

(Echoes the meeting's language about the "inventory of removed objects" and "inventory of removed desires." Not starting with "The." Uses a possessive/active construction — the house as agent, not object. Distinct from existing titles. The word "inventory" connects to the cataloguing structure; "removed" carries both physical and emotional valence — objects removed from rooms, people removed from the house, desires removed from the record.)

### Premise

In 1947, a woman named Nell Fosse arrives at Brydworth Hall in South Yorkshire to catalogue its contents for auction — the last act before the estate is broken up. Room by room, she compiles an inventory, but the gaps trouble her: objects that should exist based on the architecture (shadow-marks on walls, wear-patterns on shelves) but don't. In 1889, in the same house, a woman named Ada Bowen — neither family nor servant, the paid companion to the elder daughter — navigates the household's social hierarchies while harboring a devotion to Constance Lacey that has no language, no social form, and no future. When a man dies in the house and the coroner's jury convenes in the village pub to determine both cause and deodand, Ada discovers that the official story of the death is a construction — and that the version of events the family needs to be true requires her silence, her complicity, and ultimately her disappearance from the record. The dual timelines converge not through revelation but through rhyme: in both periods, the house selects what it remembers and what it forgets, and the women who document its contents become instruments of that selection.

### Protagonist

**1947 timeline: Nell Fosse**
- Age 34
- Employed by Marchwick & Sons, a Leeds auction house, as a valuations clerk — one of very few women in the firm, retained after the war when the men came back because she is faster and more accurate than any of them
- WANT: To complete the Brydworth catalogue perfectly, proving her competence and securing a permanent position
- NEED: To stop treating precision as a substitute for feeling — her meticulousness is a defense against grief (her younger sister died in a V-2 strike on Deptford in 1944, and Nell responded by becoming more orderly, more controlled, more exact, as if she could catalogue away the chaos)
- FLAW: She mistakes thoroughness for understanding. She believes if she records everything accurately enough, the truth will emerge from the data. It won't. The truth at Brydworth exists only in what was never recorded.
- Physical specificity: Smokes Woodbines, carries a leather-bound notebook with pre-ruled columns, has developed a callus on her right middle finger from years of writing. Left-handed, which causes smudging she has learned to compensate for. Wears her dead sister's wristwatch, which runs four minutes fast.

**1889 timeline: Ada Bowen**
- Age 26
- Lady's companion to Constance Lacey, elder daughter of the Brydworth household. Ada's father was a schoolmaster who died insolvent; her mother's family had a distant connection to the Laceys. She was placed at Brydworth as a charity case disguised as a social arrangement.
- WANT: To remain at Brydworth — the only place where her life has any structure, any meaning, any proximity to the person she loves
- NEED: To recognize that her devotion to Constance is also a form of captivity, and that the house's promise of belonging is the mechanism of her erasure
- FLAW: She confuses loyalty with love. She will do anything to protect Constance — including suppressing what she knows about the death — because she cannot separate her desire for Constance from her desire for the life Constance represents.
- Physical specificity: Keeps her hair pinned tighter than fashion requires. Has a habit of standing with her hands behind her back, as if waiting for instruction. Plays piano competently but never brilliantly — the performance of accomplishment without its conviction. Has memorized the smell of every room in Brydworth.

### Structure (Dual Timeline — Architecturally Central)

Both timelines run in present tense, as Mantel advised in the meeting. The house does not distinguish between 1889 and 1947. Transitions occur through shared spaces — the same room, the same staircase, the same crack above the lintel. The timelines are NOT interleaved evenly; the 1889 sections are denser and more numerous in the first half, the 1947 sections dominate the second half, so that the story moves from fullness to emptiness, from the house populated to the house stripped.

**BEAT 1: Opening — The Catalogue Begins (1947)**
Nell arrives at Brydworth Hall. The house is nearly empty; the family's last representative (an elderly solicitor acting on behalf of the estate) shows her in and leaves. She begins in the entrance hall, noting what is present, what is absent. The wallpaper is sun-faded in rectangles where paintings hung. The floorboards show wear-paths to rooms that are now locked. She opens her notebook and begins to write. An antimacassar, yellowed and stiff, still clings to the back of a single remaining chair. She catalogues it: "Item 1. One crocheted antimacassar, soiled, no commercial value." Present tense. The precision of the work is the opening's texture.

**BEAT 2: The House Full (1889)**
Cut to Brydworth in its late-Victorian prime — not opulent, but dense with use. Ada Bowen moves through the same entrance hall, which is now papered, lit, cluttered with coats and umbrellas and a Wardian case of ferns on the hall table. She is passing through on her way to Constance's room. The household is in motion: Mr. Lacey is meeting with his agent about tenant rents, Mrs. Lacey is planning a dinner, the cook is having a dispute with the butcher's boy. Ada's position is made clear through small exclusions — she eats with the family but enters through the side door; she is invited to sit but never to initiate conversation; Constance calls her "dear Ada" in private and "Miss Bowen" when guests are present. We see Ada's devotion to Constance rendered through material attentiveness — she knows which shawl Constance prefers, which tea, which route through the garden. It is a love that speaks entirely through objects.

**BEAT 3: The Death (1889) / The Missing Object (1947)**
1889: A visiting cousin, Mr. Gerald Holt, dies in the house. He is found in the morning room, dead in a chair, with signs suggesting a fall. The local coroner, Dr. Bewes, convenes an inquest at the Dog and Partridge in the village. The jury must view the body and then determine the cause and, crucially, the deodand — which object caused the death. The family argues it was the chair (which they would then forfeit, a negligible loss). The evidence suggests something more complicated: a piece of silver — a heavy candlestick — may have been involved. Ada knows something she cannot say: she was in the morning room the night Gerald died, not because she was involved in his death, but because she was returning from Constance's room at an hour that would expose the nature of their intimacy. She saw who was in the room. She saw the candlestick.

1947: Nell's catalogue reaches the morning room. The room is nearly bare, but the mantelpiece still holds marks — twin circular impressions in the dust — where paired candlesticks once stood. Her inventory of the household silver (cross-referenced with a Marchwick manifest from a previous valuation in 1912) shows only one candlestick in the lot. The matching piece is not listed, not in the house, not accounted for. She makes a note: "One silver candlestick, hallmarked Sheffield 1847, lion passant, missing — presumed deodand?" She writes the word without fully understanding its legal history. But she underlines it.

**BEAT 4: The Escalation (1889) / The Architecture Speaks (1947)**
1889: The inquest unfolds. Gerald's death is complicated by debts he owed to the family, by his known habit of drinking, by his reputation. The family constructs a story: Gerald fell, struck his head on the fender, died of the injury. The chair is declared deodand — a gesture that costs them nothing. Ada watches the construction of this official narrative with the calculating eye of Cromwell-consciousness (Mantel). She sees the family's solicitor coaching witnesses. She sees Mrs. Lacey rehearse her testimony at the dining table. She sees Mr. Lacey signal to the coroner — the two men are lodge brothers. Every conversation is a move in a game, and Ada tracks each move from her position of maximum observation and minimum power.

But Ada also discovers something the family doesn't know she knows: Constance was in the morning room that night too. Not at the moment of death, but before — arguing with Gerald. About what, Ada doesn't know. But Constance's presence in that room, at that hour, threatens the family's constructed story. And Constance asks Ada — not in words, but in the way Waters's characters ask, through a look, a touch on the wrist, a silence that is louder than speech — to say nothing. Ada's loyalty is the instrument of her own erasure: she will protect Constance by becoming invisible, by ensuring that the record of that night contains no mention of either of them.

1947: Nell moves deeper into the house. She discovers that certain rooms have been sealed — not locked but physically blocked, with furniture pushed against doors, carpets nailed over thresholds. In one sealed room (a small sitting room off the morning room), she finds a herbarium — a pressed-flower album — with the name "A. Bowen" inscribed on the flyleaf in faded ink. She also finds a stack of letters, unsigned, addressed to "C." The letters are not love letters in any recognizable form — they are descriptions of rooms. Detailed, precise, obsessive descriptions of the house's contents, room by room, as if the writer were creating an inventory decades before Nell's arrival. But the descriptions include things that aren't there anymore, things Nell can see the ghosts of in the architecture. The letters are Ada's memory palace — a record of the house she lived in, the house that held her and Constance, written after she left (or was made to leave), as if by cataloguing what she remembered she could preserve the life she lost.

**BEAT 5: Crisis (1889) / Convergence (1947)**
1889: The inquest reaches its verdict. Gerald Holt's death is ruled accidental. The chair is declared deodand and forfeited. The silver candlestick is never mentioned. Ada's silence has served the family perfectly — she has made herself useful by making herself absent from the record. But in the aftermath, something shifts. Constance begins to withdraw. Not from Ada specifically, but from the version of their intimacy that existed before the death. The secret they now share is not a bond but a barrier. Constance is going to marry — a suitable man, a neighboring landowner's son. The engagement is announced at dinner. Ada sits at the table and congratulates her. The moment is rendered with Waters's quality of atmospheric dread: the words are correct, the gestures appropriate, and the horror is entirely in the gap between what is said and what is felt.

Ada does one thing before she is eventually let go (given a small sum and a reference letter, erased with efficiency). She takes the silver candlestick. Not to keep — she has nowhere to keep it — but to remove it from the house. The candlestick is the deodand that should have been, the object that carries the truth of what happened to Gerald. She takes it because if she cannot speak, she can at least ensure that the house's inventory is incomplete. That the official record will have a gap that someone, someday, might notice.

1947: Nell sits in the sealed room with Ada's herbarium and the letters. She reads the descriptions of rooms she has been cataloguing — the same rooms, described from the inside by someone who lived in them, loved in them, was erased from them. She does not solve the mystery of Gerald Holt's death. She does not determine who struck him or why. But she understands, with the precision of a woman who has spent her life reading the shapes of absence, that the catalogue she is compiling is not a list of what remains. It is a list of what the house chose to remember. And Ada's letters — the shadow-catalogue, the inventory written from exile — record what the house chose to forget.

Nell does something Nell-like: she adds Ada's herbarium to the auction catalogue. Item 247. One pressed-flower album, mid-Victorian, inscribed "A. Bowen," some foxing to pages, moderate condition. She gives it a value. She enters it into the record.

### Ending (Deliberately Unresolved)

The story ends in 1947. Nell finishes her catalogue. The house will be sold, broken up, its contents dispersed to auction rooms across the north of England. She stands in the entrance hall where she began. The antimacassar is gone — she packed it herself, wrapped in tissue paper, lot number 1. The house is completely empty now, and emptied, it reveals what was always there beneath the furniture and the paintings and the lives: the architecture itself. The bricked-up window (window tax, 1696, never reopened). The crack above the lintel that no one stopped seeing. The worn step where ten thousand feet have passed.

She closes her notebook. She has catalogued everything the house contains, which is nothing, which is everything.

**What is NOT resolved**: We never learn exactly what happened between Constance and Gerald in the morning room. We never learn whether the candlestick was the instrument of Gerald's death or merely a witness to it. We never learn what happened to Ada after she left Brydworth. The letters stop. The herbarium is the last physical trace of her existence.

**What IS resolved**: Nell has entered Ada into the record. Not her story, not her love, not her silence — but her herbarium, her pressed flowers, her name in the catalogue. A presence in the inventory that is also an absence, because the catalogue can hold the object but not the woman who made it.

### Emotional Trajectory

**Start**: Clinical detachment. The reader enters through Nell's professional eye — the house is a job, a list of objects to be valued. Curiosity is procedural. In 1889, Ada's situation feels familiar (the companion in the great house) but the present tense makes it immediate, not nostalgic.

**Middle**: Growing unease. The gaps in the inventory begin to feel deliberate. The 1889 sections reveal a household constructing a lie with the precision of a legal argument. The reader begins to understand that the mystery is not whodunit but who benefits from the official story. The queer subtext between Ada and Constance builds through objects, through the material language of devotion.

**End**: A feeling that is not resolution but recognition. The reader understands the shape of what happened without possessing the facts. The feeling is grief — not for Gerald, whose death was the inciting event, but for Ada, whose life was the real casualty. And for Nell, who finds Ada's ghost not in the house but in the catalogue, in the act of documenting what was lost.

### Key Scenes (5)

1. **Nell cataloguing the morning room (1947)**: She finds the paired dust-marks on the mantelpiece, cross-references the 1912 manifest, discovers the missing candlestick. This scene must feel like detective work performed through inventory — the mystery emerging from discrepancy, not from clue-hunting. The room is nearly bare; the evidence is all negative space.

2. **Ada at the inquest in the Dog and Partridge (1889)**: The coroner's jury views the body. Ada stands at the back of the pub's upper room. The jury is twelve men. The family solicitor presents the narrative. Ada watches the construction of the official version — the angles of testimony, the rehearsed hesitations, the silence where she could speak but doesn't. Present tense. The body is on the table. Everyone is performing.

3. **Ada and Constance in Constance's room, the night of the death (1889)**: The scene BEFORE the death, rendered after it. This is the scene the reader has been waiting for — what was Ada doing in the morning room? The answer: she was returning from Constance's room, where they had been together in whatever way their relationship took form that night (ambiguous — the reader is given the shape of intimacy without its explicit content). The walk back through the darkened house. The morning room door ajar. The sound, or the absence of sound.

4. **Nell finding Ada's letters in the sealed room (1947)**: The discovery of the shadow-catalogue. Nell reads descriptions of rooms she has just catalogued — the same rooms, but full. Full of objects, full of people, full of felt experience. The letters are not addressed to her, but they function as a communication across time: one cataloguer speaking to another. This scene must NOT announce its own significance. Nell reads. She notes. She adds the herbarium to the catalogue.

5. **Constance's engagement dinner (1889)**: Ada sits at the family table. The announcement is made. She congratulates Constance. The scene is rendered with excruciating social precision — who speaks, who stands, who touches whom. The horror is domestic, not gothic. Constance does not look at Ada. Not avoiding her — simply not seeing her. Ada has already begun to disappear.

### Formula Integration Plan

**Hilary Mantel (Author A)** — Most visible in: **Ada at the inquest scene**. The coroner's jury, the political maneuvering, the construction of an official narrative through carefully managed testimony — this is Cromwell-consciousness applied to a Victorian drawing room. Ada watches the family's solicitor the way Cromwell watches Wolsey's enemies: tracking each move, understanding the game, calculating the cost of silence vs. speech. Present tense. Dense, kinetic detail. The material fact of the body on the table before any question of meaning.

**Sarah Waters (Author B)** — Most visible in: **Nell discovering Ada's letters / the sealed room**. The house as character, architecture encoding social hierarchies, the queer subtext emerging through apparently conventional domestic detail. The letters themselves are Waters-esque — desire coded as description, love expressed as inventory. The sealed room is the gothic heart of the story: what was locked away, what atmosphere builds in the spaces the family chose to close.

**Wolf Hall (Work X)** — Most visible in: **The political structure of the inquest and its aftermath**. Every conversation is a move in a game with lethal stakes. Ada is the self-made woman (the outsider, the companion, the person who lives by her wits among the born-to-rule) navigating among people who hold power by birthright. The dramatic irony: the reader knows (from the 1947 timeline) that the official story is a construction, that something was removed from the record. The 1889 sections show us how and why.

**The Little Stranger (Work Y)** — Most visible in: **The overall architecture of decline and the house as agent**. Brydworth Hall in 1947 IS Hundreds Hall — the declining country house, the class anxieties it embodies, the war and death duties that stripped it. Ada is a version of Faraday: the outsider admitted to the family who may be — not destroying it, but enabling its self-destruction through her complicity. The possibly supernatural is replaced here by the possibly conspiratorial: the house may not be haunted, but it is certainly selective in what it remembers.

### Risk Card: Dual Timeline — Architectural Integration

The dual timeline is not decorative; it IS the story's subject. The 1889 timeline shows how a version of events is constructed. The 1947 timeline shows what that construction looks like decades later — what survives, what is erased, what can be read in the negative space. The connection between timelines is material (the same rooms, the same objects, the same crack above the lintel) and structural (both women are performing acts of documentation — Ada's letters, Nell's catalogue — that are also acts of power, choosing what to include and what to omit). The timelines do not converge on a revelation. They converge on a recognition: that every inventory is an act of selection, and every act of selection is a kind of violence.

Both timelines are in present tense, collapsing the distance between 1889 and 1947. The house holds both times simultaneously.

## Additional Notes for the Writer

### Voice
Present tense, third-person limited. In 1889 sections, close to Ada's consciousness — Mantel's technique of inhabiting a calculating mind. In 1947 sections, close to Nell's consciousness — more precise, more clinical, but with the same quality of attention. The prose should be material first: objects, textures, the weight of a candlestick, the smell of a closed room. Metaphor should arrive from the material, never the reverse.

### Period Detail
1889: Late Victorian. The house is not grand — it is comfortable, provincial, the kind of house where the family has lived for three generations without either ascending or declining. Think Brodsworth Hall, not Downton. The Wardian case in the hall, the antimacassars on the chairs, the silver hallmarked in Sheffield. The cook disputes with the butcher's boy. The coroner's inquest is held in a pub because there is no other room large enough.

1947: Postwar austerity. The house has survived two wars but not the death duties. Nell travels by train from Leeds, arriving at a station where the platform has not been repaired since the Blitz. The house is cold — no fuel for heating. The electricity works in some rooms but not others. Nell wears her overcoat indoors.

### What to Avoid
- Do NOT have Nell "solve" the mystery. She finds the shape of what happened, not the facts.
- Do NOT have a ghost scene or supernatural event. The dread is social, not gothic.
- Do NOT have Ada and Constance declare their feelings. The love exists in gestures, objects, domestic attentiveness. It is never named.
- Do NOT announce the theme. The gap between what was felt and what remains must exist in the prose, never in the narrator's commentary.
- Do NOT make Gerald sympathetic. He is a presence, an obstacle, a body. His death matters for what it reveals about the household, not for the loss of him.
- Do NOT resolve the question of who struck Gerald (or whether anyone did). The story's refusal to answer this question IS its answer: the mystery was never about the death but about the silence that followed it.

### Tone
The meeting nailed it: "Omissions, not gaps." The tone is one of deliberate, controlled withholding. The writer knows everything — who struck Gerald, what Ada and Constance's relationship looked like in its physical specifics, what happened to Ada after she left — and chooses what to show. The reader should feel the shape of what is withheld, the way Nell feels the shape of the missing candlestick in the dust on the mantelpiece.
