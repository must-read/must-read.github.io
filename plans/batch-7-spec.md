# Batch 7 — Combination Specs

**Target genres**: fantasy (4→5), gothic-fiction (4→5), mystery-thriller (4→5), romance (4→5), literary-fiction (7→8)

## 1. Fantasy / Grimdark

**AuthorA**: Joe Abercrombie — Gritty, visceral, darkly funny; grimdark realism; morally grey characters rendered with cynical precision
**AuthorB**: Patrick Rothfuss — Lyrical, almost poetic prose; musicality of language; romantic-epic sensibility

**WorkX**: "Rashomon" (Akutagawa/Kurosawa) — Structure: multiple perspectives on the same event, each self-serving and unreliable; truth as composite
**WorkY**: "Macbeth" (Shakespeare) — Themes: ambition as self-destruction; prophecy as trap; the crown that poisons the one who wears it

**Tension**: Abercrombie's cynical, blood-and-mud prose against Rothfuss's lyrical romanticism. The grimdark soldier rendered with musical language; the beautiful sentence that describes an ugly truth.

**targetWordCount**: 6200
**readingTimeMinutes**: 25
**riskCard**: none

## 2. Gothic Fiction / Gothic Romance

**AuthorA**: Daphne du Maurier — Gothic atmosphere; unnamed heroines; houses as characters; romantic dread; landscape as mood
**AuthorB**: Mary Shelley — The horror of creation; science as transgression; the creature's eloquence; Romantic-era philosophical horror

**WorkX**: "Wuthering Heights" (Emily Brontë) — Structure: frame narrative; generational echo; the story told through layers of mediation and unreliable retelling
**WorkY**: "Jane Eyre" (Charlotte Brontë) — Themes: the heroine's independence within gothic constraint; the house's secret; love that must be earned through moral trial

**Tension**: Du Maurier's unnamed, uncertain heroine navigating a house that refuses to reveal itself, meets Shelley's philosophical horror of what has been created and cannot be undone. The gothic romance where the beloved may be the monster.

**targetWordCount**: 4200
**readingTimeMinutes**: 17
**riskCard**: UNRELIABLE NARRATOR — The heroine's perception of the house and its inhabitants is compromised. The reader detects what she cannot admit.

---

## 3. Mystery-Thriller / Locked Room

**AuthorA**: Arthur Conan Doyle — Watson's warm, admiring narration; Baker Street fog; deductive set-pieces; Victorian adventure
**AuthorB**: John le Carré — Dense, bureaucratic realism; moral exhaustion of espionage; layered, literary prose for the genre

**WorkX**: "And Then There Were None" (Agatha Christie) — Structure: closed circle; characters under mutual suspicion; elimination logic
**WorkY**: "The Name of the Rose" (Umberto Eco) — Themes: knowledge as dangerous; the labyrinth of institutional secrecy; the intellectual detective confronting dogma

**Tension**: Doyle's warmth and adventure-spirit meets le Carré's institutional fatigue. The detective who solves puzzles for the joy of it trapped in a system that doesn't want puzzles solved.

**targetWordCount**: 5800
**readingTimeMinutes**: 23
**riskCard**: WITHHELD INFORMATION — A key piece of information the reader expects (the method, the motive, or the identity) is deliberately never provided. The absence shapes the story.

---

## 4. Romance / Romantic Suspense

**AuthorA**: Alyssa Cole — Genre-blending romance; sharp political awareness; diverse settings from Harlem to sci-fi
**AuthorB**: Dennis Lehane — Working-class voice; emotional devastation beneath crime narratives; noir with heart

**WorkX**: "Gone Girl" (Gillian Flynn) — Structure: dual perspectives revealing different versions of the same relationship; the truth lives in the gap
**WorkY**: "Rebecca" (Daphne du Maurier) — Themes: the shadow of the previous partner; domestic space as threat; the relationship haunted by what came before

**Tension**: Cole's politically sharp, warm romance instincts against Lehane's noir emotional devastation. A love story where the danger isn't external — it's in the history the lovers carry.

**targetWordCount**: 3800
**readingTimeMinutes**: 15
**riskCard**: none

---

## 5. Literary Fiction / Campus-Academic Novel

**AuthorA**: Vladimir Nabokov — Ornate, playful prose; unreliable narrators; dazzling wordplay and linguistic pyrotechnics
**AuthorB**: Rachel Cusk — Austere, essayistic autofiction; observation as narrative; stripped-down dialogue frames

**WorkX**: "Lucky Jim" (Kingsley Amis) — Structure: comic misfit navigating institutional absurdity; escalating social disaster
**WorkY**: "Stoner" (John Williams) — Themes: the quiet devastation of an academic life; the institution as both refuge and prison; the life that doesn't amount to what was hoped

**Tension**: Nabokov's ornate verbal fireworks against Cusk's austere observation. The campus novel as both comedy and elegy — the professor who performs brilliance while living smallness.

**targetWordCount**: 5500
**readingTimeMinutes**: 22
**riskCard**: none
