# Story Plan: Salt and Mortar

**Genre**: Gothic Fiction / Gothic Romance
**Combination ID**: goth-gothicromance-001
**Target word count**: 4,200 words (window: 3,900–4,500)
**Risk card**: UNRELIABLE NARRATOR — The narrator is lying, mistaken, or self-deceived about a key element of the story. The reader should be able to detect the unreliability through contradictions, gaps, or tonal shifts — but the narrator never acknowledges it directly.

---

## Style Sources

**Author A — Daphne du Maurier**: Gothic atmosphere; unnamed heroines; houses as characters; romantic dread; landscape as mood. Du Maurier's narrators are women who have constructed versions of reality they can survive inside. The unnamed narrator of *Rebecca* lies to herself about the nature of her husband's feeling for the dead wife — but she knows she is lying. There is a layer of awareness beneath the performance, a choosing of the comfortable version you can feel her choosing. The landscape participates in the deception: the drive to Manderley overgrown, the house standing inviolate while the gardens are consumed, nature reclaiming what was ordered. Du Maurier's prose moves between precise physical observation and dream-logic, always grounded in specific texture — the smell of azaleas, the sound of the sea through an open window, the weight of silverware at dinner.

**Author B — Mary Shelley**: The horror of creation; philosophical horror in the Romantic tradition; the eloquence of the misunderstood. Shelley's creature is not a monster who speaks — he is an orator who has been made monstrous. "Remember that I am thy creature; I ought to be thy Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed." The horror in Frankenstein is not the body on the slab but the mind behind the yellow eyes: articulate, wounded, making an argument the creator cannot refute. Shelley's contribution to this story is the idea of creation as transgression — the husband who has *made* something, not built it but made it the way one makes a world for someone, curated, controlled. The vivarium. And the question of whether love can be an act of creation that is simultaneously an act of confinement.

## Structural/Thematic Sources

**Work X — *Wuthering Heights* by Emily Bronte**: Frame narrative; generational echo; the story told through layers of mediation and unreliable retelling. Lockwood hears Nelly Dean's account, Nelly Dean shapes and edits what she witnessed, the reader receives a twice-filtered version of events. The dream sequence — Lockwood reaching through the broken window, his "fingers closed on the fingers of a little, ice-cold hand" — is the frame narrative cracking open, the past refusing its mediation. For this story: the heroine is telling her own account, but the telling itself is a room in the house, another space the husband has prepared for her. The generational echo comes through a previous woman — not a ghost, not a warning, just someone who lived in these rooms before and left. The scratched-out inscription. The question the reader should ask is not what was scratched out but why the heroine does not ask.

**Work Y — *Jane Eyre* by Charlotte Bronte**: The heroine's independence within gothic constraint; the house's secret; love that must be earned through moral trial. Jane stands before Rochester and declares herself his equal despite being powerless in every material sense. But for this story, the heroine's independence is inverted: it is invisible, perhaps even to herself. She chooses which curtains to open. She walks the garden in a pattern she designed. She prepares her toast a particular way. These are not grand assertions of selfhood — they are the last territory she holds. And the crucial difference from Jane Eyre: Jane leaves Thornfield. She walks onto the moor with nothing. She nearly dies. Our heroine never leaves. She stays, and she narrates her staying as contentment. The Jane Eyre question — whether love must be earned through moral trial — becomes whether love can be the moral trial itself.

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Daphne du Maurier — *Rebecca* (1938)

**Source**: Author A
**Origin**: Opening chapter
**Why representative**: Demonstrates du Maurier's fusion of dream and precise observation, the unnamed narrator's constructed reality, and landscape as emotional architecture.

The novel opens with one of the most famous sentences in English fiction: "Last night I dreamt I went to Manderley again." The narrator describes standing by an iron gate, finding "a padlock and a chain upon the gate," seeing the lodge uninhabited — "no smoke came from the chimney, and the little lattice windows gaped forlorn." She passes "like a spirit through the barrier" and describes the drive overgrown, the woods "always a menace" having "triumphed in the end," beeches with "white, naked limbs" creating a vault overhead. The house itself "stood inviolate, untouched" while the gardens were consumed by vegetation. The chapter ends with: "Manderley was ours no longer. Manderley was no more."

What matters for the writer: du Maurier's narrator is constructing this dream-version of Manderley as a way of not thinking about the real Manderley — the one that burned. Every detail of the overgrown drive is precise (the padlock, the lattice windows, the naked beeches) but the precision serves evasion. She describes what she sees in order to avoid describing what happened. The landscape does her emotional work. This is the technique our narrator must employ: accurate observation as a form of concealment.

Key stylistic features for the writer to absorb:
- First-person retrospective narration that moves between dream and specificity
- Landscape anthropomorphized without becoming allegory — the woods "triumphed," the windows "gaped"
- The house as character: it "stood inviolate" as if making a choice
- Physical details that carry emotional weight without the narrator naming the emotion
- The gap between what the narrator shows and what she means
- Sentences that sound like calm description but are structured as confession

### Sample 2: Mary Shelley — *Frankenstein* (1818)

**Source**: Author B
**Origin**: Chapter 10, the creature's confrontation with Victor on the glacier
**Why representative**: Demonstrates Shelley's philosophical eloquence in the voice of the created being, the sublime natural setting as stage for moral argument, and the horror that comes from articulation rather than muteness.

Victor encounters the creature on the Mer de Glace glacier near Mont Blanc. The creature speaks: "Remember that I am thy creature; I ought to be thy Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed. Everywhere I see bliss, from which I alone am irrevocably excluded." And: "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend. Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous." The creature's argument is devastating precisely because it is reasonable: he did not ask to be made; he was made and then abandoned; his monstrosity is a consequence of his creator's failures, not his own nature.

The setting matters: Victor ascends the glacier seeking consolation in sublime nature, and instead finds the creature — as if the landscape itself produced the confrontation. The ice, the crevasses, the desolate beauty. The creature moves with "superhuman speed" across terrain that nearly kills Victor. He belongs to this landscape in a way Victor never can.

Key stylistic features for the writer to absorb:
- Elevated, Miltonic diction in the creature's speech — biblical cadence applied to a secular argument
- The created being as more eloquent than the creator
- Natural sublimity as moral landscape — the glacier is not decoration but argument
- The horror of being understood too well by something you have made
- The philosophical question embedded in the scene: can the creator be held responsible for the creation's suffering?
- For this story: the husband's creation (the house-as-vivarium) has its own eloquence. The house argues for itself. The heroine reads its argument and finds it persuasive.

### Sample 3: Emily Bronte — *Wuthering Heights* (1847)

**Source**: Work X
**Origin**: Chapter 3, Lockwood's dream of Catherine's ghost
**Why representative**: Demonstrates the frame narrative cracking under pressure — the past reaching through the layers of mediation to seize the present narrator.

Lockwood, sleeping in Catherine's old room, dreams of reaching through a broken window to silence a branch, and instead "my fingers closed on the fingers of a little, ice-cold hand." A voice moans: "Let me in — let me in!" and identifies itself as Catherine Linton, declaring "I'm come home: I'd lost my way on the moor!" Lockwood's response is brutal — he rubs the ghost's wrist against the broken glass until blood soaks the bedsheets. Then Heathcliff arrives, and Lockwood witnesses a man who has spent years constructing a fortress of cruelty suddenly break open: Heathcliff wrenches the window wide and calls out into the dark, "Come in! come in! Cathy, do come."

Key stylistic features for the writer to absorb:
- The frame narrative exists to be violated — the rational outsider (Lockwood) encounters what the story cannot contain
- Multiple layers of telling: Lockwood tells us, Nelly tells Lockwood, the ghosts tell themselves
- The unreliability is structural, not psychological — Nelly shapes her account according to her own sympathies, Lockwood condenses "in her own words, only a little condensed"
- Violence and tenderness in the same gesture: Lockwood's cruelty to the ghost-child, Heathcliff's desperate invitation
- For this story: the heroine's account is another layer of mediation. She tells us what happened "in her own words, only a little condensed." The question is what she has condensed away.

### Sample 4: Charlotte Bronte — *Jane Eyre* (1847)

**Source**: Work Y
**Origin**: Chapter 11 (arrival at Thornfield) and Chapter 27 (departure)
**Why representative**: Demonstrates the heroine's voice — direct, morally certain, observant — and the house as a place that keeps secrets the heroine intuits but cannot name.

Jane arrives at Thornfield and observes: "It is a very strange sensation to inexperienced youth to feel itself quite alone in the world, cut adrift from every connection." She surveys the three-story manor with "battlements round the top" and a "grey front" set against "a rookery, whose cawing tenants were now on the wing." She learns the house belongs to someone called Rochester, not to the housekeeper she'd assumed owned it. She notes: "I thought that a fairer era of life was beginning for me, one that was to have its flowers and pleasures, as well as its thorns and toils."

In Chapter 27, after discovering Rochester's secret (the mad wife in the attic), Jane departs. She addresses the reader directly — her signature rhetorical move — breaking the fourth wall not for intimacy but for moral witness. Jane's departure is the moral center of the novel: she proves she can survive outside the house, and therefore her eventual return is free choice, not capitulation.

Key stylistic features for the writer to absorb:
- Direct address to the reader as moral witness, not stylistic flourish
- The house observed with architectural precision that doubles as emotional mapping
- The heroine's awareness of her own isolation — she names it, which is how she masters it
- The secret the house keeps is domestic, not supernatural — a woman locked in a room
- For this story: the inversion. Our heroine does NOT leave. She does not walk onto the moor. She stays, and her staying is narrated as contentment. The moral center of the novel — Jane's departure — is the absence at the center of this story.

### Selected Concepts (2):

**1. Salt Glaze Pottery / Lime Mortar**
The title "Salt and Mortar" points directly here. Salt-glaze pottery: common salt thrown into a kiln at extreme heat, the sodium vapor reacting with silica in the clay to form a permanent, glossy coating. The salt becomes part of the surface — it does not sit on top of it but bonds chemically, inseparable. Lime mortar: the breathable binding in old buildings, designed to let moisture escape; when you replace it with modern cement, you trap water inside the walls and the building rots from within. The distinction matters: salt glaze seals and transforms; lime mortar breathes and releases.

**Integration**: The husband, Alistair, is an amateur ceramicist — specifically, he fires salt-glaze stoneware in a wood-fired kiln he built on the property. The kiln is behind the house, near the estuary. The heroine describes the process accurately and with fascination: the way he shovels rock salt into the kiln at peak temperature, the chlorine gas that billows out (toxic, she notes, but he wears a mask), the sodium vapor that bonds with the clay. She finds the finished pieces beautiful — glossy, the orange-peel texture, the way light moves across the surface. She never makes the metaphor explicit. She never says: the salt becomes the surface, the binding is permanent, the original clay is transformed into something that cannot be un-glazed. She does not need to. The reader will. Meanwhile, the house itself is old — Victorian, brick and flint — and the heroine notices that someone has repointed the mortar with modern cement in places, trapping moisture. She sees the damp patches on the interior walls. She mentions this once, in passing, the way she mentions many things once.

**2. Wardian Case (Victorian Sealed Terrarium)**
In 1829, Dr. Nathaniel Ward placed a chrysalis in a sealed glass jar and discovered that a fern sprouted and thrived inside it — sealed away from the soot and grime of industrial London, creating its own micro-atmosphere through transpiration. The Wardian case became a Victorian drawing-room fixture: a sealed glass world where plants grew in perpetual self-sustaining balance. Plants inside Wardian cases survived for months — even years — without watering. They were also used to transport plants across oceans: sealed environments that kept specimens alive through six-to-nine-week sea voyages.

**Integration**: Alistair keeps Wardian cases. Several of them, on windowsills and shelves throughout the house. The heroine describes them with the precision she applies to everything: the sealed glass, the condensation on the inside surface, the ferns growing in their own recycled moisture. She finds them beautiful. She says: "He has created worlds for things to grow in." She does not hear what she has said. The Wardian cases are the domestic-scale version of the vivarium metaphor from the author meeting — the sealed environment, the curated world, the container that sustains life by enclosing it. The heroine walks past them every day. She never connects them to her own situation. The reader, standing outside the glass, will.

## Step 2: Story Design

### 1. Premise

A woman writes to her sister from an isolated house on the Essex estuary, describing her first winter with her new husband Alistair — his salt-glaze kiln by the mudflats, his sealed glass terrariums, his kind eyes. The letters are warm, observant, full of precise domestic detail. But the shape of what she tells — what she lingers on, what she passes over in a clause, the door she walks past daily and never opens — reveals something she cannot or will not see. The central question: is the house a prison designed to look like a home, or is it a home being read as a prison by a reader trained to decode gothic romance?

### 2. Protagonist

- **Name**: Nell Alcott, 28. Formerly a bookbinder's assistant in Cambridge. Unmarried until seven months ago. No family except her younger sister, Harriet, to whom the letters are addressed.
- **Situation**: Married to Alistair Graves, 41, a former architectural restorer and amateur ceramicist who inherited the house — Salter's Reach — from an uncle. They met when she repaired a water-damaged volume from his uncle's library. She moved to the house after the wedding. She has her own money (a small inheritance from her mother) and could leave at any time.
- **What she WANTS**: To convince Harriet (and herself) that she is happy. That the house is beautiful. That Alistair is kind. That this is the life she chose.
- **What she NEEDS**: To stop narrating her own contentment and sit with the silence beneath it — to acknowledge the door she has never opened, the inscription she has never asked about, the previous woman whose traces she has registered and filed away without examination.
- **Her flaw/blind spot**: Nell is a skilled reader of surfaces — she can assess the age of paper by touch, identify a binding style from across a room, date a typeface by its serifs. But she applies this perceptiveness selectively. She reads everything in the house except the house itself. She catalogs Alistair's kindness the way she catalogs the contents of a shelf — accurately, thoroughly, without asking why the catalog is necessary. She says his eyes are kind. She says it too often.

### 3. Structure (5 Beats)

**Beat 1 — Opening: The First Letter (November)**
Nell writes to Harriet describing Salter's Reach in winter. The house: Victorian, brick and flint, on the estuary edge, the mudflats visible from every east-facing window. The tide changes the view twice daily — silver at high water, iron-dark mud at low. She describes the rooms she uses (the kitchen, the library, the bedroom, the small sitting room she has made her own), the salt-glaze kiln behind the house, the Wardian cases on the windowsills. She describes Alistair: his hands, his patience, his work in the kiln. His eyes are kind. The letter is warm, detailed, literate — the letter of a woman with a bookbinder's eye for texture and surface. But: she does not describe the east corridor on the second floor. She walks past a door daily — the reader will realize this later — and she never mentions it. Not as a secret. As a gap.

**Beat 2 — Inciting Disruption: The Inscription (Late November)**
Nell discovers something while reorganizing a chest of drawers in the guest bedroom: scratched into the inside of the bottom drawer, a date (1983), a set of initials (E.V.G.), and a word that has been scored through so thoroughly the wood is gouged. She describes the initials, the date, the scoring. She does not try to read the word. She does not ask Alistair about it. She puts the drawer back and writes to Harriet about the samphire she gathered on the mudflats — the salt-marsh plant that grows like miniature cacti, naturally so salty you don't add seasoning. The letter continues with domestic detail. The inscription disappears into the texture of her account like a stitch absorbed into fabric.

**Beat 3 — Escalating Middle: The Accumulation (December–January)**
Three or four letters span the winter. Each contains precise, beautiful observations of the house and landscape — the kiln firing (she describes the salt thrown in at peak heat, the chlorine billowing, the sodium bonding with clay), the Wardian cases ("He has created worlds for things to grow in"), the estuary in frost, Alistair reading aloud to her in the evenings. But the accumulation reveals a pattern the individual letters do not announce:
- She mentions the east corridor once, as a route she takes to reach the bathroom. She does not describe it.
- She mentions that Alistair keeps the kiln outbuilding locked when he is not firing. She mentions this as a fact about security, not suspicion.
- She mentions a woman in the village — Mrs. Driscoll, who runs the post office — who looks at her with an expression Nell calls "concerned friendliness" and then immediately describes as "the way village people look at newcomers."
- She mentions that Alistair suggested she write to Harriet more often. "It will do you good," he said. She quotes this without comment.
- She mentions sleeping badly. Once. In a parenthetical.
- She describes the damp patches on the interior walls where modern cement has been used instead of lime mortar. She notes that the trapped moisture is damaging the brickwork. She does not make the metaphor.
- She describes finding a hairbrush in the back of a bathroom cabinet. Long dark hairs. She cleans the brush and uses it. She does not mention it again.

The reader, accumulating these details, begins to see the architecture of Nell's evasion. Each detail is true. No sentence contains a lie. But the shape of what she tells — the consistent avoidance of certain rooms, certain questions, certain threads — constitutes an unreliability of structure, not content.

**Beat 4 — Crisis: The Kiln Firing (Late January)**
Nell describes a night firing. Alistair has been working for three days, feeding the kiln, building the temperature. She brings him tea and stands in the doorway of the outbuilding. For the first time, she describes what is inside: the kiln, yes, but also shelves of finished work — and, on one shelf, a set of pieces she has never seen. Not his usual salt-glaze stoneware. Something else. She describes them in detail — their color, their texture, the way light moves across their surface — and then she says: "They are beautiful. I did not know he could make things like this." She stays. She watches him shovel the rock salt into the port. The sodium vapor billows. The chlorine smell is sharp even through his mask. He looks at her through the vapor and his eyes are kind.

Then: a single sentence, set apart from the rest. "I went back to the house and past the door on the east corridor and it was open."

She does not describe what she saw. The next paragraph begins a new letter, dated two weeks later, and it is about the February light on the mudflats, the way the estuary at dawn looks like hammered pewter. The tone is unchanged. The warmth is unchanged. She does not mention the door again.

**Beat 5 — Ending: The Last Letter (February)**
The final letter to Harriet. Nell describes the first signs of spring — samphire beginning to push through the mud, a warmer wind from the south, the light lasting longer. She describes Alistair planting something in the garden. She describes a new Wardian case he has built for her — glass, sealed, containing a fern and a small creeping moss. "He says it will live for years without opening. The glass keeps its own weather."

She tells Harriet she is happy. She tells her the house is beautiful. She tells her not to visit just yet — the roads are bad in winter, and besides, they are still settling in. Perhaps in the summer. She signs the letter.

Final paragraph: not a letter. A shift in register — or rather, a hairline crack in the register that has held the entire story. Two or three sentences. Perhaps something Nell writes and then crosses out. Perhaps a postscript she does not send. Perhaps a sentence addressed to no one: the word from the inscription, the one that was scratched through, and now she knows what it was. She does not tell us. Or: she describes the Wardian case one more time, the condensation on the inside of the glass, and the fern presses against the surface, and she watches it, and the sentence holds the image without comment, and the story ends.

The house stands. The heroine remains inside. The horror, if it is horror, belongs to the reader alone.

### 4. Key Scenes (5)

1. **The First Letter / The Estuary Description**: Nell's opening description of Salter's Reach and the mudflat landscape. Establishes du Maurier's atmospheric technique — the landscape as mood, the tide as metaphor for instability she does not remark on. Also introduces the Wardian cases and the kiln. The reader meets a narrator who is perceptive, literate, and warm. The unreliability is invisible at first — it operates through omission, not distortion.

2. **The Inscription in the Drawer**: Nell finds the initials, date, and scored-out word. The critical test of the unreliable narrator: she does not ask what the word was. She does not ask Alistair. She does not speculate. She pivots to samphire. The reader should feel the swerve — should feel the effort of the pivot — and begin to suspect that Nell's warmth and precision are a kind of performance.

3. **The Salt-Glaze Firing Description**: Nell explains the salt-glaze process with a bookbinder's precision — the rock salt, the kiln temperature, the sodium bonding with clay. The passage should be genuinely beautiful and informative, but the reader should hear the metaphor Nell does not make: the salt becomes the surface, the transformation is permanent, the original material is sealed beneath a glaze it did not choose. This is the Shelley element: creation as transformation, love as a chemical bond that cannot be undone.

4. **"The Door Was Open"**: The single sentence that breaks the story's surface. Nell has walked past this door for months. She has never mentioned it as a door she does not open — she has simply never mentioned it. Now, in one sentence, the reader understands: she knew it was there. She always knew. She chose not to tell us. And now it is open, and she will not tell us what she saw. This is the Wuthering Heights element — the frame narrative cracking, the past reaching through to seize the present — and the Jane Eyre element — the house's secret revealed. But unlike Jane, Nell does not leave.

5. **The Wardian Case / Final Letter**: The sealed glass terrarium as the story's final image. "He says it will live for years without opening. The glass keeps its own weather." Nell inside the house. The fern inside the glass. The condensation on the surface. The reader outside, looking in, unable to break the seal. The ending that does not resolve: is the house a vivarium or a home? Is Alistair's love care or confinement? Is Nell content or complicit? The story refuses to answer. The house stands.

### 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning**: The reader feels warmth and beauty. Nell's letters are charming, precise, atmospheric. The estuary landscape is vivid. The domestic details are pleasing. The reader settles into the account the way one settles into a well-made bed. There is a faint unease — gothic romance promises unease — but it is subdued, distant, barely a draft through a closed window.

**Middle**: The unease accumulates beneath the warmth. Each letter adds a detail that, taken alone, means nothing — the inscription, the locked outbuilding, Mrs. Driscoll's look, the bad sleep, the hairbrush. But taken together, they form a pattern the narrator is not acknowledging. The reader begins to feel the architecture of evasion. The warmth becomes suspect — not false, but performed. "His eyes are kind" begins to sound like a phrase she is repeating to herself. The reader is doing the gothic work that the narrator refuses to do: decoding, suspecting, translating.

**End**: The reader feels the trap close — not around Nell, but around themselves. The door was open. Nell will not say what she saw. The final letter is warm and the warmth is unbearable. The Wardian case image should produce a specific, uncomfortable recognition: the reader is looking at a woman who may be content inside a sealed world, and the reader cannot tell whether to grieve for her or envy her, and the inability to decide is the horror. The threads are not resolved. The house stands.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Daphne du Maurier (Author A)**: Pervasive throughout, especially in: the unnamed-heroine energy of Nell's voice (she is named, but her identity is subordinated to the house and the husband); the landscape as mood (the estuary, the mudflats, the tide as clock and metaphor); the house as character (Salter's Reach as both setting and antagonist/protector); the narrator's constructed version of reality (the comfortable version she chooses, and the reader can feel her choosing). The first letter and the estuary descriptions are du Maurier's territory. The technique of making landscape do the emotional work the narrator refuses to do.

**Mary Shelley (Author B)**: Concentrated in: the philosophical dimension of the husband's creation (the kiln, the Wardian cases, the house-as-vivarium — creation as an act of love that is simultaneously an act of confinement); the eloquence of the thing that has been made (the house argues for itself through its beauty, its order, its sealed terrariums); the question of whether the creator is responsible for the creation's suffering or happiness. The salt-glaze firing scene is Shelley's territory — transformation through extreme heat, the original material sealed beneath a surface it did not choose. The creature's complaint inverted: here, the creation does not protest. That is what makes it worse.

**Wuthering Heights (Work X)**: The epistolary frame as unreliable mediation. Nell's letters to Harriet are the equivalent of Nelly Dean's narration to Lockwood — shaped, condensed, "in her own words, only a little" adjusted. The generational echo: E.V.G., the previous woman, the scratched-out word — the story rhyming across time, a woman before Nell who lived in these rooms and left. The question the reader should ask: why does Nell not ask? The dream-logic of the single sentence — "the door was open" — as the frame cracking, the past reaching through.

**Jane Eyre (Work Y)**: The heroine's independence within constraint — but inverted. Nell has money. She could leave. She stays because she has chosen to stay, and the choosing is the independence, and the independence is the mechanism of the trap. The house's secret (the door on the east corridor) echoes Thornfield's third floor. But unlike Jane, who breaks through, hears the laughter, finds the room, confronts Rochester, and leaves — Nell does not leave. The moral center of Jane Eyre (the departure that proves free choice) is the absence at the center of this story. Nell's staying is the moral question the reader must answer for themselves.

### 7. Title

**Salt and Mortar**

The title works on three levels: (1) the literal materials of the house and the husband's craft — salt-glaze pottery (sodium bonding with clay at extreme heat, permanent, transformative) and lime mortar (breathable binding in old buildings, designed to let moisture escape — when replaced with modern cement, water is trapped and the structure rots); (2) the metaphorical resonance — salt as preservation and as wound, mortar as the binding that holds things together or seals them in; (3) the domestic scale — salt and mortar are kitchen and building materials, humble, tactile, the stuff of a house, not of a castle or a moor. The gothic operates at the level of the ordinary.

## Constraints Verification

- **Banned names**: No use of Marcus or Chen. Protagonist is Nell Alcott; husband is Alistair Graves; sister is Harriet; village woman is Mrs. Driscoll; previous occupant is E.V.G. (initials only).
- **Risk card (UNRELIABLE NARRATOR)**: Architecturally central. The entire story is structured as Nell's letters — her unreliability is not a twist but the medium. Every detail she provides is accurate; the unreliability is structural (what she omits, what she does not ask, what she lingers on and what she passes over). "The content is true. The architecture is the lie." The reader detects it through accumulation: the inscription she does not investigate, the door she never mentions until it opens, the repeated "his eyes are kind," the pivot from disturbance to domestic detail. She never acknowledges her own evasion.
- **Title**: Does not start with "The." Compound noun phrase.
- **Protagonist acts**: Nell writes the letters (an act of construction/performance), discovers the inscription (and chooses not to investigate), enters the kiln outbuilding (a choice to approach the husband's creation), sees the open door (and chooses not to tell the reader what she saw), writes the final letter (and chooses to tell Harriet not to visit). These are not dramatic actions — they are the quiet, devastating actions of a person maintaining a version of reality.
- **Open threads**: What was the scored-out word? What did Nell see behind the door? Who was E.V.G. and why did she leave? Is Alistair's love care or confinement? Is the house sinister or is the reader projecting? None of these are resolved. The house stands.
- **Specific wound**: Nell was a bookbinder's assistant — her entire professional life was spent repairing and preserving surfaces, making damaged things look whole again. She applies this skill to her own life. Her wound is not a trauma but a habit of restoration: she sees cracks and instinctively covers them. She does not ask what caused the crack.

## Notes for the Writer

1. **Epistolary structure**: The story is told through Nell's letters to Harriet. The letters should feel like letters — warm, conversational, with the slight self-consciousness of someone performing their life for an audience. Not diary entries. Letters have a recipient, and the recipient shapes the telling.

2. **Pacing the unreliability**: Do not signal the unreliability early or obviously. The first letter should read as genuinely charming. The second letter (the inscription) should plant the first seed of doubt. By the third or fourth letter, the reader should be actively suspicious. The "door was open" sentence should land like a detonation precisely because Nell treats it like nothing.

3. **The du Maurier voice**: Nell's prose should be specific, textural, grounded in physical detail. She notices surfaces the way a bookbinder notices surfaces. She describes the estuary the way du Maurier describes Manderley — with love and precision and an undertow of loss she will not name.

4. **The Shelley dimension**: The salt-glaze firing and the Wardian cases are where Shelley's influence concentrates. These passages should have a quiet philosophical weight — the beauty of sealed systems, the horror of permanent transformation, the question of whether creation is an act of love or control. Do not make the metaphors explicit. Nell would not. The reader will.

5. **The single sentence**: "I went back to the house and past the door on the east corridor and it was open." This sentence must be earned by everything that precedes it. It is the only moment where Nell's careful architecture fails — where the frame narrative cracks. Handle it with the same violence Emily Bronte brings to Lockwood's dream: sudden, physical, irreversible. Then Nell covers it over. The next paragraph is about the light on the mudflats.

6. **Ending**: Do NOT resolve. Do not reveal what was behind the door. Do not explain E.V.G. Do not let Nell wake up. The Wardian case is the final image: sealed glass, condensation, the fern pressing against the surface. The reader is outside the glass. The heroine is inside. The distinction between care and confinement is a question the story asks and does not answer.

7. **Word count discipline**: Target 4,200 words. The epistolary format helps — each letter can be 600-900 words, with five or six letters plus the final non-letter paragraph. Keep the letters varied in length. Some shorter, some longer. The middle letters (the accumulation) can be briefer — fragments of domestic detail that carry the unreliability in their very brevity.
