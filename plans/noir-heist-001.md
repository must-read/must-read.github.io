# Story Plan: noir-heist-001

**Genre**: crime-noir / heist-caper
**Target**: 3,700 words (3,400-4,000)
**Risk Card**: None

## Formula

- **AuthorA**: Elmore Leonard — snappy dialogue; lean prose; criminals with personality; dialogue tags are invisible; characters reveal themselves through how they talk, not what they say about themselves
- **AuthorB**: Chester Himes — Harlem noir; absurdist violence; rage and humor coexisting; violence as the bill coming due; people whose incompetence is specific, not general
- **WorkX**: *The Friends of Eddie Coyle* (Higgins) — all-dialogue structure; conversations reveal a doomed operation; the reader assembles the plot from what people say and fail to say; no exposition
- **WorkY**: *A Confederacy of Dunces* (Toole) — grandiose self-delusion; comedy of incompetence; a protagonist who interprets every failure as confirmation of his own genius; the world conspires against him because he deserves better than the world

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Elmore Leonard (AuthorA)
**Source**: *Road Dogs*, opening chapter dialogue
**Why representative**: Shows Leonard's invisible dialogue tags, characters who reveal personality through speech rhythm, and the way criminals discuss business like it's small talk.

> "You and the wife," Foley said, "devoting your lives to caring for people."
> "Is the reason we fall in love with each other. We alike in how we know how to make people happy."
> "But running a psychic con," Foley said, "doesn't mean she's actually psychic."
> "She saw me in the fucking courtroom, didn't she?"

Leonard's rule: if it sounds like writing, rewrite it. Dialogue carries everything. Tags are "said" or nothing. Each speaker has a distinct verbal fingerprint — dropped pronouns, grammar that belongs to the character rather than the author. Conversations read like you're overhearing them at the next table.

### Sample 2: Chester Himes (AuthorB)
**Source**: *A Rage in Harlem*, character introduction + *Cotton Comes to Harlem*, action style
**Why representative**: Shows Himes's economical physical description, the way violence arrives matter-of-factly, and his fusion of the absurd with the desperate.

Jackson is "a short, black, fat man with purple-red gums and pearly white teeth made for laughing." Himes describes Harlem's rooftops as "waves of gray rooftops [that] distort the perspective like the surface of a sea." His own dictum: "Realism and absurdity are so similar in the lives of American blacks one cannot tell the difference." Violence in Himes is baroquely physical — a man gets hit by a bread truck, another is shot over sixteen dollars — but narrated with the same flat, muscular prose as someone ordering breakfast. The comedy and the horror occupy the same sentence without the author choosing between them.

### Sample 3: *The Friends of Eddie Coyle* (WorkX)
**Source**: Opening page, Higgins
**Why representative**: Shows the all-dialogue technique — no exposition, the reader dropped into a negotiation, accumulating understanding from speech patterns and hesitations.

> Jackie Brown at twenty-six, with no expression on his face, said that he could get some guns. "I can get your pieces probably by tomorrow night. I can get you, probably, six pieces. Tomorrow night. In a week or so, maybe ten days, another dozen. I got a guy coming in with at least ten of them but I already talk to another guy about four of them and he's, you know, expecting them. He's got something to do. So, six tomorrow night. Another dozen in a week."
>
> The stocky man sat across from Jackie Brown and allowed his coffee to grow cold. "I don't know as I like that," he said. "I don't know as I like buying stuff from the same lot as somebody else."

The entire novel works this way. Eighty percent dialogue. You learn who will betray whom from how they ask about price. The doomed operation materializes through conversations in diners, parking lots, bars. Higgins never tells you the plot — you overhear it.

### Sample 4: *A Confederacy of Dunces* (WorkY)
**Source**: Ignatius J. Reilly's self-assessment, Chapters 1 and 3, Toole
**Why representative**: Shows Ignatius's grandiose self-perception, the gap between how he sees himself and how the world sees him, and the comedy that lives in that gap.

Ignatius is "a self-styled intellectual" who lives with his mother and "gets off on his constant indignation and horror at the modern world, while he thinks he's always being perfectly reasonable." When he considers Boethius's advice — "When Fortuna spins you downward, go out to a movie and get more out of life" — he realizes he goes to the movies almost every night regardless of Fortuna's direction, but this pause is one of the few moments he catches himself, and he doesn't pursue the thought. Every failure confirms his theory that the world is unworthy of him. His delusion is not passive — it's an engine. He acts on it, making things worse for everyone around him while narrating his own magnificence.

### Selected Concepts (2):

**1. The Gavle Goat (Sweden)**
A giant straw Christmas goat erected in Gavle, Sweden every year since 1966 — and burned down by arsonists nearly every year. Security cameras, fire-retardant chemicals, guards — nothing stops it. In 2005, attackers dressed as Santa and gingerbread men fired flaming arrows. In 2009, hackers disabled the cameras first. 43 of 60 goats destroyed.

**Integration**: The crew's target. They're not robbing a bank or a warehouse. They're hired to steal a large decorative piece — a commissioned sculpture, a seasonal installation — from a plaza. The job seems absurdly simple, which is why it goes absurdly wrong. The target has a quasi-legendary quality: people keep trying and failing to take it, like the Gavle goat keeps getting burned. The specific detail gives the heist a comic grandiosity: they're not Ocean's Eleven, they're five guys with a borrowed flatbed truck trying to steal a twenty-foot copper weathervane from a public square at 3 AM.

**2. Dead Letter Office / Mail Recovery Center**
The USPS facility in Atlanta where 90 million pieces of undeliverable mail end up annually. Clerks "blind read" — deducing addresses from context clues, handwriting, partial information.

**Integration**: The protagonist's day job. He works at a mail sorting facility — not the romantic dead-letter-office version, but the grinding modern one. He's good at it. He reads partial addresses, decodes bad handwriting, routes the unrouteable. This competence at his day job makes his incompetence at crime more pointed: he can solve puzzles on a conveyor belt but can't read the people sitting across from him. It also gives him his delusion — he thinks his pattern-recognition skills make him a natural planner. They don't.

## 1. Premise

Devaughn Watts, a mail sorter at a USPS processing facility in Detroit, has designed what he considers a flawless plan to steal a twenty-foot copper weathervane — a locally famous folk-art rooster commissioned for a new plaza — the night before its public unveiling. He's recruited four people, none of whom respect him, all of whom have their own reasons for being there, and each of whom believes they're the only one who understands what's actually happening. The plan requires forty-five minutes and a flatbed truck. It will take six hours and produce three injuries, a small fire, and zero copper roosters.

The question the story asks: what happens when a man who's brilliant at reading dead mail tries to read living people — and discovers that people don't come with return addresses?

## 2. Protagonist

**Devaughn Watts**, 41, mail sorter at the Detroit Processing and Distribution Center for eleven years. Divorced. Lives in a one-bedroom in Brightmoor with a cat named Boethius (he doesn't know who Boethius is — the cat came with the name from the shelter). Can reconstruct a smudged zip code from the weight of the paper and the postmark region. Has never been arrested. Has never committed a crime more serious than an illegal U-turn.

- **WANT**: The score. Not the money specifically — the proof that he's the kind of man who can design and execute a plan. He wants to be the architect, not the labor.
- **NEED**: To be seen as competent by people he respects. His ex-wife called him "reliable, like a bus schedule" during the divorce, and it broke something in him that he's never examined.
- **FLAW**: He believes that being good at puzzles means he's good at people. He reads patterns in everything — mail routes, traffic flow, security camera timing — and assumes human beings follow patterns too. They don't. Every person on his crew is doing something he can't predict because he's solving for the wrong variable.

## 3. Structure

### Opening Scene
A conversation between Devaughn and LYLE BRIGGS, 55, the flatbed truck owner, in a Coney Island diner on Michigan Avenue. Devaughn is explaining the plan. Lyle is eating a patty melt and not listening. The reader learns the basics of the job — the target, the timeline, the crew — entirely through dialogue, but also learns that Lyle thinks the plan is stupid and is only here because Devaughn is paying for the truck in advance and Lyle needs $800 for his daughter's tuition payment. The hook: Devaughn's explanations are precise, logical, and completely disconnected from how Lyle is responding.

### Inciting Disruption
The night of the job. The crew assembles at the staging point (a storage unit off Livernois). Two things are immediately wrong: (1) JANAE PITTS, 29, the lookout, has brought her cousin TERRELL, 22, who nobody invited, because Terrell "needs something to do" and Janae doesn't trust him alone in her apartment. (2) The fourth crew member, OSMOND "OZ" BAPTISTE, 38, a Haitian contractor who's supposed to handle the bolt-cutting and rigging, has arrived with a modified plan of his own that he hasn't shared with anyone. Devaughn's forty-five-minute timeline is already dead.

### Escalating Middle
Three conversations happening simultaneously in different parts of the staging area:
- Devaughn and Oz argue about rigging technique. Oz has actual construction experience and knows Devaughn's plan for detaching the weathervane won't work. Devaughn won't concede because conceding means he's not the architect. This argument, conducted in technical language neither fully commands, is where Leonard's snap dialogue meets Himes's absurdist escalation.
- Lyle sits in the truck cab talking to Terrell about nothing — the Lions, a woman Lyle used to know, the price of diesel. This conversation, pure Higgins/Coyle, reveals that Lyle has already decided to leave if the job takes longer than an hour.
- Janae, alone, calls someone on her phone. We hear only her side. She's checking in with a bail bondsman. Not for herself — for someone we never meet. This call, barely three lines, opens a thread that never closes.

The crew drives to the plaza. The weathervane is bigger than Devaughn described. Oz says so. Devaughn says it's perspective. Oz says it's twenty-three feet, not twenty. Devaughn says measurements are approximate. They argue about this while standing in a public square at 2 AM.

### Crisis Point
Oz gets the weathervane partially detached — using his method, not Devaughn's — when Terrell, who was supposed to be watching the street, walks over to help and trips over the rigging cable. The cable snaps taut, the weathervane lurches, and the rooster's copper beak punctures the flatbed's rear tire. They now have a twenty-three-foot copper rooster hanging at a 40-degree angle from a concrete pedestal, a truck with a flat tire, and a kid sitting on the ground holding his ankle. Devaughn's response: he starts recalculating. He's already designing Plan B. Plan B is worse than Plan A.

Lyle announces he's leaving. He'll walk. The truck stays, but he goes. Devaughn tries to talk him out of it — this conversation is the story's center, the moment where Devaughn's self-image as the master planner collides with a man who simply doesn't care about the plan. Lyle isn't angry. He isn't disappointed. He's a man who needed $800 and has decided this isn't worth $800.

### Ending
Oz, who has been quietly working the rigging while everyone argues, gets the weathervane down. It's lying on the ground next to a truck that can't move. Devaughn, Oz, Janae, and Terrell stand around it. Nobody says anything for a moment. Then they start talking — not about what to do next, but about other things. Terrell asks Oz about Haiti. Janae mentions she's hungry. Devaughn starts describing, in detail, how the original plan would have worked if people had followed it.

They leave the rooster on the ground. They leave the truck. They walk to a diner three blocks away and sit in a booth. Devaughn is still talking about the plan. He'll be talking about the plan for years. The rooster is found the next morning by a jogger, lying on its side in the plaza like a drunk bird. No one is ever arrested because no one can figure out what the crime was supposed to be.

The final image: Devaughn at work the next Monday, sorting mail, reconstructing a smudged address with the same focused precision he brought to the heist. He's good at this. He's always been good at this. The story doesn't say whether he knows that's enough.

## 4. Key Scenes

- **Diner opening (Devaughn/Lyle)**: Establishes the plan through dialogue while simultaneously showing it won't work — Lyle's responses reveal he's already checked out. Pure Higgins structure. ~800 words.

- **Staging area three-way split**: Three conversations in parallel, each revealing a different kind of incompetence. Devaughn's is intellectual (he can't adapt). Oz's is prideful (he won't share his better plan). Lyle's is temporal (he's already counting down to leaving). Terrell's presence — the uninvited variable — is the thing no pattern-recognition can account for. ~900 words.

- **The plaza argument about measurements**: Comedy of specificity. Two men arguing about whether the rooster is twenty or twenty-three feet while standing in a public square committing a felony. This is where Leonard's lean dialogue and Himes's absurdist violence share the same breath. ~500 words.

- **Lyle walks away**: The emotional center. Devaughn's pitch to keep Lyle is the most articulate he's been all night — and it doesn't work. Not because the pitch is bad but because Lyle has already made a decision based on information Devaughn doesn't have (the daughter, the $800, the sense that some plans aren't worth completing). ~600 words.

- **Post-heist diner booth**: Five people who failed at something sitting in a booth talking about everything except the failure. This is where the Himes and Toole influences crystallize — the comedy of people who will never admit the real thing, plus Devaughn's Ignatius-like narration of how the plan *should* have gone. ~500 words.

## 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Opening**: Amusement and slight dread. The reader can see this won't work before Devaughn finishes explaining it. The comedy is in the gap between Devaughn's precision and Lyle's indifference.

**Middle**: Escalating anxiety wrapped in laughter. Each new complication is funnier than the last, but the stakes are real — these are people with rent due, people with family obligations, people who need $800. The absurdity doesn't erase the need.

**Crisis**: A wince. The flat tire, the fallen rooster, Terrell on the ground — it's slapstick, but the kind that leaves bruises. The reader feels embarrassed for Devaughn in the way you feel embarrassed for someone giving a toast at a wedding who doesn't realize the room has stopped listening.

**Ending**: Something between tenderness and pity that refuses to become either. Devaughn at his mail station, good at something nobody will ever admire him for. The reader is left sitting with the question of whether competence at the small thing is a consolation or a sentence.

## 6. Formula Integration

**Elmore Leonard (AuthorA)**: The diner opening and every Devaughn/Lyle exchange. Lean dialogue, "said" or nothing, characters who reveal themselves through what they talk around. Lyle especially is a Leonard character — a man with a code he doesn't explain, who makes decisions fast and doesn't revisit them. The prose should feel like you could cut any sentence in half and it would still stand.

**Chester Himes (AuthorB)**: The staging area chaos and the plaza sequence. The absurdist escalation — an uninvited cousin, a modified plan nobody shared, an argument about three feet of copper in a public square at 2 AM. Violence that's both slapstick and real. Himes's Harlem energy: people with debt making decisions that people with options would never understand. The moment Terrell trips over the cable is pure Himes — a bread-truck-level accident that was always coming.

**The Friends of Eddie Coyle (WorkX)**: The entire story is structured as conversations. Minimal narration. What exposition exists is squeezed into the margins of dialogue — a character mentions a detail, another character doesn't respond to it, and the reader understands the silence. The three-way split at the staging area is directly modeled on Higgins's technique of intercut conversations that reveal the same operation from different angles of ignorance.

**A Confederacy of Dunces (WorkY)**: Devaughn himself. His grandiose self-image as the architect. His inability to see that his plan failed not because of bad luck but because of who he is. His post-failure narration of how it *should* have gone — the Ignatius move of interpreting defeat as evidence that the world isn't ready for his genius. The cat named Boethius (Ignatius's favorite philosopher) is a small structural rhyme. But Devaughn is warmer than Ignatius and more competent in his daily life — the Himes influence makes his delusion specific to the score, not general to existence.

## 7. Title

**Everybody's Genius Plan**

Avoids "The." Captures the multiplicity of self-delusion — every crew member thinks they're the smart one. Has a colloquial snap that fits the Leonard tone. Slightly ironic without being clever about it.

A mail sorter with a gift for reading smudged addresses designs a flawless plan to steal a twenty-foot copper rooster from a Detroit plaza. His crew of four has five different plans. None of them work.
