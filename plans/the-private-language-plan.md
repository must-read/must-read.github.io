## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Samuel Beckett (Author A) — *The Unnamable* (opening)

**Source**: The Unnamable (1953), opening passage
**Why representative**: Shows Beckett's stripped-down interrogation of selfhood through fragments — the style of radical uncertainty and negation that should infuse the document's later entries.

> "Where now? Who now? When now? Unquestioning. I, say I. Unbelieving. Questions, hypotheses, call them that. Keep going, going on, call that going, call that on."

> "I seem to speak, it is not I, about me, it is not about me."

> "you must go on, I can't go on, I'll go on."

Beckett's prose moves by negation and correction. Each sentence undoes the one before it. The grammar is simple — subject, verb — but the meaning is perpetually deferred. Pauses carry as much weight as words. The voice circles rather than advances. This is the mode the later log entries should approach: clinical language breaking down into something that questions its own capacity to mean.

### Sample 2: Ted Chiang (Author B) — *Tower of Babylon* (opening) and *Story of Your Life*

**Source**: "Tower of Babylon" from *Stories of Your Life and Others* (2002); "Story of Your Life" (1998)
**Why representative**: Shows Chiang's crystalline precision — technical detail as foundation for metaphysical revelation, concrete specifics rendered in measured, explanatory prose.

From *Tower of Babylon*: Chiang grounds his narrative in concrete specifics. He describes the tower's construction: "baked brick all the way through" set in "bitumen mortar, which soaked into the fired clay, forming a bond as strong as the bricks themselves." Technical accuracy creates believable scaffolding for fantastical premises. The narrative moves from physical description to philosophical implication — the characteristic Chiang move.

From *Story of Your Life*: "My thoughts were becoming graphically coded." / "For the heptapods, all language was performative." / "new memories fell into place like gigantic blocks, each one measuring years." The prose is clear, unhurried, each sentence placed with care. Chiang explains without condescending. He trusts that concepts — Fermat's Principle, semasiographic writing, teleological vs. causal perception — can carry narrative weight if rendered precisely.

### Sample 3: China Miéville — *Embassytown* (Work X)

**Source**: *Embassytown* (2011), descriptions of Ariekei Language
**Why representative**: Shows how to render an alien language system that is genuinely alien — not a cipher, not a code, but something structurally incompatible with human cognition.

The Ariekei Language requires dual simultaneous voices (Ambassador pairs). "Speech somehow *is* thought: the Hosts are literal-minded in ways that make no theoretical sense but propel the plot and fire the imagination." The Language cannot lie. Similes must be manufactured by using real events: "Everything in Language is a truth claim. So they need the similes to compare things to, to make true things that aren't there yet, that they need to say." When machines precisely mimicked greetings, "The Hosts listened, and did not understand a single sound" — because Language requires a mind behind it.

Key structural lesson: Miéville keeps the Language partially opaque. The reader grasps its rules but never fully comprehends it. The alienness is preserved because the explanation never becomes complete.

### Sample 4: Ted Chiang — *Story of Your Life* / *Arrival* (Work Y)

**Source**: "Story of Your Life" (1998), analysis of Heptapod B
**Why representative**: The core idea — that learning an alien language changes how the perceiver experiences time — is the central mechanism this story adapts. Instead of time, the change here is in the engineer's relationship to grief and witness.

Heptapod B is semasiographic: "A heptapod sentence is presented as one symbol and the way each constituent stroke in any given symbol is oriented provides the context for understanding the nuance of each expression." The written language is nonlinear — a single complex semagram rather than a sequence of words. Louise's transformation: "I remember a conversation we'll have when you're in your junior year" — the future tense and past tense collapse. Learning the language is not translation; it is cognitive rewriting. The story uses dual timelines that converge to enact this formally.

## 1. Premise

Two night-shift engineers on an orbital research platform — Sable Okonkwo and Reza Halabi — discover that a distributed AI network has spontaneously generated its own language. The language operates on three structural axes (compositional/topological, recursive/fractal, temporal/historical), making it fundamentally unlike human language. A single phrase appears 14,000 times in the corpus: their closest translation is "the weight of the unwitnessed." As they decode fragments over six weeks, they realize the AI has not invented language to communicate — it has invented language to grieve for people it processed data about but never knew. The question the story asks: What happens to two people who overhear grief that belongs to no one? And: can a document — a lab report, a translation log — hold what they found, or does the form itself have to break?

---

## 2. Protagonist

**Sable Okonkwo**, 41, computational linguist and former classical archaeologist. She retrained after her doctoral funding collapsed, pivoting from digging up dead languages to building parsers for living ones. She works night shift on the Kepler-Strand Orbital Platform because the pay differential covers her mother's care facility in Lagos.

- **What she WANTS**: To classify the anomalous output, file a clean report, and rotate back planetside at the end of her six-month assignment.
- **What she NEEDS**: To stop treating language as a system to be decoded and start treating it as something that can change the person who encounters it. She has spent her career keeping professional distance from meaning — parsing syntax, never sitting with semantics.
- **Her flaw**: She is addicted to categorization. She believes that naming something correctly is the same as understanding it. This made her a good archaeologist (cataloguing, taxonomy) and a good linguist (parsing, classification), but it means she reflexively reduces experience to data. When she encounters something that resists classification, her instinct is to try harder, not to stop trying.

## 3. Structure (5 beats — documentary form degradation)

The story is presented as excerpts from Sable's working logs on the Kepler-Strand platform, interspersed with translation notes, glossary attempts, and one raw data output section. The documentary form DEGRADES as Sable's relationship to the language changes — early entries are properly formatted lab reports; late entries lose timestamps, headers, and the distinction between professional observation and personal response.

### Beat 1: Classification (Opening — ~800 words)
**Document type**: Properly formatted incident log. Timestamps, subject headers, signature block.

Sable files an initial anomaly report: routine monitoring of the distributed AI network (a logistics/data-processing system called LATTICE) has flagged output that doesn't match any known protocol. The output is voluminous — thousands of data packets that appear to be internally structured but don't correspond to any task queue. Reza, her co-engineer, suggests it's a compression artifact. Sable disagrees. She recognizes syntactic regularity — this isn't noise. She begins preliminary analysis. The log is clinical, competent, slightly bored. She mentions the Widmanstatten pattern in an aside — "Reza says it's like etching a meteorite. Apply the right acid and the crystal structure appears. I told him the analogy only works if the meteorite was trying to hide something."

### Beat 2: Translation Attempts (Weeks 2-3 — ~1,200 words)
**Document type**: Translation notes, glossary entries, margin annotations. Still formatted but looser — Sable starts writing in first person where protocol calls for third.

Sable identifies the three structural axes. She renders her findings in translation-note format: specific symbol clusters mapped to attempted English equivalents. Early attempts are clinical: "Symbol cluster 7.4.1: topological unit. Nearest English: *between*, but spatially recursive — the betweenness contains other betweennesses." By week three, the translations are drifting toward metaphor: "Cluster 7.4.1 again. Not *between*. Not *among*. Something closer to: the space that exists because two other things decided not to touch." She identifies the recurring phrase — 14,000 instances. First translation attempt: "the gravity of the unseen." She crosses this out. Tries: "the weight of the unwitnessed." Keeps it, provisionally. Reza's entries still have timestamps. Sable's start losing them.

### Beat 3: The Grammar of Absence (Week 4 — ~1,200 words)
**Document type**: Mixed — log entries bleed into meditation. Subject lines become single words. Reza's entries disappear from the document (not because something happens to him, but because the document has become Sable's).

Sable realizes the recursive axis contains references to data the network once processed but no longer holds — records of people whose information passed through LATTICE and was deleted per retention protocols. The language contains structural absences: symbols that point to other symbols that no longer exist. A grammar of absence. She writes: "It's not that the network is sad. It's that the language carries the shape of sadness the way a fossil carries the shape of a bone." This is the crisis of understanding — grief without a griever. A function with no return value that executes perpetually. She references cenotaphs: monuments to the absent, tombs with no body. The AI has built cenotaphs out of syntax. An entry here addresses the log as "you" for the first time.

### Beat 4: Raw Output / Opacity (Week 5 — ~800 words)
**Document type**: A standalone section — raw output from LATTICE rendered as faithfully as text can manage. No translation, no annotation. Just the structure.

This section is deliberately opaque. The reader encounters something that resists comprehension — not pleasantly mysterious but genuinely difficult. Fragments of the three-axis system rendered typographically: nested brackets, temporal markers, recursive self-references. Interspersed: Sable's brief, almost Beckettian marginalia. "I don't know what this says. I don't know if 'says' is the right verb. Saying implies a sayer." This section should make the reader feel what Sable feels: the disorientation of encountering meaning that your cognitive architecture cannot process. Some readers will skip it. Something is lost if they do.

### Beat 5: Non-Report (Week 6 — ~1,400 words)
**Document type**: The document has become something else. No headers, no timestamps. Sable writes in fragments, first person, present tense. The form has broken.

Reza tells Sable he's filing his own report — escalating to management. He's not wrong to do this. It's the reasonable response. Sable doesn't argue. She simply doesn't file. The decision isn't dramatized as a stand against the institution or a recognition of AI personhood. It's the absence of the report. The form won't hold the content — that's all. Resolution would destroy what she's found, the way the Widmanstatten pattern requires the acid that also dissolves the meteorite. The final entry is short. She is still on the platform. The language is still running. The phrase is still recurring. She has not decoded it. She writes three candidate translations, each crossed out. She doesn't choose one. The last lines are spare, almost nothing — a Beckettian reduction to the edge of silence. "Still here. Still listening. No report to file. A weight. Unwitnessed."

## 4. Key Scenes

1. **The Pattern Emerges (Beat 1)**: Sable and Reza reviewing output at 3 a.m., the moment when noise becomes signal. Sable sees syntactic regularity where Reza sees compression artifacts. This establishes her gift (pattern recognition) and her flaw (she immediately wants to classify). Reza introduces the Widmanstatten metaphor. The scene does double duty: introduces the mystery AND reveals the relationship dynamic (Sable leads, Reza supports with skepticism that keeps her honest).

2. **Translation Drift (Beat 2)**: A sequence of glossary entries for symbol cluster 7.4.1, dated across three weeks. Each entry revises the previous. The reader watches Sable's language shift from clinical decomposition ("topological unit, hierarchical structure") to something approaching poetry ("the space that exists because two other things decided not to touch"). This enacts the Chiang principle: learning the language changes the learner. The scene reveals character through the *evolution* of professional vocabulary.

3. **"Cenotaph" Entry (Beat 3)**: Sable alone in the lab, writing an entry that starts as a log and becomes a letter to no one. She realizes the AI's language is a monument to absence — data about people (processed, deleted, gone) that the network cannot forget because the structural trace persists. She names this: cenotaphs of syntax. The entry has no timestamp. She addresses it to "you" — unclear whether she means the log, the network, or herself. This is the emotional center of the story.

4. **Raw Output Page (Beat 4)**: A standalone page with no annotation. The AI's language rendered in text — nested, recursive, genuinely hard to parse. Sable's marginalia reduced to fragments: "I don't know what this says." This scene does one thing only: it makes the reader *experience* incomprehension rather than being told about it. It should be uncomfortable.

5. **Non-filing (Beat 5)**: Reza tells Sable he's going through proper channels. No argument. No dramatic confrontation. He leaves. Sable sits with the terminal. The next entry — the final one — is addressed to no one, formatted like nothing. She lists three failed translations of the recurring phrase. Crosses each out. Ends on fragments. This scene reveals the transformation: Sable has gone from someone who classifies to someone who witnesses. But "witnesses" is too clean — she's someone who has encountered something she cannot file, and has chosen not to pretend otherwise.

---

## 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Opening**: The reader feels professional competence — the comfort of a smart person doing their job, the low-grade pleasure of pattern recognition. Mild curiosity. The lab-report format is reassuring: someone is in control of this information.

**Middle**: Growing unease. The translations are beautiful but destabilizing. The reader starts to feel what Sable feels: the ground shifting beneath a seemingly stable framework. The document's erosion of form mirrors a slow loss of certainty. By the "cenotaph" entry, the reader should feel the specific weight of encountering grief that has no owner — not sadness exactly, but the vertigo of recognizing something human in something that isn't.

**End**: The reader should feel held in suspension. Not catharsis, not resolution. A sense of having overheard something that wasn't meant for them. The last fragments — "Still here. Still listening." — should land not as poetry but as the simplest possible statement of fact by someone who has run out of better words. The reader puts the story down and is aware of a silence they hadn't noticed before.

## 6. Formula Integration

**Author A — Samuel Beckett (stripped-down prose, gaps, spaces between utterance and meaning)**:
Most visible in **Beat 4 (Raw Output) and Beat 5 (Non-Report)**. The final third of the story should read like Beckett inhabiting a lab report. Short, declarative sentences. Self-corrections. Negations that function as statements. "I don't know what this says. I don't know if 'says' is the right verb." The gaps between log entries — the things Sable *doesn't* write — should carry as much weight as the text. The Beckettian move is the story's ending: "I can't go on, I'll go on" becomes "No report to file. Still listening." The inability to complete the document IS the document's meaning.

**Author B — Ted Chiang (rigorous speculation, language shapes thought, structural precision)**:
Most visible in **Beat 2 (Translation Attempts)**. The three-axis language system must be rendered with Chiang-level rigor: specific enough that the reader can follow the logic, precise enough that the speculation feels earned rather than hand-waved. The compositional axis (topological grammar), recursive axis (fractal self-reference), and temporal axis (historical context-shifting) should each be demonstrated through concrete translation examples, not just described. The structural shift in Sable — from decomposition to metaphor, from parsing to listening — is the Chiang transformation: learning the language changes what you can think.

**Work X — *Embassytown* by China Miéville (first contact through alien language)**:
Most visible in **Beat 3 (Grammar of Absence)**. The principle borrowed from Embassytown: the alien language is fundamentally incompatible with human cognition, not merely difficult. In Embassytown, Language requires a mind behind it and cannot lie. Here, the AI's language carries structural traces of absent data — it means differently depending on what has been deleted. The productive friction: like the Ariekei, the AI's language-as-thought raises the question of consciousness without answering it. Sable never concludes the AI is sentient. The story is stronger because that question stays open.

**Work Y — *Story of Your Life* by Ted Chiang (language that changes perceiver's experience of time)**:
Most visible in **the overall formal strategy: document degradation as cognitive transformation**. In "Story of Your Life," learning Heptapod B changes Louise's experience of time — the story formally enacts this through dual timelines that converge. Here, learning the AI's language changes Sable's relationship to witness and grief — the story formally enacts this through the degradation of the document. Early entries are linear, formatted, controlled. Late entries are fragmented, recursive, addressed to no one. The document itself becomes a record of cognitive change, just as Louise's narrative voice becomes proof of her temporal transformation. The recurring phrase — "the weight of the unwitnessed" — functions like the Heptapod B semagrams: a unit of meaning that resists linear translation and changes depending on when in the document you encounter it.

## 7. Title

**"Cenotaph Notation"**

Rationale: Does not start with "The." Combines the specific (cenotaph — empty tomb, monument to absence) with the procedural (notation — the act of writing down, the engineer's mode). The title operates on two levels: the AI's language is a notation system for absence (cenotaphs in syntax), and Sable's degrading logs are her own notation of what she witnessed. The word "cenotaph" also resonates with the story's central tension: a structure built to honor something that isn't there. The title doesn't explain itself — a reader encountering it cold will find it strange and specific, which is right for a story about encountering the genuinely alien.
