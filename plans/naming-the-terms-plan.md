# Story Plan: naming-the-terms

## Assignment

- **Genre**: Fantasy
- **Subgenre**: Romantasy
- **Combination ID**: fan-romantasy-001
- **Slug**: naming-the-terms

### Style Sources

**Author A — Madeline Miller**: Classical, elevated prose with modern emotional intelligence. Mythological reimagining centering female interiority. Transformation as both power and prison. Prose of "dreamlike simplicity" — lyrical but never ornamental. Every sentence serves the interiority of the protagonist.

**Author B — Courtney Milan**: Sharp, feminist romance with intellectual precision. Protagonists whose intelligence is their primary weapon. Emotional warmth built through vulnerability, not grand gestures. Dialogue-driven scenes where the subtext is a negotiation — every exchange has a power dynamic running beneath it.

### Structural/Thematic Sources

**Work X — Circe**: A woman discovering her power through exile and solitude. Tension between divine inheritance and self-made identity. Love as the force that both liberates and threatens autonomy. The mythic register — consequences that feel ordained rather than accidental.

**Work Y — The Duchess War**: A heroine hiding her true abilities behind social performance. Romance built on intellectual equals recognizing each other. The personal as political — private love against public systems. The moment when intelligence is revealed as dangerous.

### Target Word Count

6,500 words (acceptable range: 6,200–6,800)

### Risk Card: DUAL TIMELINE

Two timelines run in parallel — present and past. The timelines must speak to each other through echo, contrast, or causation. The connection between them is the story's real subject.

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Madeline Miller — *Circe*, Opening

**Source**: Author A — Madeline Miller
**Origin**: *Circe*, Chapter 1 (2018)
**Why representative**: Establishes Miller's signature first-person voice — elevated but intimate, classical syntax with modern emotional precision. The narrator is both mythic figure and fully interior consciousness.

> "When I was born, the name for what I was did not exist. They called me nymph, assuming I would be like my mother and aunts and thousand cousins. Least of the lesser goddesses, our powers were so modest they could scarcely ensure our eternities."

Miller's technique: the narrator defines herself through negation. She is what she is not — not nymph, not like her mother, not powerful enough to have a name. This is the voice the writer must capture in the past timeline: a woman discovering herself through the failure of every category others place on her.

### Sample 2: Madeline Miller — *Circe*, Selected Passages

**Source**: Author A — Madeline Miller
**Origin**: *Circe*, various chapters (highlighted passages via kottke.org)
**Why representative**: Shows Miller's range — from aphorism to metaphor to emotional precision.

> "However gold he shines, do not forget his fire."

> "You cannot know how frightened gods are of pain."

> "In a solitary life, there are rare moments when another soul dips near yours, as stars once a year brush the earth."

> "But he was a harp with only one string, and the note it played was himself."

> "It is a common saying that women are delicate creatures, flowers, eggs, anything that may be crushed in a moment's carelessness."

Key techniques for the writer: Miller uses extended metaphors that land as both image and argument. Her similes compare intimate experience to cosmic scale ("as stars once a year brush the earth"). She describes people as instruments, objects, forces — never as abstractions. The writer should deploy this technique particularly in the past timeline, where the protagonist's experience of her own power should be rendered through concrete metaphor rather than emotional summary.

### Sample 3: Madeline Miller — *The Song of Achilles*, Combat Passage

**Source**: Author A — Madeline Miller
**Origin**: *The Song of Achilles*, war sequence
**Why representative**: Shows Miller's ability to render violence and beauty simultaneously — the observer consumed by what they witness, unable to separate destruction from desire.

> "He was a marvel, shaft after shaft flying from him, spears that he wrenched easily from broken bodies on the ground to toss at new targets. Again and again I saw his wrist twist, exposing its pale underside, those flute-like bones thrusting elegantly forward. My spear sagged forgotten to the ground as I watched. I could not even see the ugliness of death anymore, the brains, the shattered bones that later I would wash from my skin and hair. All I saw was his beauty, his singing limbs, the quick flickering of his feet."

This passage captures the loss of self in the presence of another's power — the narrator literally cannot see the world anymore, only the person. The writer should echo this in the past-timeline moment when the protagonist first sees her own hands change: a total perceptual shift where the familiar body becomes alien and compelling simultaneously.

### Sample 4: Courtney Milan — *The Duchess War*, Opening Encounter

**Source**: Author B — Courtney Milan
**Origin**: *The Duchess War*, Chapter 1 (2012)
**Why representative**: Establishes Milan's signature technique — two intelligent people circling each other, each reading the other, dialogue as negotiation. The heroine's defiance beneath her wallflower exterior.

From the opening: Minerva Lane, quiet and bespectacled, is discovered behind a sofa during a musicale by the Duke of Clermont. Her mask of timidity is precisely that — a mask, constructed to hide a scandalous past. When the Duke says "If a woman I cared about was hiding behind a sofa, I would hope that someone would take the time to make sure she was well," Minerva responds: **"Oh. You're attempting to be kind."** Her tone suggests kindness is foreign to her experience.

Milan's technique: character revealed through the gap between social performance and actual response. Minerva's line is simultaneously a shield and a confession — it tells the Duke (and the reader) that she doesn't expect kindness, which reveals more about her history than any backstory paragraph could. The writer should deploy this in the present timeline: sharp dialogue where each line reveals and conceals simultaneously.

### Sample 5: Courtney Milan — *The Heiress Effect*, Dialogue

**Source**: Author B — Courtney Milan
**Origin**: *The Heiress Effect* (Brothers Sinister, Book 2)
**Why representative**: Milan's emotional directness when the negotiation finally reaches its honest terms.

> "I don't need that quiet wife. I need you. Someone bold. Someone who won't let me stand back from myself, and who will tell me in no uncertain terms when I've erred."

Milan's climactic declarations work because they are specific — not "I love you" but "I need someone who will tell me when I've erred." The love is expressed as a negotiated need, not a surrender to feeling. The writer should use this register for the present-timeline love interest: his confession is not romantic in the conventional sense but precise, specific, grounded in what he actually requires from another person.

### Sample 6: Courtney Milan — Style Analysis

**Source**: Author B — Courtney Milan
**Origin**: Various novels and critical analysis
**Why representative**: Captures the analytical framework of Milan's prose.

Milan's heroines are defined by their intelligence before their attractiveness. They calculate risk, weigh options, and make choices with full awareness of consequences. Her dialogue operates on two levels: what is said, and the power dynamic underneath. Her romances work because the attraction is intellectual first — two people recognize in each other a quality of mind that excites them more than physical beauty, though beauty is present too. The "meeting of equals" structure means both characters must transform, not just the heroine. The love interest is never a fixed point around which the heroine orbits; he is also in motion, also changing, also hiding something that the relationship will demand he reveal.

### Selected Concepts (2)

**1. Iron gall ink** — The standard writing ink of Europe for 1,400 years: permanent, water-resistant, made from oak galls and iron sulfate — but acidic. Over centuries, it eats through the very surface it was used to inscribe. The ink that preserves also destroys.

**Integration**: In the story's magical system, binding contracts between practitioners are written in a substance analogous to iron gall ink — a magical reagent that fixes terms permanently but, over generations, corrodes the very relationships it was meant to preserve. The Compact between the Ashward family (practitioners of ward-magic) and the High Seat (the governing body) was inscribed two centuries ago. It granted the Ashwards authority over binding magic in exchange for their submission to the Seat's oversight. But the terms have eaten through the original intent. What was meant to protect practitioners from unregulated power has become a mechanism for controlling who may practice, what forms magic may take, and who qualifies as legitimate. Ilva's grandmother's wild magic — her ability to transform the substance of things through touch — was classified as "unbound practice" under the Compact's corroded terms. Legal, technically. Punishable, practically. The acid in the ink is literal and metaphorical: the binding contracts physically degrade, and so the Seat rewrites them every generation, each iteration more restrictive than the last. When Ilva discovers she has inherited her grandmother's gift, she understands that the ink will eat through her too.

**2. Fasciation** — A plant mutation where the growing tip splits and the stem flattens, widens, twists into something alien and beautiful. Not disease, not health — a disruption to normal growth that produces something the plant was never meant to be.

**Integration**: Ilva's magic manifests as fasciation — literal and metaphorical. When her power surges, the plants near her fasciate: stems flatten and twist, flowers multiply and merge, growth goes sideways in ways that are gorgeous and disturbing. Her hands, when she works magic, undergo their own fasciation — the skin tightens, the fingers spread and flatten slightly, the texture changes to something between skin and bark. It is not glamorous. It is not painful. It is a disruption to the normal pattern of her body that produces something she was never meant to be. In the past timeline, this physical manifestation is what betrays her: she cannot hide her nature when her hands visibly change. The fasciation motif connects to Milan's theme of performance — Ilva has been performing normality, and fasciation is the body's refusal to cooperate with the performance. In the present timeline, Daire (the love interest) recognizes the fasciated growth near Ilva's workbench before he recognizes the woman responsible for it. He studies the twisted stems with the careful attention of someone who knows what disrupted growth looks like from the inside.

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

In a city where magical practice is governed by binding contracts that corrode over generations, a woman who transforms the substance of things through touch has spent twelve years hiding her gift behind a mask of clerical competence. When she is assigned to audit the binding records alongside a ward-architect whose own power depends on the very system that outlawed hers, two timelines converge: the past, in which she was young and her magic revealed itself and cost her everything; and the present, in which she must decide whether to let another person see what she is, knowing the contract between them will be written in ink that eventually eats through the page.

### 2. Protagonist

**Ilva Denn**, 34, senior clerk in the Registrar's Office of the High Seat — the bureaucratic body that governs magical practice through the Compact, a system of binding contracts between practitioner families and the state. She processes applications, reviews credentials, files amendments. She is efficient, precise, invisible.

She is also a touch-transformer: she can alter the material substance of things through sustained contact. Wood becomes glass. Clay becomes silver. Skin becomes — she doesn't let herself find out. The ability is hereditary, passed through the women in her family, and it has been classified under the Compact as "unbound practice" — legal to possess, illegal to use without registration, and registration requires a level of institutional exposure that has historically ended in confiscation, restriction, or worse.

- **What she WANTS**: To survive undetected. Specifically: to reach her pension without anyone learning what her hands can do. She has twenty-three more years of service. She has a plan.
- **What she NEEDS**: To stop treating her own power as a liability to be managed and recognize it as a capacity to be negotiated — with the world, and with the specific person standing in front of her. She needs to discover that hiding is not the same as safety, and that the corrosive contracts she files every day have been eating through her as surely as they eat through the parchment.
- **Her flaw**: She conflates control with survival. She believes that if she can manage every variable — the timing of her gloves, the angle of her hands, the neutrality of her expression — she can prevent the catastrophe that happened when she was twenty-two. But control is not the same as agency. She is so committed to preventing the past from repeating that she has stopped allowing the present to happen.
- **Specific wound**: At twenty-two, during her apprenticeship at the Registrar's Office, Ilva's magic manifested involuntarily when she was cataloguing a damaged binding contract. Her hands changed — the fasciation — and the contract beneath them transformed from brittle parchment into something alive, a page that pulsed and rewrote itself. Her supervisor, a woman named Hastra, saw it. Hastra was kind about it. Hastra reported it anyway, because the Compact required her to. Ilva was not arrested or expelled — instead, she was offered a choice: submit to formal assessment and registration (which would flag her in every system, limit her employment, and require annual review of her magical output) or sign a voluntary cessation agreement, pledging never to use her ability and consenting to monitoring. She signed the cessation. Hastra gave her a cup of tea and said, "It's for the best." Ilva has not spoken to her since. The cessation agreement sits in a file she can locate in under forty seconds. The ink on it has already begun to eat through the paper.

### 3. Structure

**DUAL TIMELINE ARCHITECTURE:**

The story alternates between PAST (Ilva at 22, the year of her exposure) and PRESENT (Ilva at 34, the audit). Past scenes are written in Miller's register: elevated, mythic, interior, the language of transformation and its costs. Present scenes are written in Milan's register: sharp, dialogue-driven, the language of negotiation and intellectual circling. The two timelines echo each other through repeated gestures, images, and phrases that gain different meanings in each context. The connection between timelines is the story's true subject: can the present diverge from the past, and what does it cost?

**Timeline interleaving pattern**: Past / Present / Past / Present / Past / Present / Merged (the final section, where both registers collapse into each other).

**Opening image (PRESENT)**: Ilva at her desk in the Registrar's Office, wearing her gloves — thin kid leather, always worn, explained as a circulation condition. She is processing a stack of binding amendments when her supervisor informs her that the Seat has ordered a full audit of the Ashward family's binding contracts, following irregularities in their latest renewal. She is to work with the Ashward family's designated representative. His name is Daire Ashward. He will arrive this afternoon. Ilva goes still. She knows the Ashward contracts — she has filed them for twelve years. She knows their irregularities better than anyone. The audit will require her to handle the original documents. The original documents are written in binding ink, the substance that reacts to magical proximity. If her hands touch those contracts without gloves, they will know what she is.

**Inciting disruption (PAST)**: Ilva at twenty-two, new to the Registrar's Office, assigned to catalogue damaged binding contracts from the previous century. She works alone in the archive, handling documents that are already corroding — the iron gall ink eating through parchment, text becoming illegible, terms dissolving into holes. She finds a contract from her grandmother's generation — a cessation agreement signed by a woman named Fen Denn, her grandmother's sister, a touch-transformer who was registered, monitored, and eventually restricted from all practice. Ilva reads the terms and her hands begin to change without her willing it. The fasciation: fingers spreading, skin tightening, texture shifting. The contract beneath her bare hands transforms — the dead parchment becomes living fiber, the corroded ink rewrites itself in fresh strokes. She stares at what her hands have done. The contract now reads differently. The original terms are restored, uncorroded, and they are more generous than what replaced them. Her grandmother's sister was promised things that were never delivered. And then Hastra walks in.

**Escalating middle (PRESENT)**: Daire Ashward arrives. He is not what Ilva expected. She expected institutional arrogance — a ward-architect from a powerful family sent to manage the audit in his family's favor. Instead he is quiet, precise, and genuinely interested in the documents. He handles the contracts with the careful attention of someone who has read them before and found them wanting. He asks questions she doesn't expect — not about the Ashward family's privileges but about the original terms, the first-generation Compact, what was promised and what was delivered. He notices the fasciated plants on her windowsill (she can't help it; the herbs she grows for her desk always twist) and studies them with an attention that makes her breath catch.

Their working dynamic establishes itself: she knows the archive, he knows the magical theory. She can locate any document in minutes. He can read the binding structure embedded in the ink — the wards, the clauses, the magical architecture of a contract. They are complementary in a way that is immediately productive and immediately dangerous. Because his ability to read binding structure means he can detect magical residue. If she has ever touched a contract without her gloves, he will know.

**Escalating middle (PAST)**: After Hastra's report, Ilva is brought before a review panel. The scene is written in Miller's elevated register — the panel described as figures on a frieze, immovable, their judgment carrying the weight of stone. They are not cruel. They are procedural. They explain the options: registration or cessation. They explain the consequences of each with the dispassion of people reading a contract aloud. Ilva's hands are still changed — the fasciation hasn't faded — and she sits with them hidden in her lap, feeling the altered texture of her own skin against the fabric of her skirt. She is grieving already. Not for the magic — she barely knows what the magic is. She is grieving for the woman she was performing: the careful clerk, the reliable apprentice, the person whose hands did nothing extraordinary. That woman is dead. The panel did not kill her. Ilva's own body killed her.

She signs the cessation. She puts on gloves for the first time.

**Crisis (PRESENT)**: The audit uncovers a pattern: the Ashward family's binding contracts have been systematically amended over two centuries, each amendment slightly more restrictive than the last, each one transferring a little more authority from individual practitioners to the Seat. The corrosion of the ink is not accidental — it's structural. The contracts are designed to degrade so they must be rewritten, and each rewriting is an opportunity to tighten the terms. Daire sees it. Ilva has always seen it. They look at each other across the table covered in corroding documents and both understand what the audit means: the entire Compact is a mechanism of incremental confiscation, and they are both trapped inside it — he as the family whose power is being siphoned, she as the clerk who has been filing the instruments of her own suppression.

Daire says: "You've known this. You've known it the entire time you've worked here."

Ilva says: "Knowing and being able to act on knowing are different things."

He reaches for her hand — not romantically, professionally, to take a document from her — and she flinches. The flinch is enormous. It fills the room. He notices. He does not ask why. Instead, he sets the document down and says, very carefully, "I am going to tell you something, and I'd like you to hear it as information rather than as a threat. I can read residue. Every contract you've filed in the last twelve years is clean. But there is one document in the deep archive — a cessation agreement from two centuries ago, restored and rewritten in a hand that is not human — that carries a signature I have never encountered before. The Seat doesn't know. I have not told them." A beat. "The signature is on your windowsill."

He's seen the fasciated herbs. He's read the residue. He knows.

**Crisis (PAST)**: The night after signing the cessation, Ilva goes back to the archive alone. She finds the restored contract — her grandmother's sister's cessation, the one her hands rewrote. She reads it in its original, uncorroded form. The terms are clear: Fen Denn was promised that her ability would be studied, not suppressed. That her children would not be flagged. That the cessation was temporary, pending review. None of these promises were kept. The contract was allowed to corrode, and the corroded version — the one the Seat used — omitted every protection. Ilva's hands changed the contract back. Her magic did what the system refused to do: it restored the original terms.

She understands now what her power is. Not transformation for its own sake. Restoration. Her touch returns things to what they were meant to be before corrosion, compromise, and amendment altered them. She understands, and she puts her gloves back on, because understanding makes it worse, not better. What she can do is legal. What she can prove — that the Compact has been systematically corrupted — is political. And she is twenty-two, and alone, and terrified.

**Ending (PRESENT — registers merge)**: Ilva does not deny it. She does not run. She sits across from Daire Ashward in the audit room with its corroding contracts and says: "What are you going to do with that information?"

He says: "That depends on what you want to do with it."

This is the negotiation. Not surrender, not transformation — a negotiation between two people who hold pieces of the same problem. He is an Ashward. His family's authority is being eroded by the same system that suppressed her grandmother's line. His ward-magic depends on the Compact, but the Compact is eating his family alive just as surely as it ate hers. They are on opposite sides of the same corrosion.

Ilva takes off her gloves. Slowly, deliberately, the way Milan's heroines make choices — with full awareness of cost. Her hands are ordinary. They stay ordinary. She lays them flat on the table between the contracts and does not use her power. She shows him what her hands look like at rest: unremarkable, clerk's hands, ink-stained, steady.

"These are my hands without gloves," she says. "They don't do anything unless I choose."

"I know," he says.

"Not flinching is easy," she says. "What comes after is the hard part."

He looks at her hands and then at her face and says: "I am aware of that. Can we talk about terms?"

They talk. The scene is not romantic in the conventional sense. It is two people sitting in a room full of corroding documents, negotiating what it would mean to work together — really together, her power and his power touching the same contracts, her ability to restore original terms and his ability to read binding structure combined into something neither the Ashward family nor the High Seat has accounted for. The terms of their collaboration are specific, practical, and terrifyingly intimate: she will have to touch documents in his presence, and her hands will change, and he will see.

He does not promise to protect her. He promises to be present.

She does not promise to trust him. She promises to start.

The story ends mid-negotiation. They have not touched the contracts yet. They have not begun the work. The last paragraph is Ilva looking at her own bare hands on the table and feeling, for the first time in twelve years, not the grief of the woman she used to perform and not the fury at the system that made the performance necessary, but something newer and harder to name: the specific, physical vertigo of being seen.

**Thread left open**: What happens when they actually touch the original Compact — the founding document, the one from which all amendments flow? Ilva's restoration magic and Daire's structural reading applied to the same contract at the same moment: neither of them knows what it will produce. The collaboration hasn't happened yet. The story ends before the ink dries.

### 4. Key Scenes

1. **Ilva at her desk, told about the audit (PRESENT, opening)**: Establishes the mask — gloves, precision, twelve years of careful invisibility. The reader sees a competent clerk. The subtext is a woman maintaining a containment protocol on her own body. The fasciated herbs on the windowsill are mentioned in passing, unremarkable to anyone who isn't looking. Advances: character, world, stakes.

2. **Ilva in the archive, hands changing over the contract (PAST, inciting disruption)**: The Miller scene. Elevated, interior, the language of transformation. Her hands fasciate for the first time and the dead contract comes alive beneath them. The moment she understands what she can do is the moment she understands what it will cost. The discovery of her grandmother's sister's betrayed terms gives the magic moral weight — this isn't random power, it's restorative justice manifesting through the body. Advances: magic system, backstory, emotional core.

3. **Daire noticing the fasciated plants (PRESENT, escalation)**: He studies the twisted stems on her windowsill with the careful attention of someone who recognizes what disrupted growth looks like. He doesn't say anything. But Ilva sees him looking, and the reader sees her see him, and the tension is in the gap between noticing and naming. This is the Austen beat filtered through Milan: everything that matters is happening in the subtext. Advances: romantic tension, threat escalation.

4. **Ilva before the review panel, signing the cessation (PAST, crisis)**: The Miller-register apex. Figures on a frieze. The procedural language of the panel as a kind of ritual incantation — not cruel but immovable. Ilva's interior grief rendered in classical, elevated prose: she is mourning the death of the self she performed. Her hands still changed, hidden in her lap. The gloves going on for the first time — described with the weight of a transformation, because it is one. Advances: wound, thematic depth, emotional trajectory.

5. **"I can read residue" — Daire's revelation (PRESENT, crisis)**: The Milan-register apex. Everything in this scene is dialogue and micro-gesture. Daire reveals what he knows without threat, without leverage, as pure information. Ilva's flinch when he reaches for her hand is the loudest moment in the story. His response — setting down the document, changing his approach — demonstrates the specific intelligence Milan demands of her love interests: not the dramatic gesture, but the precise recalibration. Advances: romantic pivot, plot convergence, dual-timeline convergence.

6. **Ilva takes off her gloves (PRESENT, ending)**: The registers merge. Miller's transformation and Milan's negotiation occurring simultaneously. The gloves coming off echoes the gloves going on in the past timeline — same gesture, opposite direction. Her hands are ordinary, and that ordinariness is the bravest thing in the story, because she is showing him the version of her that doesn't perform. "Not flinching is easy. What comes after is the hard part." The negotiation of terms — practical, specific, intimate — replaces the conventional romantic confession. Advances: climax, thematic resolution, emotional payoff.

### 5. Emotional Trajectory

**PAST timeline**: Begins in wonder (what can my hands do?) and descends through exposure, judgment, and grief into the specific sorrow of voluntary self-suppression. The emotional register is Miller: mythic, interior, the body as the site of transformation and its cost. The past timeline ends with gloves going on — a small death, the burial of a self.

**PRESENT timeline**: Begins in control (I have survived twelve years) and escalates through disruption (the audit), recognition (Daire's attention), and exposure (his revelation) into the specific terror of choosing to be seen. The emotional register is Milan: sharp, intellectual, the mind as the site of negotiation and its risk. The present timeline ends with gloves coming off — a small resurrection, tentative, unfinished.

**The arc between timelines**: The reader should feel the past timeline's grief pressing against the present timeline's possibilities. Every moment of connection in the present should carry the shadow of what happened last time. The emotional experience of reading should be: the past says this is what it costs, the present says yes, and I'm going to try anyway. The tension is in the "try" — not triumph, not tragedy, but the specific vertigo of a woman who has been hiding for twelve years choosing, with full awareness and open eyes, to show someone her hands.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Madeline Miller (Author A) — Past timeline and interior passages**: Miller's influence governs the past timeline entirely: elevated, classical prose, the body as site of transformation, consequences that feel ordained. Specific passage: Ilva's hands fasciating for the first time, rendered with the same lyrical precision Miller uses for Circe's first witchcraft — the marvel and the horror intertwined, the body becoming something it was never meant to be, the narrator unable to look away from her own transformation. "Her fingers spread and the skin between them tightened until it was translucent, and she could see through her own hands to the contract beneath, the words rearranging like roots finding better soil." Miller also governs the interior voice throughout — Ilva's self-narration has Miller's quality of mythic distance, describing her own life as if it were a story she is reading rather than living.

**Courtney Milan (Author B) — Present timeline and all dialogue**: Milan's influence governs the present timeline: sharp, dialogue-driven, every exchange a negotiation. Specific passage: the scene where Daire reveals he can read residue. No romantic language. No grand gesture. Instead: precise information, carefully delivered, followed by silence in which both characters calculate their next move. "He set the document down. Not dropped it — set it, with the deliberate placement of someone rearranging the pieces on a board." Milan also governs the love story's architecture: the attraction is intellectual first (he is interested in the documents before he is interested in her), the recognition is mutual (she sees his intelligence before she acknowledges her desire), and the resolution is a negotiation of specific terms, not a surrender to feeling.

**Circe (Work X) — Power through exile and self-made identity**: The Circe influence is structural: Ilva's journey mirrors Circe's arc from unrecognized divine to self-made witch. Her magic is discovered, not chosen. Her exile is internal — the cessation agreement, the gloves, the twelve years of performed ordinariness. Like Circe, she finds her power through isolation, and the central question is whether love can exist alongside that power without diminishing it. The tension between divine inheritance and self-made identity maps onto Ilva's tension between her grandmother's hereditary gift and the identity she has built as a clerk. The threat of love is the Circe-threat: that loving someone means allowing them close enough to see the god in you, and gods are frightening.

**The Duchess War (Work Y) — Hiding ability behind performance**: The Duchess War influence is thematic and characterological. Ilva IS Minerva Lane: a woman of extraordinary capability hiding behind a performance of ordinariness because the world punishes women who are too much. The romance structure — intellectual equals recognizing each other — drives the Ilva-Daire dynamic. The personal-as-political theme shapes the plot: their private relationship has public consequences because the Compact governs both their lives, and what they discover in the audit is not personal revelation but systemic corruption. The Duchess War's insistence that the heroine's intelligence is her primary weapon manifests in Ilva's twelve years of archival knowledge: she has been filing the instruments of her own suppression, and she knows the system better than anyone who runs it.

### 7. Title

**"Naming the Terms"**

Works on four levels: (1) the literal act of naming the terms of a contract — what the Registrar's Office does, what Ilva has spent twelve years doing; (2) the specific negotiation at the story's climax, where Ilva and Daire name the terms of their collaboration; (3) the act of naming the thing that has been hidden — Ilva's power, her family history, the system's corruption, made real and speakable by being named aloud; and (4) the romantic register — naming the terms of a relationship, the specific conditions under which two people agree to be known to each other. Does not start with "The." Echoes legal and contractual language rather than romantasy-genre convention (no thorns, no crowns, no flames).

## Constraints Verification

- **Banned names**: No Marcus or Chen. Protagonist is Ilva Denn; love interest is Daire Ashward. Supervisor is Hastra. Grandmother's sister is Fen Denn.
- **Active protagonist**: Ilva acts throughout — she discovers the contract, she endures the panel, she signs the cessation, she flinches, she takes off her gloves, she negotiates. She is never passive.
- **Open thread**: What happens when they actually apply their combined abilities to the original Compact? The founding document has never been read by a touch-transformer and a ward-reader simultaneously. The collaboration is agreed to but not yet executed. The story ends before the work begins.

- **Dual timeline**: Architecturally central. The past is Miller's register, the present is Milan's. The connection between them — echo, contrast, causation — is the story's real subject. Gestures repeat across timelines: hands changing / hands still. Gloves on / gloves off. Contracts corrupted / contracts restored. The past provides the precedent; the present negotiates the divergence.

## Notes for the Writer

### Voice Modulation
The story has two voices, and the writer must be able to shift between them cleanly:

**Past (Miller)**: Elevated, interior, classical syntax. Longer sentences with subordinate clauses. Metaphor drawn from material transformation — stone, glass, bark, ink. The body is the primary instrument of perception. Ilva in the past perceives the world through her hands.

**Present (Milan)**: Sharp, dialogue-driven, modern rhythm. Shorter sentences, more white space, more exchange. Metaphor drawn from negotiation, calculation, and architecture. The mind is the primary instrument of perception. Ilva in the present perceives the world through assessment and calculation.

**Merged ending**: Both voices present simultaneously. The sentence rhythm of Milan's register carrying the mythic weight of Miller's imagery. The negotiation scene should read like a contract being written in ink that glows.

### The Fasciation
The fasciation of Ilva's hands must be described with specificity — not "her hands changed" but the particular way the skin tightens, the fingers spread, the texture becomes fibrous, the faint pattern of growth lines visible beneath the surface. It should be viscerally real and slightly alien. Not beautiful in a conventional sense. Not horrifying. Fascinating — in the word's original meaning: compelling attention, impossible to look away from. The writer should describe it the way Miller describes Circe's first witchcraft: with wonder and dread intertwined.

### Daire
He is not a mystery to be decoded. He is a man doing his own math. His family's power is being eroded by the same system that suppressed Ilva's. He has his own reasons for wanting the audit to uncover what it uncovers. His interest in Ilva is genuine but also strategic — and she should know it, and he should know she knows it, and the mutual awareness should be the foundation of their respect rather than an obstacle to it. He is a Milan love interest: intelligent, precise, capable of recalibration, willing to be changed by what he learns.

### What the Magic Means
Ilva's touch-transformation is specifically restorative — she returns things to their original state, before corrosion, amendment, and compromise. This is not neutral. It is political. The Compact has been systematically corrupted over two centuries, and her magic can undo the corruption. The fact that this ability has been suppressed is not a coincidence; it is the system protecting itself from the one form of magic that can expose its own degradation. The writer should let this implication sit beneath the surface — Ilva barely articulates it to herself, but the reader should feel it pressing upward like ink bleeding through paper.

### Pacing
The interleaving of past and present should create a rhythm of expansion and contraction. Past sections expand (interior, mythic, slow). Present sections contract (dialogue, pace, sharpness). The merged ending should pulse — both registers present, neither dominant, the prose itself negotiating between Miller's transformation and Milan's terms.
