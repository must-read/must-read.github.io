# Story Plan: Peat and Testimony

**Genre**: crime-noir / tartan-noir
**Combination ID**: cn-tartannoir-001

## Style Sources

**Author A — Derek Raymond**: Unflinching British noir. The Factory series protagonist is a nameless detective sergeant from the Department of Unexplained Deaths — someone who investigates cases no one else wants. Raymond's prose is spare but not minimalist; it achieves a stripped, almost coroner's-report precision while somehow breaking your heart. Violence is rendered without glamour — bodies are bodies, not metaphors. Existential despair saturates every page, but the despair is moral, not decorative: his detective keeps going because someone has to speak for the dead. Raymond called his work "the black novel" — mankind driven to the point of madness, dealing with the struggle against "the general contract, whose terms are unfulfillable, and where defeat is certain."

**Author B — Chester Himes**: Harlem noir. Absurdist violence that never lets you forget the system producing it. Himes's prose is lean, muscular, and spiked with mordant wit — his dialogue crackles with vernacular energy and precise comic timing even in the darkest scenes. His world is one where institutional racism is not a theme but the medium through which everything moves, like water. Rage and dark humor coexist because they must. His characters exist in a system that was never broken — it's working exactly as designed. The absurdity is not stylistic; it's documentary.

## Structural/Thematic Sources

**Work X — *Laidlaw* by William McIlvanney**: The foundational tartan noir. A Glasgow detective investigates a murder while confronting the city's class violence, institutional rot, and his own contradictions. McIlvanney's prose is poetic but grounded — "A car is psychologically sterile, a mobile oxygen tent. A bus is septic." The novel insists that crime fiction can be literature. Laidlaw is at war with his own institution and his own marriage — "A maze nobody had ever mapped, an infinity of habit and hurt and betrayal down which Ena and he wandered separately, meeting occasionally in the children." The murder is the surface; the real investigation is into the city itself. "A lot of people had been present at that murder."

**Work Y — *In a Lonely Place* by Dorothy B. Hughes**: The investigation that turns inward. Dix Steele, an ex-pilot and serial strangler, walks through Los Angeles fog while the investigation closes around him. The novel's genius is in its close third person that inhabits the killer's perspective without endorsing it — the reader slowly realizes they have been seeing through predatory eyes. Hughes's prose has a unique rhythm: "He was there for a long time. Lost in a world of swirling fog and crashing wave, a world empty of all but these things and his grief and the keening of the fog horn far at sea. Lost in a lonely place. And the red knots tightened in his brain." The thematic contribution here is the detective who discovers his own complicity — the investigation that turns inward until the boundary between investigator and perpetrator dissolves.

## Risk Card: REVERSE CHRONOLOGY

The story moves backward in time — each scene occurs before the previous one. The effect should be that the reader gains understanding of causes only after experiencing consequences. The emotional arc runs counter to the chronological arc.

**This is architecturally central.** The reverse chronology is the story's moral engine. We open with the body — a dead migrant worker in a peat processing plant. Each section moves backward in time, peeling back another layer of the institutional indifference that produced the death. The structure mirrors peat itself: layers of compressed time, each preserving what the surface has forgotten. The reader experiences consequences before causes, so by the time we arrive at the dead man's first day in the town — alive, with a name, with plans — the weight of everything we already know makes his ordinary arrival devastating. The reverse chronology also means the sergeant's investigation appears first (when we know the least) and his complicity is revealed last (when we understand the most). The reader gains knowledge in the same order as a forensic palynologist reading peat cores: deepest layer first, surface last.

**Target word count**: 5,700 words (window: 5,400-6,000)
**Reading time**: 23 min

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Derek Raymond — *He Died with His Eyes Open*

**Source**: Author A (Derek Raymond), *He Died with His Eyes Open* (1984), opening and representative passages
**Why representative**: Shows Raymond's signature blend of procedural bleakness, bodily specificity, and the detective's compulsion to speak for the dead.

> "He was found in the shrubbery in front of the Word of God House in Albatross Road, West Five. It was the thirtieth of March, during the evening rush-hour. It was bloody cold; and an office worker had tripped over the body when he was caught short going home."

> "His wife was completely mad. From time to time they discharged her from the mental hospital and sent her home, but those spells never lasted long."

> "The crew was in there, a lieutenant and a sergeant; I knew all their uniforms. They sat wearing their goggles, looking grim and practical. They smoked, but not cigarettes. They smoked all over, soaked in petrol; the fumes simmered in the sun."

> "Most people live with their eyes shut, but I mean to die with mine open. We all instinctively try to make death less difficult for ourselves... Existence is blind — neither for you nor against you."

> "I seldom agree with my colleagues because too often their reasoning is based on the results they expect, and as such is generally ill-founded."

> "I am a minor figure for whom no God waits..."

**Style notes for writer**: Raymond's sentences are declarative, short, and blunt. The precision is clinical, almost forensic — he describes horror the way a pathologist would, with controlled specificity rather than flinching away or aestheticizing it. But within that clinical voice, genuine moral outrage burns. The detective feels compelled. He is not cynical — he is angry at a world that expects him to be cynical. Note the absence of hedging, the directness of statement. Raymond never explains emotion; he records physical detail and lets the reader supply the response.

### Sample 2: Chester Himes — *A Rage in Harlem* and Harlem Cycle

**Source**: Author B (Chester Himes), *A Rage in Harlem* (1957) and other Harlem novels
**Why representative**: Shows Himes's muscular prose, vernacular dialogue, absurdist violence, and the way institutional racism is rendered through crime narrative rather than stated as theme.

> "You ain't plowing cotton in Mississippi, you black son of a bitch. This is New York City, the Big Apple, where people drive—" The colored man riding with his girl friend in the back seat leaned quickly forward and yanked at his sleeve. "Man, come back in here and shut yo' mouth," he warned anxiously. "Them is Grave Digger Jones and Coffin Ed Johnson you is talking to. Can't you see that police antenna stuck up from their tail." "Oh, that's them," the driver said, cooling off as quickly as a showgirl on a broke stud. "I didn't recognize 'em." Grave Digger had heard him but he mashed the gas without looking around. Coffin Ed drew his pistol from its shoulder sling and spun the cylinder.

> Jackson is described as "a short, black, fat man with purple-red gums and pearly white teeth..."

> [On Harlem's rooftops:] "waves of gray rooftops distort the perspective like the surface of a sea" and the "murky waters of the fetid tenements, a city of black people who are convulsed in desperate living"

**Style notes for writer**: Himes's prose is lean and propulsive — short declarative sentences that carry enormous energy. Dialogue is phonetically rendered, rhythmically precise, often darkly funny. Violence erupts with absurdist suddenness. There is no hand-wringing about injustice; the injustice is simply the medium the story moves through. The characters are vivid, physical, rendered in quick, sharp strokes. Himes never sentimentalizes — his compassion comes through specificity of observation, not tenderness of tone. For our story: Himes's influence shapes the scenes where institutional racism operates through bureaucratic indifference — the absurdity of a system that functions exactly as designed while producing death.

### Sample 3: William McIlvanney — *Laidlaw*

**Source**: Work X (structure), *Laidlaw* (1977), various passages
**Why representative**: Shows McIlvanney's poetic precision, his ability to render Glasgow as moral landscape, and the way Laidlaw's investigation of a crime becomes an investigation of an entire city's complicity.

> "Glasgow was home-made ginger biscuits and Jennifer Lawson dead in the park. It was the sententious niceness of the Commander and the threatened abrasiveness of Laidlaw. It was Milligan, insensitive as a mobile slab of cement, and Mrs Lawson, witless with hurt. It was the right hand knocking you down and the left hand picking you up, while the mouth alternated apology and threat."

> "A car is psychologically sterile, a mobile oxygen tent. A bus is septic."

> "A maze nobody had ever mapped, an infinity of habit and hurt and betrayal down which Ena and he wandered separately, meeting occasionally in the children."

> "A lot of people had been present at that murder."

> "It's the questions you don't ask that count."

> "People don't give answers, they betray them. When they think they're answering one thing, they're giving an honest answer to something else."

**Style notes for writer**: McIlvanney's prose is more openly lyrical than Raymond's or Himes's, but the lyricism is always grounded — metaphors are drawn from the physical city, from working-class experience, from the body. His Glasgow is not backdrop; it is participant. The key structural lesson from *Laidlaw*: the murder reveals the city. In our story, the death reveals the town. McIlvanney also gives us the detective's personal life as moral counterpoint — the failing marriage, the self-awareness that doesn't redeem. "A lot of people had been present at that murder" — this line is the thesis of our story.

### Sample 4: Dorothy B. Hughes — *In a Lonely Place*

**Source**: Work Y (themes), *In a Lonely Place* (1947), opening and representative passages
**Why representative**: Shows Hughes's atmospheric control, her ability to inhabit a dangerous consciousness, and the novel's central theme of investigation turning inward to reveal the investigator's complicity.

> "It was good standing there on the promontory overlooking the evening sea, the fog lifting itself like gauzy veils to touch his face... there was something in it akin to flying; the sense of being lifted high above crawling earth, of being a part of the wildness of air."

> "that feeling of power and exhilaration and freedom that came with loneness in the sky."

> "A murderer is a murderer as... an actor is an actor. He can stop acting professionally but he's still an actor."

> "He knew beauty and the intensity of a dream and he was meshed in a womb he called happiness"

> "He was there for a long time. Lost in a world of swirling fog and crashing wave, a world empty of all but these things and his grief and the keening of the fog horn far at sea. Lost in a lonely place. And the red knots tightened in his brain."

> "The callous way we'd sit around and map out our plans to kill people. People who didn't want to die any more than we wanted to die... As if we'd been killing ants, not men."

**Style notes for writer**: Hughes's prose is more atmospheric and interior than the other three sources — foggy, rhythmic, hypnotic. She inhabits consciousness from the inside. The key thematic lesson: in our story, the sergeant is not a killer, but the investigation must turn inward — he must discover that he is closer to the system that produced the death than to justice. His complicity is not dramatic (he didn't murder anyone) but structural (he looked where he was trained to look, and the dead man was never in his field of vision). Hughes gives us the template for how a noir protagonist slowly discovers that they have been the wrong person all along — not the detective but the accomplice.

### Selected Concepts (2):

**1. Forensic palynology** — the use of pollen and spore analysis to solve crimes. Peat preserves pollen records going back thousands of years; a forensic palynologist can read a peat core like a chronological record. Pollen grains are nearly indestructible and adhere to surfaces without notice.

**Integration**: The police bring in a forensic palynologist from Edinburgh to examine the peat around the body — not for the crime itself (which is a death by negligence, not a murder in the traditional sense) but because the Health and Safety Executive needs to determine how long the body was in the cutting face before discovery. The palynologist's work becomes a structural mirror for the story itself: reading layers of compressed time, finding what was buried, determining how long something was hidden. The sergeant watches her work and does not understand it but recognizes something in the methodology — the patient, backward reading of evidence, the attention to what ordinary people would overlook. The palynologist becomes a minor character who appears in only one scene but whose discipline echoes through the entire reverse-chronological structure.

**2. Sphagnum moss as WWI wound dressings** — during World War I, sphagnum moss was harvested across Scotland (including from Glasgow collection centers) to make wound dressings for soldiers. The moss absorbs twenty times its volume in liquid, is naturally antiseptic due to its acidity, and was used to staunch bleeding.

**Integration**: The sergeant's grandmother, now dead, had told him stories as a child about women in Greenock gathering sphagnum during the wars — pulling it from the bogs to pack soldiers' wounds. This memory surfaces as the sergeant investigates the peat plant: the idea that the same ground that once provided something that healed is now the ground that swallowed a man. It is NOT used as a heavy metaphor — the sergeant thinks of it once, briefly, in a moment of physical specificity (noticing the moss growing at the edge of the cutting face, remembering his grandmother's hands). The memory gives the peat landscape a personal history for the sergeant, connecting him to the ground in a way that makes his failure to see the dead man's exploitation more intimate and more damning.

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

A police sergeant in a dying Scottish port town investigates the death of a Romanian migrant worker found in the cutting face of a peat processing plant. The story moves backward from the body's discovery to the man's first arrival in the town, and each step backward reveals another moment when the death could have been prevented — by the sergeant, by the town, by the institution — and wasn't. The question the story asks is not whodunit but who allowed it, and whether "allowing" something to happen is different from making it happen.

### 2. Protagonist

**Sergeant Rab Duthie**, 51, Inverclyde Division, Police Scotland. Born in the town, never left. His father worked the shipyards until they closed; his mother cleaned houses in Gourock until her knees gave out. Rab has been a uniformed sergeant for fourteen years — passed over for CID twice, stopped applying. He is good at the job in the way that matters least: the paperwork, the scheduling, the community liaison meetings. He coaches under-12 football on Saturday mornings. His marriage ended seven years ago and he lives alone in a flat above a chip shop that closed during COVID and never reopened.

- **What he WANTS**: To process this death efficiently, file the report, move on. He wants it to be an accident because accidents are manageable — they have forms, procedures, outcomes. He wants the death to be containable.
- **What he NEEDS**: To acknowledge that his own small acts of not-seeing — driving past the plant, nodding to the owner at community council meetings, never asking about the workers' accommodation — are part of the architecture that killed this man.
- **His flaw/blind spot**: Rab believes that decency and complicity are opposites. He is a decent man — genuinely kind, genuinely conscientious — and he cannot conceive that a decent man can be part of a system that kills. His decency has become a shield against self-knowledge. He thinks: I am not the kind of person who lets this happen. But he is the kind of town that does.

### 3. Structure (Reverse Chronology — 6 Sections)

The story is divided into six sections, each moving backward in time. The sections are not labeled with dates or timestamps — the reader must feel the backward movement through contextual details (weather, light, what characters know and don't know). Each section is separated by a line break and a fragment from the palynologist's report (a single sentence about what the peat core reveals at each depth), which functions as a structural spine marking the temporal regression.

**Section 1 — THE BODY (present: day of discovery)**
The cutting face of the Moss Hill Peat Works, 7:40 AM. A machine operator named Tomasz reports that the excavator has unearthed something in the lower cutting face. Rab arrives. The body is partially embedded in peat — dark, compressed, initially mistaken for a bog body (an archaeological find) until someone notices the modern work boots. The dead man is Gheorghe Balan, 29, Romanian national, employed through a gangmaster intermediary. Rab stands at the edge of the cutting face and looks down at a man he has never met and cannot name. The forensic palynologist arrives from Edinburgh. Rab watches her extract a core sample with clinical precision. She explains that pollen trapped in peat preserves a record of what grew above it — seasons, years, centuries, all compressed into layers. "The peat remembers everything," she says. "It's the ground that can't forget." Rab's stomach is bad. He can't eat his sandwiches. He puts them in the bin and doesn't know why.

**Section 2 — THE REPORT (three weeks earlier)**
Rab is at his desk, processing a complaint about noise from the peat plant — a neighbor says the machinery runs too early, before six AM, violating the planning permission. Rab files the complaint. He does not visit the plant. He has driven past it a thousand times — the low corrugated buildings, the stacked peat bales, the occasional figures moving in the cutting face. He has never gone inside. In this section, we see Rab's routines: the football coaching, the community council meeting where the plant owner (Donald Petrie) sits two rows ahead and nods to him, the Sunday walk along the waterfront where the old shipyard cranes stand like abandoned theology. Rab's body is beginning to malfunction in ways he attributes to age: heartburn, a persistent ache in his lower back, difficulty sleeping. He takes paracetamol at four in the morning, standing in the kitchen in his underwear, staring at the fridge light. He does not yet know that Gheorghe Balan is alive, is working twelve-hour shifts in the cutting face, is sleeping in a bothy converted from a shipping container on the plant grounds with five other men.

**Section 3 — THE INSPECTION (two months earlier)**
The Health and Safety inspector, a woman named Janet Fosse, arrives at Moss Hill for a routine visit. Donald Petrie walks her through the operational areas, the drying sheds, the baling machinery. The cutting face is not included in the tour. Janet notes the omission in her report but does not insist. She has forty-seven sites on her inspection list this quarter and seventeen have been carried over from last quarter because the council cut the department's travel budget. She writes: "No significant concerns noted. Next inspection in twelve months." We see the plant through a bureaucratic lens — the forms, the checkboxes, the language of compliance that sounds like language and isn't. Gheorghe Balan passes in the background, carrying a peat-cutting tool (a tushkar — a flanged spade with a right-angled heel, capable of slicing peat with a vertical thrust). He is not inspected, spoken to, or noted. In the inspector's report, the workers do not appear. They are not a category on the form.

**Section 4 — THE ARRANGEMENT (four months earlier)**
Gheorghe arrives at the plant for his first day of work. He came through a chain: a cousin in Craiova who knew a man in London who knew a gangmaster named Darren who supplies labor to agricultural and extraction operations in rural Scotland. Gheorghe has been in the UK for eighteen months. He worked a season picking soft fruit in Fife, then six weeks on a mushroom farm in Angus. The peat plant is different — the work is harder, the hours longer, the accommodation worse. The bothy is a shipping container with plywood partitions, a gas ring, and no hot water. But the pay is marginally better and Gheorghe is sending money to his mother in Craiova and to his daughter, Alina, who is four. In this section we begin to see Gheorghe as a person with interior life — his homesickness, his practical intelligence, his observation of the town that houses him without seeing him. He learns the name Moss Hill. He learns the names of the streets between the plant and the Spar where he buys bread and tinned fish. He does not learn the sergeant's name because they never meet, though they pass within fifteen feet of each other every Tuesday when Rab walks to the community council and Gheorghe walks to the Spar.

**Section 5 — THE TOWN (six months earlier)**
The peat plant is hiring. Donald Petrie mentions this at the community council meeting — casually, as good news, a sign that the local economy is not entirely dead. "Eight new positions," he says. Someone asks: local? Petrie says: "Whoever applies." Nobody asks what "whoever" means. Rab is at this meeting. He takes notes for the minutes. He records: "Mr. Petrie reported eight new positions at Moss Hill Peat Works." He does not record the silence after "whoever applies," or the way Councillor Greig looked at the table, or the way Petrie's tone shifted from pride to something flatter when the question was asked. This section also shows the town's relationship to its own decline — the shuttered shops, the methadone pharmacy, the waterfront where container ships pass on their way to Glasgow and never stop. The town knows what it is. It knows what the plant is. It knows what "whoever" means. It knows and does nothing and calls this knowing a form of helplessness rather than a form of permission.

**Section 6 — THE ARRIVAL (eight months earlier)**
Gheorghe Balan steps off a bus in the town centre. It is a morning in late autumn. He has a bag, a phone number, and a name — Darren — written on a piece of paper. He stands on the pavement and looks at the Firth of Clyde, which is grey and flat and smells of industry that is no longer there. He does not know about the peat plant yet. He does not know about the bothy or the tushkar or the cutting face where, in eight months, his body will be found. He knows that there is work here, and work is what he came for. He walks toward the address Darren gave him — a pub, The Customs House, which is not the customs house — and the town opens around him the way towns open around anyone who is arriving: with indifference, with the ordinary ongoing-ness of a place that was here before you and will be here after. Gheorghe enters the pub. The barmaid is reading a paperback propped against the lager taps. A horse race plays on the television with the sound off. He sits down. He orders a tea. He waits for a man named Darren who will arrive in twenty minutes and shake his hand and call him "George" because Gheorghe is too many syllables for a transaction.

The story ends here. Gheorghe alive, waiting, not yet caught in the machine. The town outside the window. The Clyde moving toward the sea. The reader knows everything that will happen and can do nothing about it. This is the violence the reverse chronology delivers: not the violence of the death (Section 1) but the violence of the ordinary morning before it (Section 6).

### 4. Key Scenes (5)

1. **Rab at the cutting face** (Section 1): The moment Rab looks down at the body and feels something in his stomach — not nausea but recognition, though he doesn't have the word for it. He watches the palynologist work and asks what she's looking for. "Time," she says. This scene must do two things: establish the body as a real death (not a metaphor, not a symbol — specific, physical, with modern work boots and peat-darkened skin) and establish Rab's body beginning to rebel against what his mind refuses to acknowledge.

2. **Rab in the kitchen at four AM** (Section 2): Standing in his underwear with a headache, staring at the fridge light. He notices the sphagnum moss growing on the wall of the alley behind the chip shop — visible through the kitchen window — and remembers his grandmother telling him about the women who gathered it for wound dressings during the war. This memory arrives unbidden, and Rab does not connect it to anything. But the reader does. This scene must be ugly, per Raymond's rule: "Whenever the prose starts to sound good, you're lying."

3. **The inspection walkthrough** (Section 3): Janet Fosse touring the plant with Petrie. The scene is rendered with bureaucratic precision — the checklists, the language of compliance — while Gheorghe passes in the background carrying a tushkar. He is visible to the reader but invisible to the inspection regime. This scene must carry Himes's absurdist edge: the system is not broken. It is functioning exactly as designed. The inspection does not fail. It succeeds — and that success is the crime.

4. **Gheorghe and the tushkar** (Section 4): Gheorghe learning to use the peat-cutting tool. An older worker (Polish, named Krzysztof) shows him how to drive the flanged blade into the cutting face with a vertical thrust, how to lever the peat block free. Gheorghe is good with his hands. He finds a rhythm. For a page, the work has a physical dignity — the tool, the body, the ground. This scene must show Gheorghe as a skilled, specific person, not a victim-template. He notices things: the way the peat smells different at different depths, the color shift from brown to black, the occasional gleam of ancient wood preserved in the lower layers.

5. **Gheorghe at The Customs House** (Section 6, final scene): Gheorghe sitting alone with a cup of tea, waiting for Darren. The barmaid turns a page. The television shows horses. Outside, the Firth of Clyde. This scene must be the most violent thing in the story, per Himes's insight from the author meeting: "A man alive and whole and not yet caught in the machine. That's what the machine destroys — not the body. The body is just evidence. What the machine destroys is the time before. The ordinary morning. The cup of tea in a pub where nobody is going to die."

### 5. The Emotional Trajectory

**Opening (Section 1)**: The reader feels procedural distance — a body has been found, an investigation begins. Standard crime-noir territory. The body is disturbing but narratively familiar. The reader is comfortable in the genre.

**Middle (Sections 2-4)**: The reader's comfort erodes. Moving backward, we see the machinery of indifference — the unfiled complaints, the uninspected cutting face, the gangmaster's handshake. Each section makes the death more preventable and therefore more unbearable. The reader begins to feel implicated: we know how this ends, and we keep reading, and our reading is its own form of watching without acting.

**Closing (Sections 5-6)**: The reader arrives at the beginning and feels the specific grief of witnessing a life that still has a future. Gheorghe ordering tea. Gheorghe waiting. The town ordinary and ongoing around him. What the reader should feel when they put this down: the sick recognition that indifference has a body count, and that the body count is made of specific people with specific names that specific towns could not be bothered to pronounce correctly.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Derek Raymond (Author A)** — Most visible in: **Section 2 (Rab in the kitchen at four AM)**. Raymond's influence shapes the way Rab's body registers what his mind refuses to know. The paracetamol, the underwear, the fridge light, the inability to eat — this is Raymond's "black novel" territory, where the detective's body becomes the site of moral knowledge. The prose here should be blunt, declarative, stripped of ornamentation. Rab does not reflect; he stands and hurts and doesn't know why. "Keep it ugly" — the scene must resist any impulse toward literary beauty. A man with a headache is a man with a headache.

**Chester Himes (Author B)** — Most visible in: **Section 3 (The inspection)** and **Section 5 (The community council)**. Himes's influence shapes the absurdist institutional logic — the system working exactly as designed while producing death. The inspection scene should carry Himes's darkly comic edge: the checkbox that doesn't include a category for "workers," the form that generates compliance without safety. The community council scene — "Whoever applies" and the silence after — is Himes's territory: a room full of people who know exactly what is happening and choose, collectively, to call their knowledge helplessness. The rage should be structural, not expressed — built into the gap between what the forms say and what is real.

**Laidlaw / McIlvanney (Work X)** — Most visible in: **The town itself as character, and Rab's relationship to it**. McIlvanney's structural contribution is the insistence that the crime reveals the city. Our town — with its dead shipyards, its methadone pharmacy, its waterfront where container ships pass and never stop — is Laidlaw's Glasgow rendered as a smaller, more defeated place. The line "A lot of people had been present at that murder" is the thesis of the entire story. McIlvanney's poetic specificity shapes the setting descriptions throughout: the carpet the color of dried blood, the ceiling tiles stained in topographical patterns, the Firth of Clyde smelling of industry that is no longer there.

**In a Lonely Place / Hughes (Work Y)** — Most visible in: **The overall structure of investigation turning inward, culminating in Rab's self-recognition**. Hughes gives us the thematic architecture: the detective who discovers he is closer to the perpetrator than to justice. Rab is not a killer — his complicity is not dramatic but structural. He drove past the plant. He nodded at Petrie. He filed the noise complaint without visiting. He recorded "eight new positions" without recording the silence. Hughes's contribution is the slow, horrifying realization that the investigation has been pointing at the investigator all along — not because he is guilty of a crime, but because his ordinary decency was insufficient. The "lonely place" in our story is the town itself: a place where a man can die and the washing still gets hung.

### 7. Title

**Peat and Testimony**

The title works on multiple levels: the peat that preserves the body and the peat that preserves the pollen record (testimony of time); the formal testimony that the sergeant and others will give at the fatal accident inquiry (testimony as legal process); and the ground itself as a kind of witness — the peat testifies to what the town refused to say. The conjunction "and" implies that peat and testimony are separate things; the story reveals that they are the same thing.

## Additional Craft Notes for the Writer

### Prose style
The dominant register should be Raymond's: blunt, declarative, precise, unbeautiful. But allow McIlvanney's poetic specificity to surface in the setting descriptions — the town, the waterfront, the Clyde. Himes's influence is structural (the absurdity of functional systems producing death) more than stylistic, but his lean, propulsive energy should shape the pacing. Hughes's atmospheric interiority should appear only in Gheorghe's sections — his consciousness is more open, more sensory, less defended than Rab's.

### Reverse chronology craft
Each section should begin with a specific physical detail that orients the reader in time without stating the date. Weather, light, what characters know and don't know. The palynologist's report fragments between sections (single sentences about what the peat core reveals at each depth) serve as structural markers. The transitions should feel like geological layers — not jumpy or fragmented, but compressed and heavy.

### What to avoid
- Do NOT turn the peat into an extended metaphor. The peat is real. It is an industrial material. It preserved a body because of anaerobic conditions and low pH, not because of symbolism.
- Do NOT give Rab a tidy epiphany. He should end Section 1 (which is the story's final scene chronologically) with a physical symptom — the bad stomach, the uneaten sandwiches — not a moral revelation. If he understands what he's done, the understanding should be incomplete and physical, not articulated.
