# Story Plan: sf-timetravel-001

## Combination Spec

- **Genre**: Science Fiction / Time Travel
- **AuthorA**: Ted Chiang -- crystalline precision, thought-experiment, philosophical
- **AuthorB**: Ray Bradbury -- poetic, nostalgic, emotionally warm, lyrical Midwestern sensibility
- **WorkX** (structure): *Slaughterhouse-Five* -- shattered chronology, unstuck in time, nonlinear jumps
- **WorkY** (themes): *The Time Traveler's Wife* -- temporal grief, love across time, the one who stays
- **Target**: 3,700 words (+/-300) / 15 min read
- **Risk card**: none
- **Formula summary**: "Chiang's crystalline thought-experiment precision meets Bradbury's nostalgic lyricism, structured through Vonnegut's shattered chronology and suffused with Niffenegger's temporal grief -- a time travel story where knowing the future is the cruelest form of love."

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Ted Chiang -- *Story of Your Life*
**Source**: AuthorA (Ted Chiang), from the opening of "Story of Your Life" (1998)
**Why representative**: Shows Chiang's signature blend of clinical precision with devastating emotional undercurrent -- addressing the daughter in future-past tense, the thought-experiment embedded in intimate domestic detail.

> Your father is about to ask me the question. This is the most important moment in our lives, and I want to pay attention, note every detail. Your dad and I have just come back from an evening out, dinner and a show; it's after midnight. We came out onto the patio to look at the full moon; then I told your dad I wanted to dance, so he humors me and now we're slow-dancing, a pair of thirtysomethings swaying back and forth in the moonlight like kids. [...]
>
> I remember the scenario of your origin you'll suggest when you're twelve. "The only reason you had me was so you could get a maid you wouldn't have to pay," you'll say bitterly, dragging the vacuum cleaner out of the closet. "That's right," I'll say. "Thirteen years ago I knew the carpets would need vacuuming around now, and having a baby seemed to be the cheapest and easiest way to get the job done." [...]
>
> I remember the morgue, all tile and stainless steel, the hum of refrigeration and smell of antiseptic. An orderly will pull the sheet back to reveal your face. Your face will look wrong somehow, but I'll know it's you.

**Key craft elements to internalize**: Future-tense memory as narrative mode. Clinical precision yoked to emotional devastation. Short, declarative sentences that accumulate grief. The thought-experiment IS the love story.

### Sample 2: Ted Chiang -- *Exhalation*
**Source**: AuthorA (Ted Chiang), opening of "Exhalation" (2008)
**Why representative**: Demonstrates Chiang's method of building alien-yet-rigorous worlds where philosophical inquiry becomes viscerally concrete.

> It has long been said that air (which others call argon) is the source of life. This is not in fact the case, and I engrave these words to describe how I came to understand the true source of life...
>
> If a person is careless and lets his air level run too low, he feels the heaviness of his limbs and the growing need for replenishment.

**Key craft elements**: The declarative opening that reframes received wisdom. "Engrave" -- the physical act of recording knowledge. Systems thinking applied to the body. Every sentence doing philosophical work.

### Sample 3: Ray Bradbury -- *Dandelion Wine*
**Source**: AuthorB (Ray Bradbury), from *Dandelion Wine* (1957)
**Why representative**: Pure Bradbury lyricism -- the childhood sensory catalog, summer as sacrament, concrete objects carrying metaphysical weight.

> And there, row upon row, with the soft gleam of flowers opened at morning, with the light of this June sun glowing through a faint skin of dust, would stand the dandelion wine. Peer through it at the wintry day -- the snow melted to grass, the trees were reinhabitated with bird, leaf, and blossoms like a continent of butterflies breathing on the wind. And peering through, color sky from iron to blue. Hold summer in your hand, pour summer in a glass, a tiny glass of course, the smallest tingling sip for children; change the season in your veins by raising glass to lip...

> It was this then, the mystery of man seizing from the land and the land seizing back, year after year, that drew Douglas, knowing the towns never really won, they merely existed in calm peril, fully accoutered with lawn mower, bug spray and hedge shears, swimming steadily as long as civilization said to swim, but each house ready to sink in green tides, buried forever, when the last man ceased and his trowels and mowers shattered to cereal flakes of rust.

**Key craft elements**: The long sentence that accelerates through sensory images. Concrete nouns doing the work of abstraction. The imperative mood ("Hold summer in your hand"). Present-tense description of remembered experience. The mundane made numinous.

### Sample 4: Kurt Vonnegut -- *Slaughterhouse-Five* (WorkX -- structure model)
**Source**: WorkX structural model, Chapter 2 opening and characteristic passages
**Why representative**: The flat, declarative style. The shattered chronology as emotional strategy. "So it goes" as scar tissue over trauma.

> Listen: Billy Pilgrim has come unstuck in time. [...]

> When a Tralfamadorian sees a corpse, all he thinks is that the dead person is in bad condition in that particular moment, but that the same person is just fine in plenty of other moments.

> So it goes.

**Structural principles to adopt**:
- The jump-cut between temporal locations without transition. One paragraph ends in 1998; the next begins in 2031 with no connective tissue.
- The refrain. "So it goes" in Vonnegut. Our story needs its own recurring phrase -- not a copy, but a structural echo. Something that marks the temporal fractures.
- Flat affect covering deep feeling. The most devastating information delivered in the simplest possible sentence.
- Time as simultaneous rather than sequential. The narrator experiencing all moments at once.

### Sample 5: Audrey Niffenegger -- *The Time Traveler's Wife* (WorkY -- thematic model)
**Source**: WorkY thematic model, Prologue
**Why representative**: The dual-perspective structure. Clare as the one who waits. Henry as the one who vanishes. The relationship defined by absence.

> **Clare**: It's hard being left behind. I wait for Henry, not knowing where he is, wondering if he's okay. It's hard to be the one who stays. I keep myself busy. Time goes faster that way.
>
> **Henry**: [describes appearing] naked as a jaybird, up to your ankles in ice water in a ditch along an unidentified rural route. [...] I hate to be where she is not, when she is not.

**Thematic principles to adopt**:
- The relationship structured around absence, not presence.
- The one who stays develops expertise in waiting -- it becomes a discipline, a science, a form of knowledge.
- Love as asymmetric knowledge: one person always knowing more than the other about their shared future.
- The 152 documented dates -- the impulse to track, to create a record, to make the absences legible.

## Wikipedia Concepts

### 20 Articles Surveyed:
1. **Hafele-Keating Experiment** -- 1971 experiment flying cesium clocks around the world on commercial airliners; proved clocks at different velocities/altitudes tick at different rates; time dilation demonstrated in the real world.
2. **Phenology** -- The study of periodic biological events (first bloom, bird migration) and their relationship to climate. Robert Marsham began recording "Indications of Spring" in Norfolk in 1736; his family continued the record for 222 years.
3. **Cherry Blossom Front (Sakura Zensen)** -- The Japanese practice of tracking the northward progression of cherry blossom season across the country, forecasting bloom dates.
4. **Chronostasis** -- The "stopped clock" illusion where the first thing you see after a quick eye movement appears to freeze in time; the brain backdates perception to fill the gap during the saccade.
5. **Equation of Time** -- The difference between apparent solar time (what a sundial reads) and mean solar time (what a clock reads); the sun runs up to 16 minutes ahead or 14 minutes behind a clock depending on the time of year.
6. **Vernalization** -- Plants requiring prolonged cold exposure before they can bloom; the cold is not optional -- without winter, no spring flowers. An obligate requirement.
7. **Lichtenberg Figures** -- Branching, fractal patterns created by electrical discharge through a solid; lightning captured in glass or acrylic.
8. **Kintsugi** -- Japanese art of repairing broken pottery with gold lacquer, treating breakage as part of the object's history rather than something to hide.
9. **Schumann Resonance** -- Earth's electromagnetic "heartbeat" at 7.83 Hz, generated by lightning in the cavity between the surface and ionosphere.
10. **Persistence of Vision** -- The optical phenomenon where visual perception of an object continues briefly after the stimulus ends; the basis of cinema.
11. **Antikythera Mechanism** -- Ancient Greek analog computer (c. 100 BCE) for predicting astronomical positions; 30 bronze gears in a shoebox-sized case.
12. **Wardenclyffe Tower** -- Tesla's abandoned wireless energy transmission tower, demolished 1917.
13. **Varves** -- Annual layers of lake sediment; each year leaves a visible pair of layers (coarse spring melt, fine winter settlement); geological time recorded in mud.
14. **Whistlers** -- Very low frequency electromagnetic waves generated by lightning that escape into the magnetosphere and descend in pitch as they travel -- named by WWI radio operators for their eerie sound.
15. **Ball Lightning** -- Rare, unexplained luminescent spheres associated with storms; no widely accepted explanation since 1843.
16. **Sidereal Time** -- Time measured by the Earth's rotation relative to distant stars rather than the sun; a sidereal day is ~4 minutes shorter than a solar day.
17. **Water Clock (Clepsydra)** -- Ancient timekeeping by regulated flow of liquid; existed since 16th century BCE in Babylon and Egypt.
18. **Frost Flowers** -- Dendritic ice crystal formations that grow spontaneously on cold surfaces from water vapor; branching instability producing complex structures from simple physics.
19. **Libration** -- The apparent wobble of the Moon, revealing slightly different portions of its surface over time; a 74.6-year elliptical cycle.
20. **Afterimage** -- The visual percept that persists after the stimulus is removed; looking at a bright light and then seeing its ghost wherever you look.

### 2 Selected Concepts:

**1. Phenology / Robert Marsham's "Indications of Spring"**
- **Integration**: The protagonist, Wren, is a phenologist -- she records the first appearances of natural events (first crocus, first robin, first frost) in a hand-ruled notebook, continuing a family tradition. This is how she tracks her husband's temporal displacements. She notices that his arrivals correlate not with calendar dates but with phenological events -- he appears when the dogwood blooms, when the first katydid calls, when frost patterns form on the kitchen window. The natural calendar becomes her clock for measuring his absences. This draws directly from the author meeting's central insight: "staying is a kind of research that leaving can never be." Marsham kept his phenological record for 60 years. Wren will keep hers for however long it takes.

**2. Chronostasis (the stopped-clock illusion)**
- **Integration**: The moment of temporal displacement -- when Wren's husband Eliot vanishes or reappears -- is experienced by Wren as a chronostasis event. The clock on the kitchen wall appears to freeze. The second hand holds. For an instant that feels like thirty seconds but is physiologically 200-500 milliseconds, she sees the world as it was just before the change. The brain backdating perception to fill the gap. This becomes a recurring motif: Wren learning to read the stopped-clock moment, to extract information from that fraction of a second before her perception catches up to reality. It's Chiang's thought-experiment precision applied to a moment of Bradbury's sensory overwhelm. And it gives us the story's structural refrain -- "the clock held" -- marking each temporal fracture the way "So it goes" marks each death in Vonnegut.

## 1. Premise

A phenologist named Wren keeps a hand-ruled notebook of first seasonal appearances -- first crocus, first ice on the birdbath, first evening where you need a jacket -- in a small house in upstate New York. Her husband Eliot, a horologist who repairs antique clocks, has become unstuck in time. Not metaphorically. He vanishes from the kitchen on a Wednesday evening and appears in their bedroom on a Saturday morning three weeks earlier, or six months later, or in a year that hasn't happened yet. He cannot control it. He does not understand it. But Wren -- the one who stays, the one who records -- has begun to notice that his displacements correlate with phenological events, not calendar dates. He arrives with the dogwood. He leaves with the first hard frost. The natural world keeps a different clock than the one on the wall, and Eliot is moving through that clock, not ours.

The question the story asks: What does it cost to be the one who understands the pattern -- and can that understanding ever become anything more than a more precise form of grief?

## 2. Protagonist

- **Name**: Wren Calvetti, age 39
- **Situation**: Phenologist and part-time community college biology instructor in a small town in the Hudson Valley. Lives in a 1920s clapboard house with a kitchen that smells of bergamot and old wood. Has kept a phenological record since she was fourteen, when her grandmother taught her the practice. Married to Eliot for eleven years.
- **What she WANTS**: To predict Eliot's next arrival -- to decode the pattern completely so she is never again surprised by his absence or his return.
- **What she NEEDS**: To accept that knowing the pattern is not the same as controlling it, and that some forms of understanding are indistinguishable from letting go.
- **Her flaw**: Wren believes that if she can make the pattern legible -- if she can write it down, track it, correlate it, predict it -- then she has not lost him. That recording is keeping. That observation is possession. But the record is getting longer and her husband is present for fewer and fewer of its entries.

## 3. Structure

The story is told in **shattered chronology** -- twelve fragments, each labeled with a phenological event rather than a date. ("First frost." "Dogwood bloom." "Katydid call.") The fragments are not in seasonal order. We get "Last monarch sighting" before "First crocus." The reader assembles the timeline the way Wren assembles it: not from calendar progression but from the natural record.

### Beat 1: Opening -- "First ice on the birdbath"
Wren stands in the kitchen at 6:14 AM, November. The clock on the wall holds -- she feels the chronostasis, the second hand frozen in her peripheral vision -- and then Eliot is there. Sitting at the table as if he's always been there, wearing a shirt she threw out two years ago, looking at her with the expression of a man who knows something he can't say. She pours him coffee. She opens the notebook. She writes: *First ice on the birdbath. Eliot present. Duration of previous absence: 47 days.* He says: "The bergamot is stronger this time." She says: "I switched brands." They don't talk about where he's been. The clock resumes.

### Beat 2: Inciting disruption -- "Dogwood bloom"
A fragment from spring, several years earlier. Eliot's first displacement. He was in the garden, kneeling by the dogwood, and then he wasn't. Wren waited in the house for three hours before she understood he hadn't left through the gate. The dogwood was in full bloom. She records this. She will record every subsequent displacement and return, mapping them against her phenological notebook. Over the following months, the correlations emerge: not perfect, not clockwork, but unmistakable. He is entrained to the seasons, not the calendar. In this fragment, we see Wren shift from panic to observation -- the moment she decides that if she can't hold him, she can hold the data. She walks out to the dogwood and counts the open blossoms. Forty-seven. She writes this down, though she doesn't yet know why.

### Beat 3: Escalating middle -- "Katydid call" / "Last swallow" / "Ground frozen solid"
Three fragments intercut. In "Katydid call" (late summer), Eliot returns and doesn't recognize the kitchen renovation -- he's from a version of their marriage three years prior. He asks about the old stove. Wren realizes his displacements don't just move him in time; they move him to different versions of himself. The Eliot sitting at her table has not yet learned that his mother died. She has to decide whether to tell him.

In "Last swallow" (autumn), Wren visits a clock shop in Kingston where Eliot used to work. The owner, a woman named Dasha, shows Wren an 18th-century bracket clock whose escapement is failing. Dasha explains: the escapement is the part that converts continuous energy into measured intervals. Without it, the spring uncoils all at once. "Time isn't in the gears," Dasha says. "Time is in the thing that interrupts the gears." Wren stares at the mechanism. She begins to understand Eliot's condition differently -- not as displacement but as a failure of escapement. His time is uncoiling all at once.

In "Ground frozen solid" (deep winter), Wren is alone. Has been alone for 112 days -- the longest absence. She keeps the notebook. She records the first junco, the pond ice thickness, the date the furnace cycles continuously. She is meticulous. She is breaking. She calls her sister -- the only other person who knows about Eliot -- and her sister asks: "At what point does recording an absence become the absence?" Wren hangs up.

### Beat 4: Crisis -- "Frost flowers on the window"
January. Wren wakes to find frost flowers on the kitchen window -- dendritic patterns, Lichtenberg-figure branches spreading across the glass. And Eliot is in the kitchen. But something is wrong. He is older. Significantly older -- she guesses fifteen years. His hair is white. He moves carefully. He looks at her the way a person looks at a photograph. He knows things: which floorboard creaks, which mug is hers, where she keeps the notebook. He has been here many times -- but from her perspective, this is the first time she's seen this version of him. He asks her to stop. Stop keeping the record. He says: "The pattern isn't what you think it is. You think you're tracking my arrivals. But you're training yourself to see the intervals instead of me. You're becoming a clock, Wren. And clocks don't miss anyone." She doesn't believe him. She opens the notebook. She shows him the correlations -- dogwood bloom, first katydid, frost on birdbath. It's consistent. It's real. He takes her hand and closes the notebook. "I know it's consistent," he says. "I know it's real. That's not the same as it being the thing that matters."

### Beat 5: Ending -- "Vernal equinox (unrecorded)"
March. Wren is in the garden. The crocuses are emerging. She has the notebook in her hand, open to a blank page. The pen is in her pocket. She does not write down the first crocus. She watches it instead. She sees the pale green shoot, the bruise-colored bud, the way the light hits it at this particular angle at this particular hour. She is not recording. She is present. Then the clock holds. The chronostasis. That instant of frozen perception before reality catches up. Eliot is behind her. She doesn't turn around yet. She stays in the held moment -- that 200 milliseconds where the world is still the world before he arrived, where she is still the woman standing alone in the garden watching the first crocus. Then she closes the notebook. She turns. She does not check which version of him this is. She says: "The crocuses are early."

The story ends there. We do not know which Eliot this is. We do not know how long he'll stay. The notebook lies closed on the garden bench. One thread -- the question of whether Wren's phenological data actually predicts his movements or merely correlates -- remains unresolved. The reader, like Wren, must decide whether the pattern is signal or pareidolia.

## 4. Key Scenes

- **The stopped-clock moment in the opening kitchen scene**: Establishes the chronostasis motif, the phenological record, the smell of bergamot, the relationship's texture. Does two things: introduces the temporal displacement AND reveals the nature of their marriage through what they don't say.

- **Wren counting dogwood blossoms after Eliot's first disappearance**: The pivot from wife to observer. She decides to track rather than grieve. Does two things: shows her coping mechanism AND plants the seed of her flaw (she'll count instead of feel).

- **The clock shop conversation with Dasha about the escapement**: The story's conceptual spine delivered through dialogue and a physical object. Does two things: provides Wren (and the reader) a new metaphor for Eliot's condition AND mirrors Wren's own failing mechanism -- her compulsive recording as her own broken escapement.

- **Old Eliot telling Wren to stop keeping the record**: The crisis. Everything she's built -- the notebook, the correlations, the science of her own loss -- is challenged by the person it was meant to serve. Does two things: reverses the power dynamic (the one who leaves finally has more knowledge than the one who stays) AND forces Wren to choose between understanding and presence.

- **The unrecorded crocus**: The ending. Wren chooses not to write it down. Does two things: transforms her relationship to observation (she can look without recording) AND leaves the story open (we don't know if this changes anything).

## 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Opening**: The reader feels the practiced intimacy of a relationship organized around absence -- something between tenderness and exhaustion. The competence of Wren's record-keeping should feel impressive and slightly alarming, like watching someone demonstrate expertise in something no one should have to be expert in.

**Middle**: Growing unease as Wren's record becomes more detailed and her emotional life becomes more compressed. The reader should begin to feel what Wren's sister names: that the record IS the absence. The clock shop scene should produce a cold clarity -- the sense that understanding a mechanism doesn't give you power over it.

**Crisis**: When old Eliot asks Wren to stop, the reader should feel the disorientation of having their empathy redirected. They've been rooting for Wren's tracking, her science, her staying-as-research. Now the object of that research is asking her to put it down. The reader should feel the ground shift -- not certainty about who is right, but the recognition that both positions are true and incompatible.

**Ending**: The reader should feel the held breath of the chronostasis moment -- that suspended instant before the world resolves. Then the release of Wren turning around without the notebook. The feeling should be: the terrifying, specific lightness of choosing presence over record. Not hope exactly. Not resolution. The feeling of having put something down that you've been carrying for a long time, and not being sure whether what you feel is relief or amputation.

## 6. Formula Integration

### Ted Chiang (AuthorA) -- crystalline precision, thought-experiment
**Primary concentration**: The phenological tracking system. The way Wren builds a rigorous, internally consistent model of Eliot's displacements from empirical observation. The prose in her notebook entries should read like Chiang -- short, precise, loaded with implication. "First ice on the birdbath. Eliot present. Duration of previous absence: 47 days." Also: the chronostasis mechanism should be rendered with Chiang-level specificity -- the saccade, the neural antedating, the 200-500ms window. The thought-experiment question ("If you could predict someone's temporal displacement, would you?") drives the entire story.

### Ray Bradbury (AuthorB) -- poetic, nostalgic, warm
**Primary concentration**: The sensory world of the house and garden. The smell of bergamot. The crocuses. The frost flowers on the window. The dogwood in bloom. Every phenological event should arrive as a Bradbury-grade sensory experience -- not described but evoked, not cataloged but tasted. The dandelion wine passage's imperative mood ("Hold summer in your hand") should echo in Wren's relationship to her garden. The ending -- Wren watching the crocus instead of recording it -- is pure Bradbury: the numinous ordinary, the sacrament of paying attention.

### Slaughterhouse-Five (WorkX) -- shattered chronology
**Primary concentration**: The entire structure. Twelve fragments labeled by phenological event, not date, not in seasonal order. The reader is Billy Pilgrim -- unstuck, assembling the timeline from fragments. The refrain "the clock held" functions as our "So it goes" -- marking each temporal fracture, each moment of chronostasis, each displacement. The flat, declarative style of Vonnegut should appear in the transitions between fragments: no connective tissue, no "meanwhile" or "earlier that year." Just the phenological label and the new moment, as if they were always adjacent.

### The Time Traveler's Wife (WorkY) -- temporal grief, love across time
**Primary concentration**: The entire emotional architecture. Wren IS Clare -- the one who stays, who waits, who develops expertise in absence. The 152 documented dates become Wren's phenological notebook. The asymmetric knowledge (Wren knowing things old Eliot hasn't experienced yet; old Eliot knowing things Wren hasn't lived yet) is pure Niffenegger. The question at the center -- "Is this the one who knows about the miscarriage? Is this the one who hasn't met her mother yet? Is this the one who's already said goodbye?" -- from the author meeting, is literalized in every scene where Wren must assess which version of Eliot she's facing.

## 7. Title

**Indicators of Spring**

- Does not start with "The"
- Echoes Robert Marsham's "Indications of Spring" -- the phenological record that anchors the story
- Functions on two levels: the literal phenological markers that Wren tracks, and the broader suggestion of renewal/return that may or may not be trustworthy
- Structurally distinct from existing titles: no "and" construction, no possessive, no gerund. A simple noun phrase. Clinical and warm at the same time -- like the story itself.
- The plural "Indicators" (not "Indications") is deliberately imprecise -- these are signs to be interpreted, not facts to be recorded. The title contains the story's central tension.
