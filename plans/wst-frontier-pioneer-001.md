## Writing Samples

### Source 1: Annie Proulx (Author A) — Brutal, compressed, landscape-saturated prose

**Sample: "The Half-Skinned Steer" — Close Range: Wyoming Stories (1999)**

> "The country poured open on each side, reduced the Cadillac to a finger-snap."

> "Nothing had changed, not a goddamn thing, the empty pale place and its roaring wind..."

> "He felt his swollen nose, palped his chin, tender from the stun of the air bag."

**Sample: "A Lonely Coast" — Close Range: Wyoming Stories (1999)**

> "When you are bone tired of being alone, when all you want is someone to pull you close and say it's all right, all right now, and you get one like Elk Nelson you've got to see you've licked the bottom out of the dish."

**Style notes from literary criticism:**

Proulx's prose is "daring, jagged-edged, and almost abrupt." Her Wyoming stories feature "mostly inarticulate characters struggling with brutal living conditions, while submitting to relentless fates." Her work exhibits a "thorough understanding of the multiple, deterministic processes at play in the evolution of a particular place and of the individuals enmeshed within it." Each word is essential — "removing even one would damage the narrative." The landscape is a character, not atmosphere. Her characters lack introspection and verbal elaboration, generating what one critic called "giant heart-ache" through "muscular" prose that avoids sentimentality.

Proulx herself: "I always have a sharp sense of what the ending must be when I start a story whether fiction or not. And when I reach that pre-determined point I stop writing." Her gift is "capturing dialect; the diversity of settings in her stories match a diversity in language." Despite the brutality, "a curious empathy filters through Proulx's worlds like light through water."

**Why representative:** These passages demonstrate Proulx's compression — the way landscape dwarfs human effort, how physical sensation replaces emotional vocabulary, how a single image ("licked the bottom out of the dish") carries the weight of an entire emotional history.

### Source 2: Larry McMurtry (Author B) — Elegiac, character-rich storytelling

**Sample: Lonesome Dove, Chapter 1 (1985)**

> "Augustus took the jug back to the porch and placed his rope-bottomed chair so as to utilize the smidgin of shade he had to work with. As the sun sank, the shade would gradually extend itself across the porch, the wagon yard, Hat Creek, Lonesome Dove and, eventually, the Rio Grande."

> "As was his custom, Augustus drank a fair amount of whiskey as he sat and watched the sun ease out of the day. The days in Lonesome Dove were a blur of heat and as dry as chalk, but mash whiskey took some of the dry away and made Augustus feel nicely misty inside — foggy and cool as a morning in the Tennessee hills."

> "Augustus often praised the pigs' intelligence in a running argument he had been having with Call for the last few years. Augustus maintained that pigs were smarter than all horses and most people, a claim that galled Call severely."

**Style notes from literary criticism:**

McMurtry employs "a variety of styles, from the elegiac to the rapid narrative, from the hilarious to the mournful." His work features "an elegiac literature of place that demolished old myths of cowboy culture and created new ones." His narratives resist simple nostalgia, "eschewing the romanticization of frontier life" while maintaining emotional warmth. "Characters receive forgiveness and surrogate care, creating tension between brutal circumstances and human compassion." His dialogue captures "the way people actually talk" and carries whole biographies in a few exchanges. He was likened to Faulkner as "a narrator, a story teller, a populator of fiction." His early novels explore "loss and transition experienced by rural communities."

**Why representative:** These passages show McMurtry's gift for embedding warmth inside desolation — the shade moving across an entire world, whiskey turning Texas into Tennessee in a man's mind, two friends arguing about pigs as a way of arguing about everything. Dialogue that characterizes through indirection.

### Source 3: My Antonia by Willa Cather (Work X) — Structure and retrospective narration

**Sample: Book I, Chapter 2 — Jim Burden's first encounter with the prairie**

> "I can remember exactly how the country looked to me as I walked beside my grandmother along the faint wagon-tracks on that early September morning. Perhaps the glide of long railway travel was still with me, for more than anything else I felt motion in the landscape; in the fresh, easy-blowing morning wind, and in the earth itself, as if the shaggy grass were a sort of loose hide, and underneath it herds of wild buffalo were galloping, galloping..."

> "I wanted to walk straight on through the red grass and over the edge of the world, which could not be very far away. The light air about me told me that the world ended here: only the ground and sun and sky were left, and if one went a little farther there would be only sun and sky, and one would float off into them, like the tawny hawks which sailed over our heads making slow shadows on the grass."

**Sample: Book V (Cuzak's Boys) — Jim's return after twenty years**

Jim returns to find Antonia aged but vital: "a stalwart, brown woman, flat-chested, her curly brown hair a little grizzled," yet "whatever else was gone, Antonia had not lost the fire of life." He observes that she "lent herself to immemorial human attitudes," embodying universal truths about work, family, and cultivation. In the final passage, Jim sits contemplating worn wagon ruts and realizes: "Whatever we had missed, we possessed together the precious, the incommunicable past."

**Why representative:** Cather's retrospective narration — the distance between the experiencing self and the remembering self — is the structural backbone of our story. The prairie as both freedom and prison. The narrator who constructs meaning from physical details (wagon-tracks, grass, hawks) rather than abstract reflection. The gap between the symbol and the person.

### Source 4: Little House on the Prairie by Laura Ingalls Wilder (Work Y) — Domestic detail as narrative engine

**Historical/textual details from the Little House tradition:**

The books render survival through the specifics of making a home from raw land. Building a log cabin. Skinning animals and tanning hides to trade for supplies. Twisting hay for fuel when wood was unavailable. Making a button-lamp from a rag and a saucer of grease. The Christmas when they received a tin cup, a penny, and a piece of candy and it was the best Christmas any child ever had.

The sod house / dugout tradition: the first and most practical homes were small rooms dug into the lee side of low rolling hills. Walls built up with sod blocks to seven or eight feet, cottonwood poles laid side by side with prairie grass providing insulation for the roof. A site had to be chosen close to good sources of sod — buffalo grass, slough grass, big bluestem — with strong, dense root networks to hold blocks together. Bricks cut 18 inches wide by 24 inches long, weighing about 50 pounds each. Approximately 3,000 bricks for a 16-by-20-foot house: 150,000 pounds of earth, cut and carried and stacked by hand. The floor moistened and tamped with a fencepost to flatten and harden it. A simple sod house could be built in about a week for less than five dollars.

Advantages: thick walls insulated against summer heat and winter cold, withstood prairie winds better than framed walls, even survived tornadoes with no worse than roof loss. Disadvantages: dirt floors, snakes through the ceiling in warm weather, mud dripping from the roof for days after rain. In a bad rain, the roof came down entirely.

The seasonal cycle as structure: planting, growing, surviving winter. The family as the smallest viable unit of civilization. What you eat, what you burn, what you fear.

**Why representative:** The Little House tradition gives us the granularity our story needs — not just landscape but labor, not just hardship but the specific tasks of staying alive. The weight of a sod brick. The sound of rain on a dirt roof. The practical details that make homesteading real rather than mythic.

## Wikipedia Concepts

### Articles Surveyed (20 concepts via search):

1. **Compass Plant (Silphium laciniatum)** — A prairie perennial whose leaves orient themselves north-south to avoid the noonday sun. Individual plants can live over 100 years. Surveys counted an average of nearly 80 insects per stem.

2. **Controlled Burn / Prairie Fire** — Indigenous practice of intentionally setting fire to shift plant composition from non-native to native species. Fire warms the soil, increases microbial activity, releases nutrients. Occurs in early spring before native plants actively grow.

3. **Ogallala Aquifer** — Shallow water table beneath the Great Plains, 174,000 square miles across eight states. Once depleted, takes over 6,000 years to replenish naturally. Only about one inch of precipitation reaches the aquifer annually. The geological memory beneath every homestead.

4. **Phantom Island** — A purported island that appeared on maps but was later found not to exist. "No cartographer wants to be the one to erase an island without confirmation of its nonexistence." Some remained on maps for centuries before being "un-discovered."

5. **Bone China** — Porcelain made from calcined animal bones (50% bone ash). Cattle bones crushed, degelatinized, and fired at 1000-1250 degrees Celsius. The refined beauty of teacups emerging from the rendered skeleton of livestock.

6. **Soil Horizons** — The layered structure of a soil profile: O (organic), A (topsoil), E (eluviation), B (subsoil), C (parent material), R (bedrock). The A horizon is the zone of both biological activity and loss. Over time, clays and dissolved compounds are leached out.

7. **Dust Pneumonia** — Fatal condition during the 1930s Dust Bowl: dust settles into the alveoli, stops the cilia, prevents the lungs from clearing themselves. By the mid-1930s, dozens dying in Kansas alone. Children, infants, and the elderly most vulnerable.

8. **Somatic Marker Hypothesis** — The theory that emotions and bodily sensations guide decision-making. Over time, physiological changes (muscle tone, heart rate, posture) become associated with particular situations and their past outcomes. The body remembers what the mind forgets.

9. **Mycorrhiza** — Fungi that form symbiotic associations integrated into the physical structure of plant roots. Emerged 450-500 million years ago. The fungi obtain sugars from the plant; the plant obtains phosphate and minerals from the soil via the fungal network. A hidden underground web of mutual dependence.

10. **Public Land Survey System / Plat Books** — The township-range-section grid that parceled the American West. One quarter-section (160 acres) per Homestead Act claim. A mathematical abstraction laid over living landscape.

11. **Phantom Limb** — 80-100% of amputees experience sensations in the missing limb. The body's neural map outlasts the body itself. "The experience of the body is created by a wide network of interconnecting neural structures" — the body as remembered architecture.

12. **Palimpsest** — A manuscript scraped clean and overwritten. The original text eventually bleeds through. The prairie as palimpsest: Pawnee land scraped clean, homesteads written over it, the original always showing through.

13. **Desire Path** — An unplanned trail formed by erosion from repeated foot traffic. "As few as 15 passages over a site can be enough to create a distinct trail." People voting with their feet against the planned route. The path that reveals what is actually wanted versus what was designed.

14. **Solastalgia** — "The homesickness you have when you are still at home" — distress caused by negatively perceived environmental change while still inhabiting the changed place. Coined 2003. A word for the precise grief of watching your landscape become unrecognizable without leaving it.

15. **Dead Reckoning** — Navigation by estimating position from a known starting point using speed, direction, and elapsed time, without external landmarks. Errors are cumulative. Each estimate is relative to the previous one, so mistakes compound over time. Navigation by faith and arithmetic.

16. **Clinker Brick** — Bricks that were too close to the fire in the kiln, emerging harder, darker, misshapen, split. Originally discarded as defective. Around 1900, architects salvaged them as "usable, distinctive, and charming." Modern kilns cannot produce them. The beautiful thing that only comes from being damaged.

17. **Birdstone** — Prehistoric abstract bird-shaped stone carvings by Native Americans, mostly carved from dark slate. Found primarily in the Great Lakes region. Exact purpose unknown — possibly atlatl weights, possibly mat-weaving tools, possibly ceremonial. Objects whose meaning outlived their makers.

18. **Vernix Caseosa** — The waxy white coating on newborn skin. Latin: "cheesy varnish." Protective, antimicrobial, moisture-retaining. The first layer between a new body and the world.

19. **Liminality** — The threshold state during a rite of passage when participants no longer hold their pre-ritual status but have not yet achieved the new one. "Betwixt and between." The pioneer as permanent liminal figure: no longer from the old place, not yet belonging to the new.

20. **Proprioception** — The body's sense of its own position in space. Mediated by receptors in muscles, tendons, and joints. How you know where your hand is in the dark. The body's knowledge of itself.

### Two Concepts Selected for Integration:

**1. Compass Plant (Silphium laciniatum)**

Integration: The compass plant will be a recurring physical presence on the homestead — Edith discovers them growing near the claim during the first summer and learns from a neighboring settler that the leaves orient north-south. This becomes both a practical navigation detail (on the featureless prairie, the compass plant is how you find your way home) and a metaphor that the narrator carries forward: the idea of a living thing that has already figured out how to orient itself on this land, when the human settlers cannot. The narrator, as an old man looking back, will remember the compass plants outliving everything — the house, the woman, the fences. A plant that can live 100 years, standing on ground where nothing else survived.

**2. Desire Path**

Integration: The story will include the beaten path between the soddie and the well — a desire line worn into the prairie by daily trips, the most fundamental mark the family leaves on the land. When the narrator returns decades later, this path will be the only trace of habitation: a faint depression in the grass where thousands of passages wore the ground bare. Not the soddie (collapsed), not the fence posts (rotted), but the desire path — the trace of the body's repeated need. This connects to the somatic marker concept without naming it: the land remembers the body's labor as a worn groove, the way the narrator's own body remembers Edith through the motions she made rather than her face.

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

An old man named Arlo Falk sits in a hotel room in Grand Island, Nebraska in 1923, unable to sleep, and writes down everything he can remember about Edith Vanek — the Bohemian homesteader on the neighboring claim who raised six children in a sod house and died at forty-one with her hands still moving in her sleep. The writing is an act of desperation: he has realized he can remember every task she performed but cannot recall her face, and he is trying to write his way back to the woman behind the labor. The story is his document — retrospective, precise about physical detail, wrong about its own motives.

### 2. Protagonist

- **Name**: Arlo Falk
- **Age**: 67 at the time of writing (1923); 12-19 during the events remembered (1868-1875)
- **Situation**: A retired railroad surveyor staying overnight in Grand Island on his way to Omaha, where his daughter's family lives. He has been to the old homestead site that afternoon and found nothing but grass, a depression where the soddie was, and compass plants.
- **WANT**: To preserve Edith's memory before it dissolves completely — to get her face back through accumulating enough detail.
- **NEED**: To understand that his reverence for Edith was also a form of erasure — that by turning her into a monument to endurance, he lost the actual woman, and that the loss is not just of memory but of attention he failed to pay when she was alive.
- **FLAW**: Arlo mistakes devotion for understanding. He watched Edith labor every day for seven years and believed he was seeing her. He was seeing her work. He never once asked her what she was thinking during the one private moment she allowed herself — sitting on the overturned bucket outside the soddie at dusk, watching the grass. His narration is unreliable not because he lies but because his admiration is a kind of blindness.
- **SPECIFIC WOUND**: When Arlo was sixteen, Edith's youngest daughter, Mila, died of a fever in January. Arlo's mother sent him with broth. He arrived to find Edith sitting at the table with the dead child in her lap, not crying, not moving, just holding the child and looking at the wall. He stood in the doorway for a full minute before she noticed him. When she looked up, her expression was one he has never been able to name — it was not grief, not anger, not blankness. It was something he had no word for, and in fifty years he has never found one. This is the face he cannot remember: not her everyday face, but the face she wore when her daughter was dead and no one was watching.

### 3. Structure

**Opening image/scene**: Arlo in the hotel room in Grand Island, 1923. He has a pencil and a ledger book he bought at the general store. He cannot sleep. He visited the homestead site today — walked the quarter-section where the Vanek claim was — and found nothing. A depression in the earth. Compass plants. The faint trace of a path from where the house was to where the well was. He begins to write because he is afraid that if he sleeps, the visit will have finished the work that time started, and Edith will be fully gone.

**Inciting disruption (in the remembered past)**: The Falks arrive on the neighboring claim in the spring of 1868. Arlo is twelve. The Vaneks — Edith, her husband Josef, and their three children — are already there, living in a dugout cut into the side of a rise. Arlo's first sight of Edith: she is cutting sod, alone, stacking bricks for the new house that will replace the dugout. She does not look up. She is lifting fifty-pound bricks with a rhythm that suggests she has been doing this for hours. This is the disruption of his understanding of the world: a woman doing work he associates with men, doing it without complaint or commentary, doing it as if the earth were something she was having a conversation with.

**Escalating middle**: The narrative moves through seasons, but NOT in orderly sequence. Arlo's memory jumps: the summer the well went dry and Josef walked nine miles to the creek. The autumn Edith taught Arlo's mother to make headcheese from a hog they butchered together. The winter of the blizzard that buried the soddie to the chimney and they burned twisted hay because the fuel was gone. The spring Edith planted onions in a line so straight it looked surveyed. Josef's increasing silence — a man being ground down by the land in a way Edith is not, or in a different way. The birth of the fifth child, then the sixth. Arlo, growing older, becoming aware that what he feels watching Edith is not simple admiration but something tangled with desire he will not name for fifty years.

**Crisis point**: Mila's death. January 1874. The scene at the table with the dead child. But the crisis is not the death itself — it is what happens after. Josef leaves. Not immediately — over the course of that spring. He stops working. He sits. He stares. One morning he is gone, and Edith says nothing about it, not then and not ever. She does not explain. She does not weep. She goes to the well, she comes back, she feeds the children. Arlo's family helps where they can. But the silence around Josef's departure is absolute, and Arlo learns — this is the moment of his education — that there are kinds of loss that cannot be spoken because speech would give them a shape they do not have.

**Ending**: Back in the hotel room. Arlo has been writing for hours. He realizes he has written everything — the sod, the onions, the blizzard, the well, Mila, Josef's vanishing, the compass plants, the desire path — everything except her face. He can describe the hands. He can describe the way she wrung a washcloth. He can describe the sound she made humming — a Bohemian song he never learned the name of. But the face is gone. And then the final passage: he remembers the moment on the bucket. Every evening, for years, she sat on the overturned bucket outside the soddie for five or ten minutes before dark. He never asked what she was thinking. He never will. He puts down the pencil. He does not resolve the question. The story ends with Arlo looking at the pages he has written and understanding — not as insight but as a physical sensation in his chest — that the work outlasted the woman, that what he preserved was her labor and not her self, and that this might be the only kind of remembering the prairie allows.

**Open thread**: Whether Arlo's failure to remember Edith's face is a failure of his memory, his love, or the fundamental nature of how men looked at women who labored — this question is posed but not answered. Also unresolved: what Edith was thinking on the bucket. That silence belongs to her.

### 4. Key Scenes (5)

1. **The sod-cutting scene** (1868): Arlo's first sight of Edith, cutting and stacking sod bricks for the new house. The physical details are precise: the weight of the bricks, the damp-to-dry spectrum required for stacking, the three thousand bricks needed. This scene establishes the body-as-instrument-of-survival motif. Arlo watches from across the grass and does not know he is watching something he will spend his life trying to understand. *Advances*: introduces Edith, establishes the physical reality of homesteading, plants the seed of Arlo's lifelong fascination.

2. **The hog-butchering scene** (autumn, ~1870): Edith and Arlo's mother butcher a hog together while the men are away. Arlo, fourteen, assists. The scene is dense with the sensory details of rendering: the smell, the heat, the slippery weight of intestines, the precision of the cuts. Edith talks more during this scene than any other in the story — short, practical sentences about where to cut, how long to boil, what to save. Arlo realizes this is conversation for her — the labor is the language. A moment of genuine warmth between the two women, expressed entirely through competence. *Advances*: reveals Edith's relationship to community (she gives, she teaches, she does not ask), shows the McMurtry warmth laced through Proulx-level physical detail.

3. **The blizzard scene** (winter, ~1872): Three days buried. The fuel is gone. They twist hay into knots and burn it — each knot lasting minutes, the smoke thick and sweet. The children cough. Edith moves among them in the dark soddie (the snow has blocked the one window), touching foreheads, adjusting blankets, humming. The sound of the wind is so constant it becomes inaudible, replaced by the sound of the hay burning. Arlo's family is in their own house, but Arlo (sixteen) has been helping with the Vaneks' stock and is trapped here. He sleeps on the floor. He wakes to find Edith still awake, still moving, and he understands for the first time that she does not stop. That the labor is not something she does but something she is. *Advances*: escalates the physical threat, deepens the intimacy between Arlo and the Vanek household, establishes that Edith's endurance is both her strength and the thing that makes her invisible — she is so constant that she becomes background.

4. **Mila's death** (January 1874): Arlo arrives with broth. The scene in the doorway. Edith at the table with the dead child. The face he cannot name. He stands there, and she looks up, and what passes between them is the moment around which his entire life will organize — not because it was the worst thing he saw, but because it was the only time he saw Edith without her labor, without her motion, without the constant activity that constituted her identity. She was still. And in that stillness he glimpsed something he was not equipped to understand: a woman who was not her work. *Advances*: the emotional core of the story, the wound that drives the narration fifty years later, the crisis of seeing versus understanding.

5. **The bucket scene** (recurring, but placed last in the narrative): Every evening, Edith sits on the overturned bucket and watches the grass. Arlo describes this from multiple points in time — when he was thirteen and it was just something the neighbor did, when he was sixteen and he watched her watching and felt something he could not name, when he was nineteen and leaving for the railroad and saw her on the bucket from a distance and almost turned back. The final iteration is in the hotel room: Arlo trying to write the scene and realizing he cannot write what she was thinking because he never asked and she never said. The bucket scene is the story's spine — it returns and returns, each time meaning something different, each time the same woman sitting on the same bucket watching the same grass. *Advances*: the central image of the story, the thing that cannot be explained, the private moment that belongs to Edith and not to the narrator.

### 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning**: The reader enters the story in the mode of quiet attention — an old man writing in a hotel room, the mood is intimate, late-night, confessional. There is a gentle pull: what is he trying to remember?

**Middle**: The remembered scenes generate a slow accumulation of physical sensation — the reader feels the weight of the sod, the heat of the butchering, the cold of the blizzard. The warmth of community (McMurtry) is always present but always threatened by the indifference of the land (Proulx). The reader begins to admire Edith as Arlo admires her — and therein lies the trap, because the reader is being seduced into the same error.

**Crisis**: At Mila's death, the reader should feel a floor drop out — not from the death itself (child death on the frontier was common and the reader knows this) but from the glimpse of Edith outside her labor. The reader should feel the vertiginous recognition that they, too, have been watching Edith work and calling it knowing her.

**End**: The reader should feel the specific ache of a remembered thing that was never fully seen — not nostalgia, not grief, but the hollowness of realizing that attention is not the same as understanding. The compass plants are still there. The woman is gone. The path from the house to the well is a faint line in the grass. What the reader feels at the end is the weight of what was never asked.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Annie Proulx (Author A) — most visible in**: The sod-cutting scene and the blizzard scene. Proulx's influence should saturate every sentence about the physical world — the compressed, brutal precision of labor, the land as an indifferent force that does not care whether you survive. The prose in these scenes should be dense, jagged, image-driven. No hedging, no emotional vocabulary. The body broken by the land. The sentence: "A house takes three thousand bricks. That's a hundred and fifty thousand pounds of earth, cut and carried and stacked by hand." This is pure Proulx: the arithmetic of endurance.

**Larry McMurtry (Author B) — most visible in**: The hog-butchering scene and in Arlo's narrating voice throughout, especially when he reflects on Josef. McMurtry's influence is the warmth — the way dialogue carries whole biographies, the way competence is a form of love. The two women butchering together, talking in short practical sentences, creating intimacy through shared skill. Also in Arlo's digressive, elegiac narrating voice — the way he circles back to things, tells you something and then tells you what he thought about it and then tells you he was wrong. McMurtry's characteristic blend of humor and loss should surface in Arlo's voice: dry, self-aware, rueful.

**My Antonia (Work X) — most visible in**: The overall structure of the narrative — a man looking back across decades at a woman who embodied the frontier, constructing her through accumulated detail, aware that the construction is also a distortion. The return to the homestead site and the gap between what is remembered and what is. The final line about the "incommunicable past" should echo without being quoted. Also the specific image of wagon ruts / the desire path — the worn trace as evidence of a life lived.

**Little House on the Prairie (Work Y) — most visible in**: The domestic detail throughout — every scene about what they eat, what they burn, what they fear. The seasonal rhythm. The specific tasks: twisting hay for fuel, making headcheese, the button-lamp. The Christmas detail (the penny in the tin cup) should find its echo in some small gift or small sufficiency that the story renders with the same Wilder-quality of making scarcity feel like abundance. The sod house construction details — 50-pound bricks, the sweating walls, the snakes in the ceiling — all come from this tradition.

### 7. Title

**Grass Over the Wanting**

This title avoids starting with "The." It evokes the central image — grass growing over the traces of human desire on the prairie — while carrying the ambiguity of "wanting" (both desire and lack). It suggests the land's indifference to human need, the way the prairie reclaims everything, and the narrator's own wanting — his desire to remember what the grass has covered. It does not duplicate any existing title structure in the catalog.

## Notes for the Writer Agent

### Prose register:
The prose should alternate between two registers: (1) Arlo's narrating voice, which is warm, digressive, and self-aware — McMurtry's elegiac mode — and (2) the remembered scenes, which should shift into Proulx's compressed, brutal, image-driven present. The remembered scenes should feel physically immediate even though they are being recalled from fifty years' distance. The narrating voice should acknowledge this gap ("I am telling you she cut sod as if I saw it yesterday, but I am telling it wrong — memory smooths everything, even the blisters").

### POV:
First person, retrospective. Arlo writing in the hotel room. The frame is light — we are in the hotel room at the beginning and end, with brief returns to the frame between major scenes. The majority of the story is in the remembered past.

### Pacing:
The remembered scenes should NOT proceed chronologically. Arlo's memory jumps. He starts with the sod-cutting because it was the first thing he saw. He jumps to the blizzard because the cold is in his hands tonight. He circles back to the butchering because he is hungry. The hog-butchering leads to Josef, which leads to Mila, which leads to the table, which leads to the bucket. The non-linearity should feel natural — the way a person actually remembers, not the way a story is typically organized.

### What to avoid:
- Do NOT give Edith an internal monologue. She is seen entirely from outside. Her silence is not a deficit to be filled but a boundary to be respected.
- Do NOT resolve whether Arlo loved Edith romantically. The story should make it clear that his feelings were complicated — admiration, desire, something parental, something filial — and leave the tangle intact.
- Do NOT have Arlo achieve a tidy epiphany. His understanding at the end is partial, physical (a sensation in his chest), and does not resolve the central question.
- Do NOT end with weather as metaphor. End with the pencil, the pages, the silence.
- Do NOT use the words: tapestry, testament, delve, journey (as metaphor), resilience, indomitable.

### Specific details to include:
- The compass plant leaves orienting north-south (Arlo learns this from Edith or a neighbor; it becomes part of how he navigates the featureless prairie as a boy)
- The desire path from soddie to well (described in the remembered past and found again in 1923)
- The weight of sod bricks: 50 pounds each, 3,000 for a house
- Twisted hay as fuel
- Headcheese preparation (rendered from hog skull)
- Edith humming a Bohemian song (never named, never identified)
- Josef's silence and departure (no explanation given, ever)
- The one window of the soddie, blocked by snow during the blizzard
