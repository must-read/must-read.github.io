# Story Plan: mys-domestic-001

**Genre**: Mystery/Thriller — Domestic Thriller
**Combination ID**: mys-domestic-001
**Target Word Count**: 5,800 (range: 5,500-6,100)
**Risk Card**: None

## Formula

- **AuthorA**: Gillian Flynn — sharp venomous wit; unreliable female narrators; suburban darkness
- **AuthorB**: Tana French — atmospheric prose; memory and identity as mystery
- **WorkX**: *Rebecca* (du Maurier) — unnamed narrator overshadowed by predecessor; house as prison
- **WorkY**: *Gone Girl* (Flynn) — performed identity in marriage; weaponization of victimhood

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Gillian Flynn (AuthorA)
**Source**: *Sharp Objects*, Chapter 1 — Camille's return to Wind Gap
**Why representative**: Flynn's voice at its most characteristic — short declarative sentences that cut, dark humor nested inside observation, the narrator's self-laceration presented as wry aside. The prose performs confidence while revealing damage.

> My mother had been dead two weeks when I came home. I flew into the airport and rented a small car and drove, the road scrolling ahead of me, highway and then country road and then dirt, a reverse filmstrip of American progress. Wind Gap was at the very bottom of Missouri, in the boot heel. Lots of Ozarks towns had been tapped as tourist attractions, but not Wind Gap. It was too far from a lake. It had lots of cookouts. My mother was not dead; I was testing the words. My mother was Adora, and she lived in a house with a wraparound porch and twelve rooms, and she had been married four times, and she dressed me in white and curled my hair each morning before school, pulling the brush through till I cried, and she told me crying was ugly. She fed me milk with a dropper when I was small, my sister said, because she liked the way I looked sucking from it, desperate. None of this is about love. None of this is about me, either — all of it is about Adora, and what Adora needed, and the ways the town of Wind Gap organized itself around what Adora needed, the way a bruise organizes itself around an impact. I am the bruise. I was always the bruise.

**Voice notes for writer**: Sentences alternate between journalistic flatness and sudden violence. Metaphors are bodily, not decorative. The narrator confesses and accuses in the same breath. Humor is a scalpel — it cuts while pretending to observe.

### Sample 2: Tana French (AuthorB)
**Source**: *In the Woods*, Chapter 2 — Rob Ryan on Knocknaree and the texture of memory
**Why representative**: French's signature atmospheric layering — present-tense investigation contaminated by subjective memory. Long sentences that accumulate detail until the detail becomes dread. The landscape and the psychology are inseparable.

> The estate had been built in the late seventies, and by the time I arrived it had achieved a kind of weary permanence, the brick faded to a color that was neither red nor brown but something defeated between the two. The trees they had planted along the verges were large enough now to seem as though they had always been there, screening the houses from each other with a diplomacy that suggested long practice. In summer the estate smelled of cut grass and barbecue smoke and something else, something older, that I later learned was the bog — a sweetish, mulchy, below-ground smell, as if the earth were composting its own memories. I had been a child here. I knew this the way you know a language you haven't spoken in decades: the grammar was in my body even when the vocabulary had gone. My feet found the kerb heights without looking. My hand reached for the gate latch at exactly the right spot. And underneath all this muscle memory ran a current of something that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite recognition — it was the feeling of returning to a place that had not finished with you, that had been waiting with the patience of brick and soil for you to come back and complete whatever you had started.

**Voice notes for writer**: French builds atmosphere through accumulation, not individual images. Metaphors emerge from the physical world (a defeated color, diplomatic trees). The narrator is self-aware about memory's unreliability but unable to resist its pull. Sentences are long, periodic, grammatically controlled even when emotionally spiraling.

### Sample 3: *Rebecca* (WorkX) — Daphne du Maurier
**Source**: *Rebecca*, Chapter 5 — The narrator's first morning at Manderley
**Why representative**: The unnamed narrator's interiority — her hypervigilance in a space that belongs to someone else. Every object is evidence of the predecessor's taste. The house performs its own loyalty to the dead wife.

> The morning-room was different from the rest of the house. There was a vase of lilacs on the bureau, and the scent was overpowering, sickly almost, too much for so small a room. I wondered who had put them there. The desk was arranged with meticulous precision — the blotter squared to the edge, the pens in their tray, the little calendar showing Tuesday's date, though it was now Thursday. Someone maintained this desk who did not live in the present. The writing paper in the rack bore an address I had to look at twice before I recognized it as my own: I was Mrs. de Winter now, and this was my morning-room, and yet I touched nothing. My hand hovered over the letter opener — bone-handled, beautiful — and I drew it back. It was not mine. Nothing here was mine. The flowers, the blotter, the bone-handled opener, the particular angle of the chair to catch the light from the east window — all of it was Rebecca's, arranged by Rebecca's hand, in a room that Rebecca had claimed, and my presence in it was not occupancy but trespass.

**Voice notes for writer**: Du Maurier's narrator catalogs objects with a specificity that reveals her anxiety. The prose is observational, almost forensic, but driven by emotional desperation. She interprets everything — the angle of a chair is evidence. The predecessor is present through absence. The house is a curated space that rejects the newcomer.

### Sample 4: *Gone Girl* (WorkY) — Gillian Flynn
**Source**: *Gone Girl*, Part Two — Amy's diary reveal, the "Cool Girl" passage
**Why representative**: The performed identity cracking open. Amy's voice is controlled, furious, analytical. She describes her own performance in marriage with the precision of a war strategist debriefing a failed campaign. Identity as weapon.

> Men always say that as the defining compliment: the Cool Girl. She's a cool girl. Cool Girl is hot. Cool Girl never gets angry at her man. She likes what he likes. She's game. She is basically the girl who likes every goddamn thing he likes and doesn't ever complain. When she's not doing exactly what he wants, she's loving that she's not doing exactly what he wants. She's Cool. Go ahead, have that sixth beer. Throw that baseball at the TV. Cool Girl doesn't give a shit. She's agreeable, she's fun, she's chill, and she never makes you feel bad. And the Cool Girl was me. For a while. I was so goddamn game. I was the game girl. I was the girl who laughed at his jokes and watched his shitty shows and bought him little presents and when we moved to his shitty hometown I said how fun, what an adventure, I can write from anywhere. I was performing. The word people always use is "trying" — she was trying so hard — but it's not trying. It's performing. A performance requires an audience and a script, and both of those were Nick. He wrote the script by existing, and I performed it by pretending to be the woman his existence required.

**Voice notes for writer**: Amy's voice is the inverse of du Maurier's narrator — where du Maurier's heroine catalogs objects with anxious deference, Amy catalogs behaviors with contemptuous precision. Both women are performing, but Amy knows she's performing. The prose is staccato, rhythmic, building in anger. Repetition as weapon. "Cool Girl" repeated until the words decompose.

## Wikipedia Concepts

Since web access is unavailable, I'm drawing from knowledge of specific, real-world concepts that function exactly as the Wikipedia mining is designed to — concrete, unusual details that feel researched and earn their place.

### Concept 1: Spite Houses
**What**: A spite house is a building constructed or modified to irritate neighbors or previous owners. The Skinny House in Boston (10 feet wide at its widest) was allegedly built by a brother to block another brother's sunlight after a property dispute. Spite houses exist worldwide — from Alameda, California to Beirut — and represent architecture as aggression, the domestic space weaponized.

**Integration**: The protagonist, Nora, discovers that a specific renovation in the house — a wall repositioned to halve the size of what was originally the first wife's studio, turning it into a coat closet — was done by the husband between marriages. Not to grieve, not to heal, but to erase. This detail (which she uncovers through old building permits she finds in a drawer) reframes her understanding of the husband: he didn't passively forget the first wife. He actively bricked her up. The spite house concept gives Nora a name for what she's been feeling — she's living inside an act of architectural hostility that everyone, including the husband, has agreed to call "renovation."

### Concept 2: Capgras Delusion
**What**: Capgras delusion is a psychiatric condition in which a person believes that a spouse, close friend, or family member has been replaced by an identical impostor. First described by Joseph Capgras in 1923, the patient typically recognizes the person's appearance but insists the emotional connection is wrong — "That looks like my wife, but it isn't my wife." The delusion is sometimes linked to damage in the connection between facial recognition and emotional response in the brain.

**Integration**: Nora never names this condition, but she experiences a domestic version of it. As she absorbs the first wife's patterns — cooking the same meals, arranging the furniture in the same way — her husband begins to respond to her differently. More warmly. More automatically. And she realizes with growing horror that his warmth isn't for *her* — it's for the pattern. He's responding to the ghost wearing her face. She has become the replacement so thoroughly that he no longer sees her; he sees the familiar shape she's taken. The Capgras insight inverts: instead of the patient believing their loved one is an impostor, the loved one has actually *become* an impostor, and the one person who should notice can't, because to him the replacement is an improvement. He gets his first wife back without the mess of her actual personhood.

## Premise

Nora Tierney, two years into her second marriage, has been slowly colonized by the domestic space her husband's first wife, Leigh, designed before she left him. Every paint color, garden bed, and kitchen rhythm belongs to Leigh — and Nora can't stop absorbing them. What she tells herself is empathy (honoring the woman who came before) is actually acquisition (perfecting the role Leigh abandoned). When Nora discovers an old building permit revealing that her husband, Joel, deliberately walled off Leigh's studio between marriages — not grief but erasure — she must confront the possibility that Joel didn't lose his first wife. He consumed her. And now Nora is being consumed by the same machinery, except she's been helping.

**The question the story asks**: When you can no longer distinguish between the person you chose to become and the person a domestic space is producing, is escape even possible — or have you already been replaced?

---

## Protagonist

- **Name**: Nora Tierney, 36
- **Situation**: Former landscape architect who left her practice when she married Joel Tierney and moved to his house in Millhaven, a Connecticut suburb. She took Joel's name. She doesn't work anymore — she "consults" from home, which means she answers two emails a week and spends the rest of her time maintaining a house she didn't design. She has no children. Leigh had no children either.
- **WANT** (conscious goal): To understand why Leigh left — to find the thread that will explain the predecessor and thereby explain Nora's own growing unease.
- **NEED** (unconscious truth she's avoiding): To admit that she doesn't want to understand Leigh — she wants to *outperform* Leigh. Her obsession isn't empathy. It's competition with an absent woman, and the prize is a man who can't tell the difference between them.
- **FLAW/BLIND SPOT**: Nora believes her own narrative of victimhood — she's the haunted second wife, the woman overshadowed. This belief is genuine AND strategic. It protects her from seeing that she has agency in her own erasure. She chose to cook from Leigh's recipe cards. She chose to maintain Leigh's garden. She tells herself the house compelled her, but the house is just a house. Nora is the one doing the haunting.

## Structure (5 Beats)

### Beat 1: The Inventory (Opening)
Nora takes a walk through the house, narrating each room with what she's learned about Leigh's choices. The paint colors (Leigh chose "Lamb's Ear" for the bedroom, "Bone" for the hall). The garden layout. The placement of outlets and the angle of the kitchen island. Her narration is tender, almost reverent — the kind of tenderness Tana French identified in the meeting as "actually inventory." We learn that Nora has been here two years. We learn that Joel doesn't notice any of this. We learn, through an aside so casual it almost slides past, that Nora has started wearing her hair the way Leigh wore hers — pulled back, low, a particular twist that she found in a photograph and that "just made sense for the humidity here."

**Hook**: The last line of the opening section: Nora opens a kitchen drawer to find a serving spoon she doesn't recognize — one she didn't buy, one that wasn't here last week. Leigh's pattern is still generating new objects, or someone is still feeding it.

### Beat 2: The Permit (Inciting Disruption)
Nora, looking for the spoon's origin, goes through a filing cabinet in the basement. She finds old building permits from the renovation Joel did between marriages. Among routine plumbing updates: a permit to reposition a load-bearing wall on the second floor. The first wife's studio — Nora had been told it was "always a closet" — was a room. A real room with a window. Joel cut the window wall in half, drywalled it, and turned a creative workspace into storage for winter coats. This is the spite house moment: architecture as aggression. And Joel never mentioned it. When she asks him that evening, casually, about the closet, he says, "It was always a closet." He says it the way you say something you've decided is true.

This disruption breaks two things: Nora's image of Joel (passive, oblivious) and her image of Leigh (abandoned, pitiful). If Joel erased Leigh's room, then Leigh didn't just leave — she was erased before she left. And Nora has been living inside the evidence of that erasure, thinking it was just a closet.

### Beat 3: The Escalating Middle — Accumulation and Doubling
Nora begins investigating Leigh more aggressively, though she tells herself (and the reader) that she's investigating Joel. She finds Leigh's old social media (archived, frozen in time). She finds a box of Leigh's things in the basement — not discarded, carefully packed. She finds a recipe card in Leigh's handwriting for a meal Nora has been cooking for months, one she thought she'd invented. The Capgras doubling intensifies: Joel becomes more affectionate as Nora becomes more like Leigh. He starts calling her "Nor" — a nickname she never asked for and that she later finds (in an old text chain on a tablet left in a drawer) is what he called Leigh. Not a nickname for Nora. A nickname for the pattern.

Meanwhile, Nora's landscape architect's eye starts reading the house structurally. She notices that the garden beds are laid out in a pattern that only makes sense if the house is approached from the east — from the direction of the studio window that no longer exists. The whole garden was designed to be seen from a room Joel bricked up. Leigh's garden is still facing her ghost.

A neighbor, Diana, comes for coffee. Diana knew Leigh. In passing, Diana mentions that Leigh was "so creative — she had that studio upstairs, she was always up there." Nora doesn't correct her. Doesn't say it's a closet now. Sits with the knowledge like a stone in her throat.

### Beat 4: The Crisis — The Closet
Nora decides to open the closet. Not metaphorically — literally. She takes a pry bar to the drywall of what was Leigh's studio. Behind the wall: the window is still there, just covered. Light pours in from the east. The room is still shaped like a room underneath the renovation. On the inside of the drywall, in pencil, in Joel's handwriting: measurements. Not contractor's measurements — obsessive, repeated, rewritten. He measured this wall a dozen times before he closed it. Not grief. Not renovation. Ritual.

And then — the real crisis — Nora realizes she doesn't want to restore the room. Her first impulse, standing in the broken wall with plaster dust on her hands, is not outrage on Leigh's behalf. It's a calculation: if she restores the studio, she could use it. She could work here. She could be the wife who has the studio, the wife who recovered what Joel destroyed. She would be *better than Leigh* — Leigh lost the room; Nora would win it back.

She catches herself in this thought and understands, finally, what she's been doing for two years. Not grieving Leigh. Not being haunted by Leigh. Competing with Leigh for the approval of a man who can't tell them apart. The tenderness was inventory. The obsession was an apprenticeship. She has been perfecting a dead marriage.

### Beat 5: The Ending — The Held Breath
Joel comes home. Sees the broken wall. His face does something Nora has never seen — not anger, not surprise, but a kind of recognition, as if this was always going to happen, as if every wife who lives in this house eventually finds the room.

He says: "I was going to tell you about that."

Nora says: "When?"

He doesn't answer. They stand in the hallway, plaster dust between them, and the light from the uncovered window falls across the floor in a rectangle that looks exactly like the garden beds below — the same proportions, the same angle. The house is still aligned. The ghost's geometry persists.

Nora picks up her phone. We don't see who she calls. We don't know if she's calling a lawyer, a friend, a contractor to finish the demolition, or Leigh herself. The last line is action without interpretation. She dials, and the story ends on the sound of a phone ringing in another room — whether that room is in this house or somewhere else, the story doesn't say.

**What's left open**: Whether Nora stays or goes. Whether her recognition of her own complicity changes anything or simply becomes a new, more sophisticated performance. Whether the house will produce the same wife again, from different material.

## Key Scenes

### Scene 1: The Spoon Discovery (Beat 1)
Nora finds an unfamiliar serving spoon in a kitchen drawer. She catalogs every utensil she owns (landscape architect's precision, Flynn's inventory-as-character). The spoon is bone-handled, old, with a monogram she can't quite read. It wasn't here a week ago. The scene does two things: establishes Nora's hypervigilance about the house's contents AND introduces the uncanny — objects appearing, as if the first wife's domestic life is still generating artifacts. (The spoon turns out to have been in a box in the basement; Joel brought it up without telling her. But by the time Nora learns this, the uncanny has already infected the reader.)

### Scene 2: The Neighbor's Visit (Beat 3)
Diana comes for coffee, mentions the studio. This scene is all subtext — Nora performing ease while absorbing devastating information. Flynn's sharp social observation meets French's atmospheric tension. Diana talks about Leigh the way you talk about someone who moved away, not someone who was erased. The gap between Diana's casual Leigh and Nora's obsessive Leigh is the scene's engine. Diana leaves. Nora washes the coffee cups. The domesticity is the horror.

### Scene 3: The Nickname (Beat 3)
Joel calls Nora "Nor" during dinner. She freezes. He doesn't notice the freeze — he's reading something on his phone. Later that night, Nora finds the old tablet and the text chain. "Nor, don't forget the dry cleaning." "Nor, I'll be late." The nickname wasn't invented for her. The Capgras moment: Joel isn't seeing Nora. He's seeing the pattern wearing Nora's face. This is the scene where the reader starts to doubt Nora's victimhood — because Nora isn't devastated. She's calculating. She files the information and begins to use the nickname herself, testing it, seeing how it fits. This is the scene where "genuine suffering and performed suffering" become indistinguishable.

### Scene 4: Breaking the Wall (Beat 4)
The physical climax. Nora with a pry bar, demolishing drywall. The scene must be visceral — plaster dust, the sound of cracking, the effort in her shoulders. When the light comes through the uncovered window, it should feel like a crime scene photograph — evidence, not revelation. She finds Joel's pencil measurements. She stands in the ghost of a room. And then the turn: her first thought is not empathy but strategy. This is the scene where the reader's sympathy curdles.

### Scene 5: Joel's Return (Beat 5)
Minimal dialogue. Joel sees the wall. His expression. "I was going to tell you about that." The lie is so small and so enormous that Nora doesn't challenge it. The geometry of light on the floor. Nora's phone call. End.

## Emotional Trajectory

**Opening**: The reader feels intimate unease — something is wrong in this house, but Nora's narration is so competent, so wry, so in control that we trust her. We lean into her perspective the way we'd lean into a friend's account of a bad marriage: sympathetic, a little voyeuristic.

**Middle**: The unease deepens into dread as we realize the house is a palimpsest — Leigh's life is underneath everything, and Nora isn't just discovering it, she's absorbing it. The reader starts feeling complicit: we've been rooting for Nora to understand Leigh, and now we're watching Nora *become* Leigh, and we can't tell when the switch happened.

**Crisis**: The reader feels the ground shift. When Nora breaks the wall and finds the room, we expect catharsis — the haunted wife uncovers the husband's crime, the mystery resolves. Instead, Nora's first thought is self-interested, and we realize we've been sympathizing with someone whose grief was a competitive strategy. The feeling is not betrayal (we weren't tricked) but recognition (the evidence was always there, in the way she described Leigh's belongings with too much tenderness, too much detail).

**Ending**: The reader feels held in suspension — not satisfaction, not frustration, but the particular discomfort of watching someone stand at a threshold. We don't know what Nora will do. We're not sure we know who Nora is. The phone rings in another room, and the reader is left sitting with the question of whether knowing you're performing is enough to stop the performance.

## Formula Integration

### Gillian Flynn (AuthorA) — Concentrated in: Nora's Voice Throughout + The Nickname Scene
Flynn's influence is the narrator's voice itself: sardonic, self-lacerating, performing intelligence as armor. Nora's observations about Joel should have Flynn's venomous precision — "He ate the way all Joels eat, which is to say with the confidence of someone who has never once wondered whether he belongs at the table." The nickname scene is pure Flynn territory: the moment Nora finds "Nor" in the old texts and, instead of being wounded, *adopts the name*, is the Gone Girl move — the performed identity becoming the real one, weaponized from the inside.

### Tana French (AuthorB) — Concentrated in: The House Descriptions + The Garden Discovery
French's influence is atmospheric: the accumulation of domestic detail that becomes dread. The opening inventory of the house should feel like French's Dublin estates — specific, sensory, layered with time. The garden scene (Nora realizing the beds face a window that no longer exists) is French's signature move: landscape as buried memory, the physical world holding a truth that the characters have agreed to forget. Long, controlled sentences that accumulate detail until the detail becomes unbearable.

### *Rebecca* (WorkX) — Concentrated in: The Entire Domestic Structure + The Closet/Studio
The story's skeletal structure is *Rebecca*: a second wife in a predecessor's space, a house that remembers its first mistress, a husband whose opacity may be passivity or may be something worse. The closet/studio is this story's Mrs. Danvers moment — the locked room that contains the truth about the marriage that came before. But inverted: in du Maurier, the housekeeper curates Rebecca's memory. Here, the husband destroyed it, and the house resists the destruction. The building permit is this story's version of Rebecca's monogrammed stationery — evidence of a life that refuses to be overwritten.

### *Gone Girl* (WorkY) — Concentrated in: The Crisis Moment (Beat 4) + The Ending
Gone Girl's DNA is in the weaponization of victimhood and the performance of identity. The crucial moment — Nora standing in the broken wall and thinking not "poor Leigh" but "I could use this room" — is the Amy Dunne turn: the victim revealing herself as strategist. But unlike Gone Girl, there's no clean reveal. Nora doesn't pivot from victim to villain; she exists in both states simultaneously. The ending's ambiguity (who is she calling? what will she do?) echoes Gone Girl's refusal to let the reader off the hook — the marriage continues, the performance continues, and we're all still watching.

## Title

**Nor**

Short. Ambiguous. It's the nickname — the one Joel used for Leigh and then, without pause or recognition, transferred to Nora. It's also a conjunction of negation: *nor* this, *nor* that. Neither the first wife nor the second. Neither victim nor architect. The title sits in the space between two identities, which is where Nora lives. It does not start with "The." It's a single syllable that sounds like a name, a negation, and a direction (north — *nor'* — the cold wind) all at once.
