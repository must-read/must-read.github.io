## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Edgar Allan Poe — "The Fall of the House of Usher" (Opening)

**Source**: Author A — Edgar Allan Poe
**Origin**: "The Fall of the House of Usher" (1839), opening paragraphs
**Why representative**: Demonstrates Poe's accumulative dread through rhythmic, clause-heavy sentences; the house as externalization of psychological decay; the narrator's obsessive cataloguing of sensory detail.

> During the whole of a dull, dark, and soundless day in the autumn of the year, when the clouds hung oppressively low in the heavens, I had been passing alone, on horseback, through a singularly dreary tract of country; and at length found myself, as the shades of the evening drew on, within view of the melancholy House of Usher. I know not how it was — but, with the first glimpse of the building, a sense of insufferable gloom pervaded my spirit. I say insufferable; for the feeling was unrelieved by any of that half-pleasurable, because poetic, sentiment, with which the mind usually receives even the sternest natural images of the desolate or terrible.

> I looked upon the scene before me — upon the mere house, and the simple landscape features of the domain — upon the bleak walls — upon the vacant eye-like windows — upon a few rank sedges — and upon a few white trunks of decayed trees — with an utter depression of soul which I can compare to no earthly sensation more properly than to the after-dream of the reveller upon opium — the bitter lapse into everyday life — the hideous dropping off of the veil.

Key Poe techniques to replicate:
- Long sentences built from accumulating clauses, each adding weight
- Dashes used as rhythmic interruptions — the prose breathes in gasps
- The narrator's self-conscious attempts to explain their own dread, failing, trying again
- Sensory overload catalogued methodically (walls, windows, sedges, trees)
- The house as face ("vacant eye-like windows")
- Emotional vocabulary that borders on physiological ("insufferable," "depression of soul")

### Sample 2: Silvia Moreno-Garcia — *Mexican Gothic* (Style Analysis)

**Source**: Author B — Silvia Moreno-Garcia
**Origin**: *Mexican Gothic* (2020), stylistic analysis from multiple critical sources
**Why representative**: Shows Moreno-Garcia's controlled gothic atmosphere built through precise material detail rather than overwrought language; the house as political entity.

Moreno-Garcia's prose is characterized by what critics call "archly intelligent tone and insightful writing" — she weaponizes domestic detail to create dread. Unlike Poe's fevered accumulation, her sentences are measured, observational, and grounded in specific material culture. A representative line: "This house, with all its fabrics and rugs and tassels, could smother a whole army." The horror is in the concrete particular — fabrics, rugs, tassels — made sinister through the verb "smother."

Key Moreno-Garcia techniques to replicate:
- Gothic atmosphere built from specific material objects (textiles, food, architecture) rather than abstract dread
- The protagonist's sharp, skeptical intelligence as counterpoint to the house's pull
- Colonial architecture as living system — the hacienda that embodies its own history of exploitation
- Fungal imagery (in *Mexican Gothic*, the house is literally connected to a fungal organism)
- Controlled pacing — dread through accumulation of wrong details noticed by a rational observer
- The house's influence registered through the body before the mind understands

### Sample 3: *House of Leaves* by Mark Z. Danielewski (Structure)

**Source**: Work X — *House of Leaves* (2000)
**Origin**: Structural analysis and key passages
**Why representative**: The novel's core innovation — spatial impossibility mediated through nested, unreliable documents — is the structural DNA to draw from.

The novel operates through three nested narrative layers: (1) Will Navidson's documentary about a house on Ash Tree Lane whose interior measurements exceed its exterior dimensions; (2) Zampano's academic manuscript analyzing this documentary; (3) Johnny Truant's footnotes as he transcribes and slowly unravels. Each layer contradicts and undermines the others. The house itself manifests as: a hallway that appears from nowhere and extends into a vast labyrinth of rooms, stairs, and ever-shifting dark spaces. Pages with minimal text, varying fonts, and unconventional layouts mirror the house's architecture. The horror is not the spatial impossibility itself but the impossibility of documenting it — every account is filtered, contradicted, annotated. The house exists most powerfully in the gaps between narratives.

Key structural elements to adapt:
- The idea that spatial impossibility is most horrifying when mediated through unreliable accounts (not shown directly)
- The house as something that resists measurement — the more precisely you try to define it, the more it escapes
- Documents within documents — the story told through layers of interpretation
- The house as indifferent void vs. the house as purposeful predator (Danielewski leans void; this story should lean predator-becoming-void)

### Sample 4: *White is for Witching* by Helen Oyeyemi (Themes)

**Source**: Work Y — *White is for Witching* (2009)
**Origin**: Thematic and structural analysis
**Why representative**: The novel's treatment of the house as xenophobic consuming entity, and pica as metaphor for colonial appetite, is the thematic DNA for this story.

The Silver family home in Dover — England's gateway port, the interface between Britain and the continent — is a sentient, xenophobic entity. Miranda Silver suffers from pica, compulsively eating chalk and plastic ("England's physical stuff"), a condition the novel positions as both neurological disorder and symptom of the house's appetite working through her body. The house has a voice, a personality, and preferences: it welcomes some visitors and rejects others based on race and origin. The "goodlady" is a spectral figure connected to the house's history. Ore, Miranda's lover (Nigerian-descended, adopted by white parents), represents what the house considers foreign and wants to consume or expel.

Multiple narrators — Miranda, Eliot (her twin), Ore, and the house itself — create deliberate ambiguity. The house narrates in first person plural ("we"), suggesting it contains multitudes — the accumulated will of generations.

Key thematic elements to adapt:
- Pica as the body enacting historical appetite — the house teaches the body what to eat
- The house's xenophobia as systemic rather than personal — it has outlived its builders
- The consuming entity that makes digestion feel like belonging
- The house's voice as narrative perspective — it speaks, it has preferences, it acts

### Selected Concepts (2):

**1. Efflorescence** — The white salt crystals that form on masonry walls when moisture migrates through porous stone and evaporates at the surface, leaving mineral deposits behind. This becomes a central physical detail in the story: the walls of the house literally weep white mineral residue, which the protagonist begins eating (connecting pica to the house's material body). The efflorescence is not decoration — it's the house's substance pushing outward, and the protagonist consuming it is the house feeding her itself, making her part of its body. The detail is specific enough to feel researched ("subflorescence" when it happens below the surface, causing stone to crack from within), and it bridges the Poe element (the house as externalized pathology) with the Oyeyemi element (pica as architectural appetite). The protagonist would know the word — she's renovating the house, she's looked up why the walls bloom white.

**Integration**: The protagonist, Irene, notices efflorescence on the walls during renovation. She researches it, learns the term, understands the capillary action drawing dissolved salts to the surface. Then she begins tasting it — first absently, then deliberately. The white powder tastes of mineral and age. She tells herself it's curiosity. Then habit. The house is feeding her itself, grain by grain.

**2. Cordyceps / Ophiocordyceps unilateralis** — The parasitic fungus that infects ants, secretes neuromodulatory chemicals near (but not inside) their brains, and compels them to climb vegetation, lock their mandibles in a "death grip," and die in a position optimal for the fungus to fruit from their bodies. The fungus controls behavior without entering the brain — it works through the muscles, through the body's own systems turned against it.

**Integration**: This becomes the metaphorical architecture of the house's control. The house doesn't possess minds — it works through the body. Through appetite (pica/efflorescence), through habit (the way Irene stops wanting to leave not because she can't but because the desire has been eaten), through muscle memory (she finds herself repairing walls she'd planned to tear down). One character — perhaps Irene's mother-in-law or a neighbor — actually names the fungus in conversation about the garden, mentioning how cordyceps infects insects and makes them serve the fungus's reproductive needs. Irene hears this and the knowledge sits in her like a seed, but by then the house has already gotten into her body and the comparison arrives too late to be useful.

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

A young Mexican-American architect takes on the renovation of a colonial-era house in Guanajuato that her husband's family has owned for three centuries, planning to convert it into a boutique hotel. The house does not want to be opened. It wants to be fed. As Irene strips plaster and replaces pipes, she discovers the house's interior measurements are subtly, impossibly wrong — rooms she's measured at 4.2 meters come back at 4.5 the next day, and the discrepancies always grow inward, as if the house is thickening its own walls. She begins eating the white mineral deposits that bloom on the stone, and the more she eats, the less she wants to leave, and the less she can remember why she came.

### 2. Protagonist

- **Name**: Irene Vidal-Aldama, 31
- **Situation**: Architect from Tucson, married two years to Sebastián Aldama, whose family owns a crumbling 18th-century casa solariega in Guanajuato's old quarter. Irene has never been to the house before. She volunteered for the renovation project because she specializes in adaptive reuse of historic buildings — converting them for modern habitation while preserving their character. She speaks fluent Spanish but grew up on the American side. She has not spoken to her own mother in four years, since her mother sold the family home in Nogales without consulting her.
- **What she WANTS**: To prove she can transform this house — to demonstrate her professional skill, to earn the Aldama family's approval, to create something beautiful from something decayed.
- **What she NEEDS**: To recognize that "preservation" can be a form of submission — that some structures demand you serve them, and the pride she takes in respecting old buildings has blinded her to the possibility that an old building can be predatory.
- **Flaw/blind spot**: Irene believes architecture is neutral — that buildings are containers, not agents. She sees the house as a professional challenge, not as something with appetite. Her reverence for historical structures makes her unable to recognize when one is consuming her. She keeps rationalizing the house's wrongness as "settling" or "measurement error" because admitting the house is impossible would mean admitting her expertise is inadequate.

### 3. Structure

**Opening image/scene**: Irene is alone in the house at dusk, running a laser measure along the interior wall of the main corridor. The measurement reads 14.7 meters. She recorded it as 14.2 yesterday. She re-measures. 14.7. She writes it down, crosses out yesterday's number, and tells herself the laser was miscalibrated. Then she notices the white bloom on the base of the wall — efflorescence, she knows the word — and without thinking, she runs her finger along it and touches it to her tongue. It tastes of salt and chalk and something older. She doesn't know why she did that. She does it again.

**Inciting disruption**: Irene discovers a room. Not a hidden room — a room that wasn't there. Behind a wall she had opened to inspect the original stonework, there is a space — small, closet-sized, with tezontle walls and a packed-earth floor — that does not appear on any of the blueprints, the colonial-era plans, or the family's own sketches. When she measures it and adds its dimensions to the floor plan, the total interior area exceeds the exterior footprint by 1.3 square meters. She tells Sebastián. He says the old plans must be wrong. She knows they're not.

**Escalating middle**: The discrepancies multiply. Rooms grow — not dramatically, not House-of-Leaves hallways extending into infinity, but by centimeters, by inches, always inward, as if the walls are accreting mass. Irene begins finding efflorescence everywhere — on walls she's already cleaned, on surfaces she's already sealed. She eats it. First from the walls, then she finds herself scraping it into a small jar she keeps in her tool bag. The taste changes — becomes richer, earthier, like the clay pottery Sebastián's grandmother kept in the kitchen. She stops wanting to go into town. She stops calling her contacts in Tucson. She starts repairing walls she'd planned to demolish, as if her hands have their own agenda. Sebastián visits on weekends and notices she's lost weight but her eyes look different — brighter, he says, which he means as a compliment. She discovers, in the closet-room, that the packed-earth floor is damp — moisture rising from below, through the old lakebed or aquifer, capillary action bringing water and minerals upward through three centuries of stone. The walls are sweating. The house is digesting the ground beneath it and pushing the residue to the surface. She understands this, technically. She eats it anyway.

**Crisis point**: Sebastián's aunt, Doña Rosalba, visits. She is 74, grew up in the house, left at 18 and never returned. She tells Irene, casually, over a dinner Irene has barely touched, about the workers who built the house in the 1730s — indigenous laborers paid in scrip redeemable only at the hacienda's own store, the tienda de raya. Some of them, Rosalba says, never left. Not because they were imprisoned but because the debt made leaving irrational. The house provided. The house fed them. Why would they go? Rosalba looks at Irene's hands — the cracked fingertips, the white dust under her nails — and says nothing about what she sees, but she tells a story about her own mother, who also ate the walls. "She said it helped her think," Rosalba says. "She said the house tasted like belonging." Then Rosalba leaves, and she does not come back. That night, Irene measures the hidden room again. It has grown. It is now the size of a bedroom. The packed-earth floor is soft, yielding, almost warm. She lies down on it. It feels like being held.

**Ending**: Irene sends Sebastián a message: the renovation is going well, she needs two more months, the house is beautiful, he should see it. She writes her measurements in a notebook she keeps by the bed. The numbers are wrong and she knows they're wrong and she writes them anyway, each entry a small act of collaboration with the house's geometry. The last scene: she is in the hidden room, which is now the largest room in the house, though from outside the building looks no different. She is eating efflorescence from the wall with the focused, unselfconscious attention of someone performing a devotion. The white powder on her lips. The taste of mineral and time. She has a thought she does not finish: that she should — and the thought ends there, the verb without its object, the desire eaten before it could complete itself. She picks up her notebook and writes: *All measurements confirmed. Structure sound.*

### 4. Key Scenes (5)

1. **The first taste** (opening): Irene licks efflorescence from the corridor wall — the gesture is unconscious, almost reflexive, and she rationalizes it as professional curiosity. Establishes pica as the entry point for the house's colonization AND introduces the measurement anomaly. Does double work: character (Irene's rationalist denial) and plot (the house is already wrong).

2. **The hidden room**: Irene breaks through a wall and finds a space that shouldn't exist. She measures obsessively, checks the exterior, re-measures. The math doesn't work. Her reaction reveals her flaw — she adjusts the numbers rather than confronting the impossibility. She tells Sebastián; his dismissal seals her isolation.

3. **The jar**: A scene where Irene realizes she has been scraping efflorescence into a jar and carrying it with her. She's been eating it between meals, the way someone might eat sunflower seeds or trail mix. The moment of realization should be clinical, almost detached — she sees herself and describes what she sees with the precision of an architect documenting a structural fault, but she doesn't stop. She can't locate the desire to stop. This is the Cordyceps moment — the behavior continues because the part of her that would resist has been quietly consumed.

4. **Doña Rosalba's visit**: The aunt who escaped tells the history. The tienda de raya. The workers who stayed. Her own mother's pica. This scene does the heaviest lifting for the colonial-violence-as-haunting theme. Rosalba is not frightened — she is matter-of-fact, which is worse. She has seen this before. The house does this. It has always done this. The horror is in the systematics: not a singular haunting but a repeating pattern, a colonial machine that has outlived its operators.

5. **The final measurement** (ending): Irene in the hidden room, which has grown impossibly large. She writes her notebook entry. The prose itself should show the house's infection — Irene's language has become the house's language, technical and precise and emptied of alarm. The reader should feel the house in the sentences: clauses that thicken, descriptions that accrete, a narrator who has been edited by what she describes.

### 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning**: Intellectual excitement tinged with mild unease. The reader shares Irene's professional interest in the house — the pleasure of restoration, the puzzle of old architecture. There's a current of loneliness (she's far from home, Sebastián is absent) but it reads as independence, even adventure.

**Middle**: The excitement curdles into something more adhesive. The reader should feel the pull of the house — the descriptions of efflorescence, of mineral taste, of warm earth should be almost sensual. The horror is not revulsion but seduction. The reader, like Irene, should find themselves wanting to stay, wanting to know more, leaning into the descriptions of taste and texture. Then a slow creep of wrongness — not "this is scary" but "why am I comfortable with this?"

**End**: The reader should feel complicit. Irene's final notebook entry — "All measurements confirmed. Structure sound." — should land like a door closing. Not with a slam, but with a click. The reader should realize they have been inside the house for the entire story and that the story's prose has been doing to them what the house does to Irene: feeding them beauty, feeding them specificity, feeding them the pleasure of precise description until they forgot to be afraid. The feeling is: I didn't notice when I stopped wanting to leave.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Edgar Allan Poe (Author A)** — Most visible in: the OPENING SCENE and THROUGHOUT THE PROSE VOICE. The story's sentence-level rhythm should echo Poe's accumulative, clause-heavy style — dashes, semicolons, subordinate clauses that pile weight onto weight. Irene's interior monologue should mirror the Usher narrator's compulsive cataloguing of sensory detail, his attempts to rationalize dread through precision. The confined-space psychology: Irene alone in rooms, measuring, the house as the inside of her own increasingly colonized mind. The obsessive repetition of measurement — like the tell-tale heart's beating, the numbers keep coming back wrong and she keeps recording them.

**Silvia Moreno-Garcia (Author B)** — Most visible in: DOÑA ROSALBA'S SCENE and the MATERIAL SPECIFICITY throughout. The house must be grounded in specific Mexican colonial architecture — tezontle stone, the tienda de raya system, the hacienda's self-contained economy. The colonial violence is not metaphorical; it is structural, historical, named. Rosalba's monologue about the workers who never left is pure Moreno-Garcia: the gothic as political history, the haunting as systemic rather than supernatural. The house's appetite is not demonic — it is institutional. Also: the fungal undertone. The efflorescence is mineral, but its behavior — spreading, feeding, colonizing — echoes the mycological horror of *Mexican Gothic*.

**House of Leaves (Work X)** — Most visible in: the MEASUREMENT ANOMALIES and the HIDDEN ROOM. The spatial impossibility is adapted from Danielewski but scaled down, made intimate — not a vast labyrinth but a house that grows by centimeters, thickening its walls, accreting mass inward. The nested-document element appears in Irene's notebook: her measurements become an unreliable record, a document that collaborates with the house's geometry. The reader experiences the spatial impossibility only through Irene's records, never directly — like *House of Leaves*, the horror is mediated, filtered through documentation that may already be compromised.

**White is for Witching (Work Y)** — Most visible in: the PICA and the HOUSE'S SELECTIVITY. Eating the walls is drawn directly from Oyeyemi's treatment of pica as the body enacting the house's appetite. The efflorescence-eating is not random compulsion — it's the house feeding Irene itself, making her part of its body. The xenophobia element: the house has always consumed outsiders — the indigenous workers, the women who married into the Aldama family, now Irene, a Mexican-American who is both of this place and not of it. The house's preference for certain inhabitants over others. The "belonging as digestion" motif from the author meeting is the story's core.

### 7. Title

**Appetite of the Walls**

This title avoids starting with "The" (reducing the percentage toward 30%). It foregrounds the story's central image — walls that have appetite, that feed and are fed upon — and it carries a double meaning: the walls' hunger for the people inside them, and the appetite that the walls create in those people. It echoes the author meeting's key phrase ("architectural appetite") without quoting it directly.
