# Story Plan: Philosophical Fiction / Allegorical

## Assignment

- **Genre**: Philosophical Fiction
- **Subgenre**: Allegorical
- **Author A**: Jose Saramago (long flowing sentences, sardonic omniscient voice, characters stripped of names, oral quality, the absurd treated with matter-of-fact gravity)
- **Author B**: Albert Camus (spare declarative sentences, philosophical weight through understatement, detached clarity, emotional flatness that reveals, Mediterranean physicality — sun, sea, stone)
- **Work X**: The Stranger (ordinary existence disrupted by irrational act; institutional systems imposing narrative meaning on what resists it; refusal to perform expected emotions as radical honesty; the trial as mechanism to judge the person's failure to conform)
- **Work Y**: Blindness (single impossible change in reality that strips away social convention; allegorical method with ruthless realism; institutional collapse through incremental failure of systems designed for a world that no longer exists)
- **Risk Card**: STORY-AS-DOCUMENT (the story takes the form of a non-literary document)
- **Target Word Count**: 3,900 words (range: 3,600-4,200)

## Research Synthesis

### Saramago's Prose Style
Long, flowing sentences where commas replace periods, quotation marks, semicolons, and colons. Dialogue embedded within narration without conventional punctuation marks. Characters referred to by description rather than name ("the doctor's wife," "the first blind man"). An omniscient narrator who treats the catastrophic with bureaucratic equanimity. The sentence accumulates subordinate clauses like a snowball rolling downhill. Key quote from Blindness: "Words are like that, they deceive, they pile up, it seems they do not know where to go, and, suddenly, because of two or three or four that suddenly come out, simple in themselves, a personal pronoun, an adverb, an adjective, we have the excitement of seeing them coming irresistibly to the surface through the skin and the eyes and upsetting the composure of our feelings."

### Camus's Prose Style
Short, declarative sentences. Subject-verb-object. The world rendered with detached precision. "Maman died today. Or yesterday maybe, I don't know." Physical sensations carry philosophical weight — "cymbals of sunlight crashing on my forehead." Emotional flatness as a lens. Meursault does not lie about what he feels; he simply fails to perform the emotions society expects. The trial condemns him not for killing a man but for not crying at his mother's funeral. The Mediterranean physicality: sun, heat, sand, sea, stone.

### The Stranger's Architecture
Ordinary life (Part I) → irrational act → institutional prosecution (Part II). But the prosecution is really about the person, not the act. Society needs a narrative — the prosecutor constructs one from Meursault's failure to mourn, his swimming trip the day after the funeral, his going to a comedy film. The system cannot tolerate a man who refuses to lie about what he feels.

### Blindness's Architecture
A single impossible premise (white blindness spreading through a city) played out with ruthless logical realism. The government's first response is paperwork — quarantine orders, intake forms. The forms are the most terrifying part. Bureaucracy is the last religion: long after people stop believing in God, in justice, in progress, they continue to believe in the correct form, the properly filed document, the stamp. Institutional collapse happens not through revolution but through incremental failure of systems designed for a world that no longer exists.

### Two selected for fusion:

**1. Quorum** — The minimum number of members required for a deliberative body to conduct valid business. Without a quorum, proceedings are suspended. No business may be transacted. The body exists but cannot act. This concept fuses perfectly with both Saramago's committee-minutes idea from the author meeting and with the story's allegorical structure: a committee that keeps meeting after the conditions for valid proceedings have been lost. The minutes record a body that technically lacks the authority to decide anything, but continues deciding. This is the document — minutes of meetings that may not be legitimate.

**2. Quorum sensing** — In microbiology, quorum sensing is the mechanism by which individual bacteria collectively change behavior when their population density exceeds a threshold. Each bacterium secretes signaling molecules; at low density these diffuse away to nothing, but at high density the local concentration triggers a synchronized group response. The inverse is also true: when density drops below the threshold, the collective behavior ceases, even though individual organisms remain. This provides the allegorical substrate: a city (or institution, or civilization) as an organism whose collective behavior depends on a density threshold of participation, attention, belief. When enough people stop paying attention — not through malice but through a gradual, bacteria-like individual withdrawal — the collective behavior that sustains the social order simply stops triggering. The city doesn't collapse dramatically. The quorum is simply no longer present. The signals diffuse away to nothing.

### Integration plan:
The parliamentary concept of quorum and the biological concept of quorum sensing merge into the story's central allegory. A Municipal Committee on Civic Continuity keeps meeting even as the city around it loses its quorum — not of members, but of participants in the social contract. Attendance at the committee itself dwindles toward the threshold. The minutes record, with bureaucratic precision, the slow failure of the collective signal.

## Premise

An unnamed city begins experiencing what officials initially call "participatory decline" — residents are not leaving, not dying, not protesting, but simply ceasing to participate. They stop answering doors. They stop attending. They stop filing. They stop appearing at intersections when the lights change. No one can identify a cause because there is no cause — or rather, the cause is the same as the effect: a density threshold has been crossed below which collective social behavior no longer triggers. The Municipal Committee on Civic Continuity is convened to address the situation. The story IS the minutes of this committee's meetings — taken faithfully, formatted correctly, timestamped, with motions proposed and seconded and carried — as the committee itself gradually loses members and the situation it was convened to address becomes the situation inside the room.

The document form is minutes of an official committee. The productive friction: the minutes are the voice of institutional order, dry and procedural, recording the dissolution of the very order they represent. The form holds even as the content becomes unmaintainable.

## Protagonist

**The Recording Secretary** (referred to in the minutes only through the institutional voice — "It was noted that," "The Secretary recorded," and eventually through cracks in the professional mask — editorial asides, timestamps that begin to drift, notations that become almost confessional).

**Specific wound**: The Recording Secretary's spouse stopped participating three months before the committee was convened. Not left — stopped. Stopped speaking at meals, stopped answering when spoken to, stopped going out, stopped coming home on time, then stopped coming home. The Secretary does not mention this in the minutes. The Secretary's refusal to editorialize about the crisis — to maintain professional neutrality in the face of personal devastation — is the Meursault connection. The committee eventually notices and cannot tolerate this neutrality.

**Action**: The Secretary does not just observe — they actively shape the record. What they include, exclude, mark as "(inaudible)" or "(laughter)" or "(pause)" constitutes editorial action. As the crisis deepens, the Secretary makes increasingly consequential choices about what the minutes say happened. In the climactic meeting, the Secretary records something the committee explicitly voted to strike from the record.

## Structure (5 Beats)

### Beat 1: Convening (Minutes of Meeting No. 1)
~700 words

The committee is formally convened. Seven members present. Proper headers, timestamps, attendance roll. The situation is described in anodyne bureaucratic language: "a statistically significant decline in civic participation metrics across multiple indices." Motions to form subcommittees. The tone is calm, procedural, almost tedious. Through the bureaucratic language, the reader infers something strange: people are not just disengaged — they are somehow withdrawing from the act of being present. One member notes that "the phenomenon appears to lack intentionality," which another member disputes. A motion is carried to commission a study. The Secretary records it all with neutral precision. A single notation — "(The sound of traffic outside the window was noted to be unusually sparse for the time of day)" — is the first crack.

### Beat 2: Escalation (Minutes of Meetings No. 3, 5, and 7 — excerpted)
~1,100 words

Jump-cut through several meetings. The format establishes a rhythm, then begins breaking it. Meeting No. 3: six members present. The city's transit ridership has dropped 40%. Lampposts are being left on because no one files complaints about them being on during the day. A motion to declare a civic emergency is tabled. Meeting No. 5: five members present. The committee receives a report that three of the city's hospitals have functionally ceased operations — not closed, because no one filed the paperwork to close them, but emptied of both patients and staff. The member who chaired the health subcommittee has not attended since Meeting No. 4. No resignation letter was received. Meeting No. 7: four members present. The Secretary notes: "The committee discussed at length the question of whether the committee itself maintained quorum." A legal opinion is cited. The threshold is three. A member suggests that quorum should be redefined. Another member says you cannot redefine quorum when the act of redefining requires quorum. The circular logic is recorded without comment.

### Beat 3: The Trial of the Secretary (Minutes of Meeting No. 9)
~1,000 words

Three members present (the legal minimum). The atmosphere has shifted — the minutes are now the subject of discussion. The Chairperson questions the Secretary's notations. Why did the Secretary record "(long pause)" during the discussion of evacuating the eastern district? Why was a member's statement about her own missing family members recorded under "Other Business" rather than "Urgent Matters"? Why has the Secretary never, in nine meetings, expressed a personal opinion about the crisis? The interrogation escalates. One member states that the Secretary's neutrality is itself a form of the disease — that maintaining procedural detachment while recording the dissolution of the city is indistinguishable from the nonparticipation they are trying to address. The Secretary's response is recorded only as: "The Secretary noted that the function of minutes is to record what occurred, not to editorialize on what occurred." The Chairperson moves that the Secretary be censured. The motion fails for lack of a second.

This is the Stranger/trial beat: the Secretary is judged not for what they have done but for what they have failed to feel — or failed to perform feeling.

### Beat 4: The Confession in the Margins (Minutes of Meeting No. 11)
~600 words

Two members present. The committee technically lacks quorum. The Secretary records the meeting anyway. The format begins to crack. A timestamp reads "14:07 or perhaps 14:12, I am no longer certain." An agenda item is listed as: "Item 3: What remains." The discussion between the Chairperson and the single remaining member becomes something closer to conversation than proceedings, and the Secretary's notation drifts — "(The Chairperson looked at her hands for what felt like a long time)" and then, finally, embedded in a note about attendance: "The Secretary wishes to note, for the record, that the Secretary's spouse has not been seen in one hundred and seventeen days, and that the Secretary continued to attend these meetings not because the Secretary believed attendance would restore civic function but because the act of recording was the last signal the Secretary knew how to send, and that sending a signal requires believing someone will receive it, and that believing someone will receive it is itself a form of participation, and that participation was the thing we were losing, and so the Secretary continued." This is the one long Saramago sentence in the document — the form cracking open.

### Beat 5: Final Minutes (Meeting No. 12)
~500 words

One member present: the Secretary. The Secretary convenes the meeting, notes the absence of all other members, records that quorum is not achieved, and then proceeds anyway. The format is immaculate — headers, timestamps, agenda items. But the agenda items are strange and tender: "Item 1: The sound the building makes when it is empty. Item 2: Whether the streetlights are still operating (confirmed: yes). Item 3: The minutes of this meeting." Under Item 3, the Secretary records: "The Secretary moved that these minutes be entered into the permanent record. There being no second, the motion was not carried. The Secretary entered the minutes into the permanent record." The final line is the standard closing formula: "There being no further business, the meeting was adjourned at [timestamp]." But the timestamp is blank.

No resolution. The Secretary has not changed, or healed, or found meaning. The act of recording continues after the thing being recorded has vanished. The form survives. The city does not.

## Key Scenes

1. **The quorum debate (Beat 2, Meeting No. 7)**: The committee discusses whether it can legally redefine quorum while potentially lacking quorum to do so. Played straight — no irony announced. The reader supplies the allegory.

2. **The trial of the Secretary (Beat 3, Meeting No. 9)**: The Chairperson interrogates the Secretary's emotional neutrality. The Meursault beat — condemned not for an action but for a failure to feel appropriately. The Secretary's defense is purely procedural: the function of minutes is to record.

3. **The long sentence (Beat 4, Meeting No. 11)**: The Secretary breaks format with a single sprawling Saramago-style sentence about their missing spouse and why they keep attending. This is the crack in the container — the moment the form cannot hold the content. But the form does not shatter; it absorbs the confession and continues.

4. **The self-convened meeting (Beat 5, Meeting No. 12)**: The Secretary alone, recording a meeting with no members, moving motions with no seconds, entering minutes into a permanent record that no one will read. The image of institutional procedure continuing past the end of its purpose.

## Emotional Trajectory

**Opening**: Bureaucratic calm. The reader is oriented by the familiarity of committee minutes — tedious, dry, the language of governance. Slight unease from what the language describes, but the form domesticates the content.

**Middle**: Growing dread. The form remains stable but the content beneath it deteriorates. Each meeting has fewer members. Each report describes wider failure. The tension between the container (orderly minutes) and the content (social collapse) becomes the primary emotional engine.

**Turn**: The trial of the Secretary. The emotional trajectory pivots when the committee turns on the Secretary — when the document becomes about the person writing it. The reader's relationship to the text changes: they are no longer reading institutional minutes but a personal record of someone being judged for their composure.

**Climax**: The Secretary's confession. Not a speech — a notation. One long sentence embedded in the minutes where professional neutrality finally breaks. But the break is not cathartic. It is recorded, formatted, and the minutes continue.

**Close**: Flatness. Not resolution but continuation. The Secretary records a meeting with no one. The form holds. The timestamp is blank. The emotional effect should be desolation masked as procedure — the reader feels what the Secretary does not say.

## Formula Integration Plan

### From Saramago (Author A):
- **Long flowing sentences**: The story is predominantly in the clipped, procedural voice of minutes — but Saramago's style erupts in specific moments: the Secretary's confession in Beat 4 (one unbroken sentence that sprawls across half a page), and in passages where the omniscient voice of the minutes-taker begins editorializing despite themselves. The accumulation of subordinate clauses mirrors the accumulation of failures the committee records.
- **Sardonic omniscient voice**: The minutes-taker is technically neutral, but the choice of what to record — "(long pause)," "(the sound of rain was noted)," or recording a statement under "Other Business" rather than "Urgent Matters" — constitutes sardonic commentary through bureaucratic form.
- **Characters stripped of names**: All characters are referred to by function: the Chairperson, the Health Subcommittee Representative, the Secretary, the Member Who Arrived Late. When members stop attending, they are noted only by absence.
- **The absurd treated with matter-of-fact gravity**: The entire premise — a city losing its quorum of participation — is presented through the most matter-of-fact form imaginable (meeting minutes), creating the Saramago effect of catastrophe rendered in mundane prose.

### From Camus (Author B):
- **Spare declarative sentences**: The default voice of the minutes. "The motion was carried. The committee moved to the next item. The Chairperson noted that attendance had declined." Short, flat, precise. Philosophical weight through understatement.
- **Detached clarity as subject**: The Secretary's professional neutrality — the refusal to editorialize — mirrors Meursault's refusal to perform expected emotions. This neutrality becomes the thing the committee judges. Emotional flatness as a style that reveals rather than conceals.
- **Mediterranean physicality**: The occasional notations of physical reality intruding on the proceedings — the sound of traffic (or its absence), the quality of light through the committee room windows, the temperature of the room as the building's heating system fails. Physical, sensory details in the margins of the bureaucratic document.
- **The irrational act**: The Secretary recording the meeting when no one is present. This is the story's equivalent of Meursault pulling the trigger — an act that makes no logical sense but is the truest thing the character does.

### From The Stranger (Work X):
- **The trial**: The committee's interrogation of the Secretary (Beat 3) replicates the trial structure — judgment falls not on the act but on the person's character. The Secretary is censured not for incompetence but for insufficient distress.
- **Refusal to perform**: The Secretary will not editorialize, will not mourn publicly, will not insert feeling into the record. This radical honesty — or radical detachment — is what society cannot tolerate.
- **Institutional systems imposing meaning**: The committee itself is an institutional system trying to impose narrative meaning (cause, solution, progress) on something that resists meaning. The minutes are the tool of that imposition.

### From Blindness (Work Y):
- **Single impossible change**: People stop participating. Not a disease, not a political event — a threshold crossed, the social quorum no longer met. Like blindness, it arrives without authorship.
- **Institutional collapse through incremental failure**: Hospitals don't close; they empty. Transit doesn't stop; ridership drops to nothing. The committee doesn't dissolve; it loses members one by one. Everything fails not through dramatic rupture but through the accumulation of small withdrawals.
- **The allegorical method with ruthless realism**: The premise is fantastical (an entire city ceasing to participate) but it is played out with grinding institutional realism — committee meetings, reports, attendance rolls, motions carried or tabled.

## Title

**Quorum Not Present**

Rationale: Does not start with "The." Diverges from existing title patterns (no possessives, no gerunds, no "and" constructions). The phrase is both parliamentary procedure (a formal declaration that a body lacks the membership required to act) and the story's allegorical core (the city, the committee, the social contract — all have lost their quorum). It is blunt, declarative, in Camus's register. And it carries an undertone of absence that resonates beyond the bureaucratic: someone, something, is not present.

## Writer's Notes

**Tonal challenge**: The story is a document. The writer must resist the temptation to make the minutes obviously ironic or darkly humorous from the start. The early minutes should feel genuinely tedious — the reader should experience the actual boredom of committee proceedings. The horror and the beauty emerge from the accumulation, from the gradual realization that the dry language is recording something devastating.

**The Saramago-Camus tension**: These two styles are in productive opposition. Camus's brevity is the default mode (the language of minutes). Saramago's sprawl is the exception — the eruption. The story should feel like a Camus document with Saramago moments threatening to break through, and finally breaking through in Beat 4.

**What not to do**: Do not announce the allegory. Do not have a character say "we are the bacteria that have lost our quorum." Do not have the Secretary reflect on the meaning of what they are recording. The allegory arrives uninvited, like a relative at a funeral (Saramago's phrase from the author meeting). Let the reader do the work.

**The ending**: The timestamp is blank. That is the ending. Do not add a coda, an epilogue, an authorial note. The document ends because there is no one to end it. The form outlives its function. The container survives the content. The ship sails without a crew and the log continues to be written.

**Physical details**: Despite the bureaucratic form, ground the proceedings in sensory reality. The committee room has a window. The light changes across meetings. The coffee gets worse, then disappears. A clock is mentioned. The heating fails. The physical world insists on being recorded even as the institutional world dissolves. These are the Camus moments — sun, stone, the body reminding the mind that it exists.
