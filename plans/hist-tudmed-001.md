## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Viet Thanh Nguyen — *The Sympathizer* (Opening)

**Source**: AuthorA — Viet Thanh Nguyen
**Origin**: *The Sympathizer*, Chapter 1 opening
**Why representative**: Establishes Nguyen's signature first-person confessional voice — intellectually precise, self-aware, sardonic, a doubled consciousness narrating from a position of permanent duality.

> I am a spy, a sleeper, a spook, a man of two faces. Perhaps not surprisingly, I am also a man of two minds. I am not some misunderstood mutant from a comic book or a horror movie, although some have treated me as such. I am simply able to see any issue from both sides. Sometimes I flatter myself that this is a talent, and although it is admittedly one of a minor nature, it is perhaps also the sole talent I possess. At other times, when I reflect on how I cannot help but observe the world in such a fashion, I wonder if what I have should even be called talent. After all, a talent is something you use, not something that uses you. The talent you cannot not use, the talent that possesses you — that is a hazard, I must confess.

**Voice markers for writer**: Long, recursive sentences that circle back on themselves. Philosophical self-examination embedded in confessional narration. Wit that cuts both ways — aimed at self and at the structures surrounding the narrator. Declarative openings ("I am...") that establish identity through negation. The prose thinks aloud; it revises its own claims mid-sentence.

---

### Sample 2: Ken Follett — *The Pillars of the Earth* (Style Analysis)

**Source**: AuthorB — Ken Follett
**Origin**: *The Pillars of the Earth*, general prose style (from BookRags, editorial analysis)
**Why representative**: Demonstrates Follett's accessible, grounded, cinematic prose — plain diction, concrete sensory detail, characters defined by their physical actions and material surroundings.

Follett's prose is characterized by:
- Third-person close narration confined to specific characters, with no historical narrator — the reader experiences events only through the present-moment perception of the viewpoint character
- Economic, down-to-earth prose where every word serves the narrative — plain Anglo-Saxon vocabulary over Latinate abstraction
- Characters who are known through what they DO: build, weave, cut stone, bargain, run. Physical labor is a form of characterization
- Cinematic pacing — scenes structured as set-pieces with rising action, spatial awareness, and a clear sense of bodies moving through constructed environments
- Exposition folded into action — historical context emerges through characters encountering it, never through authorial lecturing
- Material specificity: the weight of stone, the cost of wool, the angle of a roof beam. The world is built from things you can touch

**Voice markers for writer**: Short declarative sentences interspersed with longer action sequences. Tactile, material description (textures, weights, temperatures). Characters defined by their competence or incompetence at practical tasks. Clear cause-and-effect plotting. The reader always knows where they are in physical space.

### Sample 3: Umberto Eco — *The Name of the Rose* (Opening Narration)

**Source**: WorkX — *The Name of the Rose*
**Origin**: Prologue / Opening of the First Day (William Weaver translation)
**Why representative**: Establishes Eco's layered narrator — Adso as an old man looking back, combining intellectual inquiry with sensory memory, the monastery as a total institution where architecture IS argument.

> Having reached the end of my poor sinner's life, my hair now white, I grow old as the world does, waiting to be lost in the bottomless pit of silent and deserted divinity, sharing in the light of angelic intelligences; confined now with my heavy, ailing body in this cell in the dear monastery of Melk, I prepare to leave on this parchment my testimony as to the wondrous and terrible events that I happened to witness in my youth, now repeating verbatim all I saw and heard, without venturing to seek a design, as if to leave to those who will come after (if the Antichrist has not come first) signs of signs, so that the prayer of deciphering may be exercised on them.

**Voice markers for writer**: The retrospective narrator — old age looking back at youth with mingled wonder and regret. Sentences that accumulate clauses like rooms in a monastery — each one opening onto the next, the reader always deeper inside. Intellectual precision married to sensory memory. Architecture as metaphor and literal subject. The investigation as a form of reading — deciphering signs within signs.

### Sample 4: Hilary Mantel — *Wolf Hall* (Opening)

**Source**: WorkY — *Wolf Hall*
**Origin**: Part One, Chapter 1, "Across the Narrow Sea, 1500"
**Why representative**: Mantel's radical present tense, her close third person that reads like interior first person, the body as the ground of all knowledge, and the refusal to explain — the reader must do the work.

> 'So now get up.' Felled, dazed, silent, he has fallen; knocked full length on the cobbles of the yard. His head turns sideways; his eyes are turned towards the gate, as if someone might arrive to help him out. One blow, properly placed, could kill him now.

And later, the catalogue of Cromwell's competences:

> He can draft a contract, train a falcon, draw a map, stop a street fight, furnish a house and fix a jury. He will quote you a nice point in the old authors, from Plato to Plautus and back again. He knows new poetry, and can say it in Italian. He works all hours, first up and last to bed. He makes money and he spends it. He will take a bet on anything.

**Voice markers for writer**: Present tense throughout — no retrospection, pure immersion. Pronoun ambiguity ("he" without immediate antecedent) that forces the reader to track consciousness. Short, physical sentences for violence; longer accumulative sentences for competence and memory. The body always first — pain, position, what the eyes see from the ground. Interior life conveyed through what the character notices, not what they confess. Self-erasure as survival: Cromwell is defined by his usefulness to others, not by his own desires.

## Wikipedia Concepts

### Research Log (20 concepts mined)

1. **Teasel** — Plant with hooked seed heads used in medieval cloth finishing; drawn across wet cloth to raise the nap before shearing. The naturally occurring hooks made synthetic replacements impossible for centuries.
2. **Woad** — *Isatis tinctoria*; primary blue dye in medieval England, cultivated in Lincolnshire and Somerset. Chemically identical to indigo but required vastly more plant material for the same saturation.
3. **Anchorite** — Religious recluse walled into a cell attached to a church, sealed by a bishop's authority. Three windows: one to the altar, one for food, one (sometimes) to the outside world. The bishop put his seal upon the wall.
4. **Paston Letters** — Largest surviving collection of 15th-century English correspondence (1422-1509); documents daily life, property disputes, marriage negotiations, and war during the Wars of the Roses.
5. **Tally stick** — Hazel wood sticks used as Exchequer accounting records. Notched and split lengthwise — the "stock" (lender) and "foil" (borrower) halves had to match. A thousand pounds: a cut the thickness of a palm. A penny: a single cut without removing wood.
6. **Rood screen** — Carved wooden screen separating chancel from nave in medieval churches. The dado panels painted with saints; the upper portion open tracery. Most destroyed during the Reformation.
7. **Sweating sickness** — Mysterious 1485 epidemic that killed 15,000 Londoners in six weeks. Henry VII postponed his coronation. Cause unknown. Onset sudden, death or recovery within 8-10 hours.
8. **Misericord** — Small carved shelf on the underside of a hinged choir stall, used to support standing monks during prayer. Because buttocks rested against them, religious subjects were forbidden — so carvers depicted secular and pagan scenes: folklore, animals, domestic comedy.
9. **Sympathetic strings** — Auxiliary strings on instruments that are never played directly, only resonating in response to notes struck on the main strings. The musician retunes them for each mode.
10. **Palimpsest** — Manuscript page where text has been scraped off and overwritten. With time, the original writing reappears beneath the new. The most valuable palimpsests are those overwritten earliest, before the scraping technique became total.
11. **Flemish weavers' migration** — Edward III's 1331 letters patent invited John Kempe of Flanders and other textile workers to England. A separate guild of foreign weavers established in London by 1362. Successive waves of Flemish immigrants brought weaving expertise.
12. **Ergotism / St. Anthony's Fire** — Poisoning from ergot fungus in rye bread. Convulsions, hallucinations of demons, gangrenous limbs falling off. By the late 15th century, ~370 hospitals across Europe to treat outbreaks.
13. **Danse macabre (Lübeck)** — Bernt Notke's 1463 painting on 26m of linen, showing 24 life-sized couples dancing with Death. The dead dancers were lively and agile; the living looked clumsy and passive. The cityscape behind included ships and local structures. Destroyed by bombing in 1942.
14. **Trial by ordeal** — Judicial practice where the accused plunged a hand into boiling water to retrieve a stone. The hand was bound, examined after three days. Healing = innocent. Festering = guilty. Abolished by the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215, but persisted informally.
15. **Fulling** — Cloth-finishing process: wetting the fabric, pounding or walking on it (hence "fulling mills" and the surname "Fuller"). Removes impurities, thickens the weave.
16. **Medieval wool trade decline** — 25,000 sacks of raw wool exported from England in 1280; by 1476, only 9,706. Exports shifted from raw wool to finished cloth — 10,000 cloths/year in 1350 to 60,000 by 1447.
17. **Dissolution of the monasteries** — Henry VIII's 1536-1540 campaign. What became of the monks: some received pensions, some became parish priests, some simply vanished from records.
18. **L'abberdaan** — The term for Aberdeen cod in Flanders and Cologne in the 13th century. Fish shipped in large Hamburg barrels across the North Sea.
19. **Powdered pumice** — Used to scrape text from vellum in later medieval palimpsests. The earlier method (milk and oat bran) left traces; pumice was more thorough but destroyed the under-text irretrievably.
20. **Hagioscope / squint** — The small window in an anchorite's cell wall that allowed viewing the altar and receiving the Eucharist. Sometimes fitted with a shutter that could be closed from either side.

### Two Selected Concepts for Fusion

**1. Teasel (cloth finishing)** — The Flemish weaver narrator will be a *teaseler* specifically — the person who draws the hooked teasel frames across wet cloth to raise the nap. This is not decorative detail. The teasel's hooks are natural — no human-made tool can replicate them. The weaver's narration will be structured around the metaphor of raising what is hidden in the fabric to the surface: pulling buried fibers into visibility. The teasel work is also a form of reading — feeling the cloth's resistance, knowing by touch where the weave is weak. The weaver "teasels" the truth out of the monastery's smooth surface.

**2. Misericord (hidden choir carvings)** — The institutional chronicle narrator (the monastery's official record) will describe the commissioning and installation of new choir stalls with misericords. The carvings on the hidden undersides — depicting a fox preaching to chickens, a woman beating a man with a distaff, a pig playing a hurdy-gurdy — become the counter-text within the institution itself. The monastery commissions pious art for public view while tolerating profane art where only monks' buttocks will encounter it. This becomes a structural metaphor for the chronicle's own blindness: what is hidden from the official record is still carved into the institution's body, visible to anyone willing to look underneath.

## 1. Premise

England, 1487. A Flemish teaseler named Pieter van Audenarde has fled trade violence in Bruges and taken work at a Yorkshire priory whose prior is entangled in the aftermath of Lambert Simnel's failed rebellion. Three voices tell the story of a single week: the teaseler who reads cloth like text, the prior's secretary who reads the court's lethal signals and erases himself to survive, and the priory's own chronicle — the institutional voice that records acquisitions, liturgical hours, and deaths with the same untroubled confidence. A monk has been found dead in the scriptorium. The chronicle says he died peacefully. The secretary knows he was strangled. The teaseler knows whose wool was used to do it — because he recognizes the fiber under the dead man's fingernails as a cloth he raised the nap on three days earlier, in the priory's own fulling house.

The engine: three unreliable accounts of the same death, each narrator concealing something different, the truth available only in the contradictions between them.

---

## 2. Protagonist(s)

Because the risk card demands multiple voices, there are three narrators. But the story's emotional center — the character who ACTS and whose action carries consequences — is:

### Pieter van Audenarde, 31, Flemish teaseler

- **Situation**: Displaced from Bruges after guild riots over English cloth imports. Has spent two years moving between English towns, offering his finishing skills to whoever will pay. Arrived at the priory three weeks ago to raise the nap on a large consignment of broadcloth commissioned by the Earl of Lincoln's household. Speaks English with an accent he cannot shed. Carries a teasel frame his dead wife made — she carved the handles before she died of the sweating sickness in 1485, two years into their marriage. He has not remarried. The frame is the only object he brought from Flanders.
- **What he WANTS**: To finish the cloth, collect his wage, and move on. He does not want to be involved. He has learned that involvement with English institutional politics is how foreigners lose their hands or their lives.
- **What he NEEDS**: To stop treating survival as sufficient. His wife's death and his displacement have taught him that attachment is a liability — but his refusal to act when he possesses knowledge that could save others is its own form of violence. He needs to understand that the refugee's silence, while rational, is also the silence the powerful depend on.
- **His flaw**: He believes he is uninvolved. He believes that because he is foreign, temporary, passing through, the priory's politics cannot touch him and he bears no responsibility for what he witnesses. He is wrong. The cloth he finished is the murder weapon. His hands raised the nap that the killer gripped. He is already inside the story.

### Secondary narrator: Brother Eadric, 44, the prior's secretary

- **Situation**: Born the son of a fuller in Wakefield. Entered the priory at fourteen. Has served three priors. His literacy and administrative skill made him indispensable; his lack of family connections made him safe. Now serves Prior Godfrey, who backed the wrong side in the Simnel affair and is frantically rewriting the priory's recent correspondence to conceal his involvement. Eadric does the rewriting. He is the man who erases.
- **What he wants**: To protect the priory as an institution — not out of faith, but because the priory is the only structure that has ever valued him. If it falls, he falls. He has no identity outside it.
- **His flaw**: He has confused loyalty to the institution with loyalty to truth. He genuinely believes that protecting the priory's reputation IS the moral act, because he cannot imagine himself existing apart from it.

### Third narrator: The Chronicle of the Priory of St. Oswald

- **Nature**: Not a person but a document. Written in confident third-person institutional voice by whoever holds the post of chronicler (currently Brother Eadric, which creates a recursive problem — the secretary writes the record that conceals his own actions). Entries are dated, formal, impersonal. Records land grants, liturgical observances, weather, deaths, visitors. Does not lie through commission but through taxonomy — its categories have no space for murder, political conspiracy, or the interior lives of displaced foreigners.

## 3. Structure

### Opening Image/Scene (Hook)

*The teaseler's voice.* Pieter is working in the priory's fulling house at dawn, drawing a teasel frame across a length of wet grey broadcloth. The prose is tactile, specific — the resistance of the fiber, the angle of the hooks, the way the nap rises unevenly where the weave is flawed. He is thinking about his wife's hands, how she carved the handles with the grain of the ash. Then a novice runs in, white-faced, and says Brother Aldhelm has been found dead in the scriptorium with a piece of cloth in his fist. Pieter recognizes the cloth. He raised that nap yesterday. He says nothing.

### The Inciting Disruption

*Shift to the chronicle's voice.* A dated entry records Brother Aldhelm's death: "peacefully, in the night, at his labors, commending his soul to God." The chronicle notes that the scriptorium will be closed for a day of prayer. It also records, in the same neutral tone, the arrival of a royal commissioner — Sir Thomas Greystone — to inspect the priory's accounts and correspondence in connection with "recent disturbances in the realm" (the Simnel rebellion). The juxtaposition of a death and a political inspection in the same paragraph, with the same tonal weight, is the chronicle's blindness made visible.

### The Escalating Middle

*Eadric's voice.* The secretary is in the prior's chamber, recopying letters on fresh vellum. The originals — which contained the prior's cautious expressions of support for Simnel's claim — are being fed to the fire. Eadric is making palimpsests in reverse: not scraping old text to write new, but burning the original so only the rewritten version survives. He is also the chronicler. He wrote the entry about Aldhelm's death. He knows Aldhelm was strangled because Aldhelm discovered the letters before they could be destroyed — Aldhelm, the illuminator, who spent his nights in the scriptorium and whose hands were trained to read ink-weight and paper-age, and who recognized that the priory's account of its Simnel correspondence did not match what he remembered copying six months ago.

*Back to Pieter.* The royal commissioner interviews the teaseler — a routine inquiry about his origins, his trade credentials, his reason for being at the priory. The interview is conducted through an interpreter (Pieter's English is functional but not fluent enough for legal nuance). The commissioner is thorough and uninterested: Pieter is a foreigner, therefore a nobody, therefore not a suspect or a witness. This invisibility is exactly what Pieter has cultivated. But during the interview, he sees the commissioner's clerk recording his answers in a ledger — and realizes that the ledger is covered in the same broadcloth he raised the nap on. The priory sells its cloth. The cloth goes everywhere. His work is already entangled in every record, every transaction, every surface. There is no uninvolved position.

*The chronicle records:* the installation of new misericords in the choir. A carver from York has been working for three weeks. The chronicle describes the pious subjects: the Annunciation, St. Oswald's martyrdom, the lamb of God. It does not mention what is carved on the undersides — a fox in a monk's cowl preaching to geese, a demon stirring a cauldron, a woman holding a man's severed hand. The carver's hidden commentaries on the institution that paid him. The chronicle cannot see them because the chronicle does not look underneath.

### The Crisis Point

*Eadric's voice.* The commissioner has found inconsistencies in the priory's accounts — not the letters (those have been successfully rewritten) but the tally sticks. The physical financial records. Notches can't be recopied on fresh vellum. The stock-and-foil system means the priory's half must match the Exchequer's half, and the prior, in his panic, authorized payments to Simnel's agents that were recorded in hazel wood and cannot be unrecorded. Eadric is ordered to "lose" the tally sticks. He knows that destroying Exchequer records is treason. He also knows that if the tallies are found, the prior will name Eadric as the one who made the payments. Eadric visits Pieter in the fulling house — the first time the two narrators' accounts directly overlap. Eadric asks Pieter if he could hide the tallies inside a bale of finished cloth, packed among the teasel frames where no commissioner would search. Pieter understands what is being asked. He is being asked to swallow another institution's crime into his own portable, disposable, foreign body.

*Pieter's voice.* The moment of choice. He can hide the tallies and remain the invisible, uninvolved foreigner. Or he can refuse, knowing that refusal makes him visible — makes him a person with agency, and therefore a person who can be punished. His wife would have said: survive. His wife survived the crossing from Bruges and died of the sweat anyway. Survival is not a plan. It is a description of what hasn't happened yet.

### The Ending

*Three voices, final entries.*

**The chronicle:** Records that the priory has donated a large consignment of finished cloth to the commissioner's household, as a gesture of loyalty to the crown. Records that Brother Eadric has been transferred to a daughter house in Lincolnshire for "reasons of health." Records that the Flemish teaseler has departed. Does not record what Pieter actually did: he hid not the tallies but the original letters — the ones Eadric thought he had burned, but which Pieter had retrieved from the ashes, half-charred, because a man who works with fiber knows that parchment does not burn as easily as paper, and because a man who has lost everything knows the value of a document that proves what the institution wants forgotten. He hid them in the lining of a cloth bale addressed to the Earl's household. They will surface, or they will not. He cannot control what happens to the record once it leaves his hands.

**Eadric:** Writes one final letter — not for the chronicle, not for the prior, but for himself. A confession that no one will read. He addresses it to Aldhelm. He does not say "I killed you." He says "I wrote the entry that says you died peacefully." The distinction matters to him. He does not know if it should.

**Pieter:** Walks out of the priory gates carrying his wife's teasel frame. The road south. He does not look back at the buildings. He thinks about the cloth — all the cloth he has ever raised the nap on, the surfaces he made smooth, the hidden fibers he pulled into the light. He thinks about his wife's hands on the ash handles. He does not think about whether the letters will be found. That is someone else's story now. His hands are not clean. His hands have never been clean. The teasel's hooks are a tool for making hidden things visible, and visibility is a form of violence, and he chose it anyway.

**One thread left unresolved:** Did the letters survive? Will the Earl's household find treasonous correspondence in a cloth lining? Pieter does not know. The chronicle will never record it. The reader will never be certain.

## 4. Key Scenes

1. **The teaseling scene (opening)** — Pieter working the cloth at dawn, the texture and resistance of the fiber, the memory of his wife's hands on the frame. Then the novice's interruption. This scene establishes Pieter's expertise, his grief, and his instinct toward uninvolvement — all through physical labor. It also plants the murder weapon as something his hands have touched.

2. **The palimpsest-in-reverse (Eadric rewriting the letters)** — Eadric in the prior's chamber, feeding originals to the fire while copying sanitized versions on fresh vellum. The prose is precise about the craft of forgery: matching ink age, simulating a careless hand, leaving deliberate minor errors so the copies don't look too clean. This scene does double duty: establishes Eadric's skills AND reveals the murder's motive. Aldhelm found the discrepancy. Aldhelm is dead.

3. **The commissioner's interview with Pieter** — A scene of bureaucratic power rendered as a kind of comedy: the commissioner's boredom, the interpreter's imperfect translation, Pieter's strategic performance of foreignness. But the comedy curdles when Pieter sees the cloth-covered ledger. The moment he realizes his labor is not separate from the institution's records. His work is literally the surface on which power writes itself.

4. **The misericord installation (chronicle voice)** — The chronicle describes the new choir stalls as a pious achievement. A separate scene — perhaps glimpsed through Pieter's narration — shows the undersides: the subversive carvings the institution has already absorbed without recognizing them. The carver, like the teaseler, encodes truths in surfaces the powerful will never examine.

5. **The tally stick crisis / Eadric asks Pieter** — The two narrators finally share a scene, and their accounts of it will contradict. Eadric will describe Pieter as reluctant but compliant, a man who understands that foreigners cooperate. Pieter will describe Eadric as desperate, a man who has mistaken the institution's survival for his own. The reader must hold both accounts and find the third meaning between them.

## 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning**: The reader feels the texture of a world — wool, stone, cold water, the scratch of a quill. There is an initial pleasure in the specificity, the sense of being inside a lost material culture. This is grounded, tactile, almost comforting.

**Middle**: Discomfort arrives as the reader realizes that all three narrators are unreliable in different ways — Pieter through deliberate omission (he withholds what he knows), Eadric through self-justifying revision (he rewrites his own motives), and the chronicle through systemic incapacity (it literally cannot categorize what happened). The reader becomes an investigator, weighing contradictions, noting what each voice refuses to say. This should feel intellectually engaging but also increasingly claustrophobic — the truth is visible but no single narrator will say it plainly.

**End**: The reader feels the weight of Pieter's choice — not as triumph (he has not solved the murder, has not achieved justice, has not saved himself) but as a small, costly act of visibility. He has entered the record. He has placed evidence where it might be found. He has refused to be the invisible foreigner whose silence makes institutional crimes possible. But the evidence may never surface. The reader should feel something close to vertigo: the sense that history is made of exactly these buried documents, these half-burned letters hidden in cloth linings, and that most of them are never found. The feeling is: what survives is not what happened, and what happened is not what was recorded, and the gap between those things is where people actually live.

## 6. Formula Integration

### AuthorA — Viet Thanh Nguyen
**Most visible in: Pieter's narration throughout, but concentrated in his interior monologue during the commissioner's interview.**

The refugee consciousness — the doubled vision of a person who sees both the institution's self-image and its actual workings, because he stands outside its assumptions. Pieter's understanding that foreignness is simultaneously a form of vulnerability and a form of knowledge: he sees what the monks cannot see because he has no investment in not seeing it. The prose in Pieter's sections should have Nguyen's recursive, self-examining quality — long sentences that revise their own claims, philosophical asides embedded in sensory observation, a narrator who cannot stop analyzing his own position even as he tries to act from it. The confessional address, the sense of speaking to someone who may or may not be listening.

### AuthorB — Ken Follett
**Most visible in: The fulling house scenes, the material specificity of cloth production, and the multi-plot architecture.**

The sweeping, cinematic quality of Follett's prose governs the story's physical world: the fulling house with its vats and teasel frames, the scriptorium with its vellum and ink, the prior's chamber with its fire and ledgers. Characters are known through their skills — Pieter by how he handles cloth, Eadric by how he forges documents, the carver by his hidden misericords. The accessible, forward-driving plot momentum (a death, an investigation, a political conspiracy) comes from Follett. The story should be readable as a thriller even by someone uninterested in postcolonial theory. Every scene moves something forward. The reader always knows what is at stake.

### WorkX — *The Name of the Rose* (Eco)
**Most visible in: The scriptorium murder and the investigation's structure.**

A death in a house of learning, caused by a text the institution cannot tolerate. Aldhelm the illuminator dies because he can read the physical properties of a document — ink weight, paper age — the way William of Baskerville reads signs. The investigation proceeds through reading: reading cloth, reading parchment, reading the chronicle's omissions, reading the misericords' hidden faces. Intellectual inquiry as a dangerous act. The monastery as a total institution where knowledge is simultaneously the highest value and the greatest threat. The labyrinth in Eco's monastery becomes, here, the layered textuality of the priory's records — chronicle, tally sticks, letters, cloth — each one a surface that both reveals and conceals.

### WorkY — *Wolf Hall* (Mantel)
**Most visible in: Eadric's narration — the man who survives by erasing himself.**

Eadric IS the Cromwell figure: a man of low birth who has made himself indispensable to a vulnerable institution, who navigates lethal politics by becoming whatever the room requires, whose interiority is a fog. His prose should have Mantel's present-tense immediacy, the pronoun ambiguity, the sense of a consciousness that is always calculating and never quite revealing its calculations. The court's lethal machinery is translated here into the priory's post-Simnel panic — a smaller stage but the same stakes. Self-erasure as survival strategy. The distinction between loyalty and complicity, which Eadric cannot or will not make. His final letter to Aldhelm — "I wrote the entry that says you died peacefully" — is the Mantel moment: the confession that isn't quite a confession, the man who has become so identified with the institution's voice that his own voice is nearly inaudible.

## 7. Title

**Nap and Ash**

"Nap" — the raised surface of finished cloth, created by teaseling; also the surface that conceals the weave beneath. "Ash" — what remains after Eadric burns the letters; also the wood of Pieter's teasel frame, carved by his dead wife. The title is a compound form (two nouns joined by "and") that sounds material, specific, un-literary. It does not explain itself. It does not start with "The." It sits in the mouth like something real — two textures, one soft, one powdery, both residues of a process that has already happened.

## Structural Notes for Writer

### Voice Differentiation (Risk Card Implementation)

The three narrators must be **formally distinguishable** — not just in content but in prose style:

1. **Pieter (the teaseler)**: First person, past tense. Long, searching sentences that revise themselves. Sensory-rich but philosophically layered. The refugee's doubled consciousness: always seeing two versions of every event. Flemish word choices occasionally bleeding through — a foreign rhythm in the English syntax. He addresses an unnamed "you" occasionally, as if writing a letter to someone. (Nguyen influence dominant.)

2. **Eadric (the secretary)**: Close third person, present tense. Short, clipped sentences for action; longer ones when he is rationalizing. The prose has a calculating quality — always assessing, always weighing. Pronoun ambiguity: "he" could refer to Eadric or the prior, and the reader must work it out. Minimal interiority; we know Eadric through what he does and what he notices, not what he confesses. (Mantel influence dominant.)

3. **The Chronicle**: Third person, institutional present ("It is recorded that..."). Formal, confident, dated entries. Latin abbreviations. No hedging, no uncertainty. The prose's very smoothness is its lie: it has no category for violence, conspiracy, or doubt. Its omissions are legible only in contrast to the other two voices. When the chronicle fails to mention something the reader has already learned from another narrator, the absence becomes the loudest thing on the page.

### Sections should alternate: Pieter — Chronicle — Eadric — Chronicle — Pieter — Eadric — Chronicle — Pieter — Eadric — Chronicle — Pieter

The chronicle entries are shorter (200-400 words each). Pieter's and Eadric's sections are longer (600-1,200 words each). The overlapping event — Eadric's visit to the fulling house — should be narrated twice, once by each human narrator, with contradictions the reader must adjudicate.

### Historical Setting Notes

- **1487**: Two years after Bosworth. Henry VII is king but his grip is insecure. Lambert Simnel was crowned in Dublin in May 1487; defeated at the Battle of Stoke Field in June. The aftermath involved investigations of anyone who had expressed sympathy for the Yorkist cause.
- **Yorkshire priory**: A Benedictine house, not large, dependent on wool and cloth production for its income. Has produced broadcloth for noble households for three generations.
- **Flemish diaspora**: By 1487, Flemish weavers and cloth workers have been present in England for over a century, but they remain identifiably foreign — different guild traditions, different techniques, different language. They are tolerated for their skills and resented for the same reason.
- **The sweating sickness**: First outbreak was 1485. Pieter's wife died in it. This grounds his displacement in specific historical fact.
