**Risk card**: #10 — THE PROTAGONIST IS WRONG

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Michael Crichton (Author A)
**Source**: *Jurassic Park*, Ian Malcolm's speeches on scientific hubris
**Why representative**: Shows Crichton's signature mode — clinical precision, systems-thinking vocabulary, a character dismantling institutional arrogance through controlled argument.

> "You know what's wrong with scientific power? ... It's a form of inherited wealth. And you know what astonishes me? Most people don't think about it at all. I mean, you seriously want to know why the world is in such a mess? Because it's inherited knowledge. ... Most kinds of power require a substantial sacrifice by whoever wants the power. There is an apprenticeship, a discipline lasting many years. ... But scientific power is like inherited wealth: attained without discipline. You read what others have done, and you take the next step. ... There is no discipline lasting many decades. There is no mastery: old scientists are ignored. There is no humility before nature. ... And because you can stand on the shoulders of giants, you can accomplish something quickly. You don't even know exactly what you have done, but already you have reported it, patented it, and sold it. And the buyer will have even less discipline than you. The buyer simply purchases the power, like any commodity. The buyer doesn't even conceive that any discipline might be necessary."

### Sample 2: Alexandre Dumas (Author B)
**Source**: *The Three Musketeers*, Chapter 1 — d'Artagnan's arrival
**Why representative**: Dumas's kinetic confrontation-driven prose, characters defined by action and affront, momentum that never slows for reflection.

> "I say, sir, you sir, who are hiding yourself behind that shutter — yes, you, sir, tell me what you are laughing at, and we will laugh together!" The stranger looked at the young man with a faint expression of disdain, and retired from the window. D'Artagnan, not of a character to allow a man who had the insolence to laugh at him to escape, drew his sword and cried: "Turn, turn, Master Joker, lest I strike you behind!" The stranger turned on the staircase, and regarding the young man with an air of cool astonishment: "This horse is decidedly, or rather has been in his youth, a buttercup. It is a color very well known in botany, but till the present time very rare among horses." D'Artagnan's anger rose to such a pitch that he lunged with his sword without further hesitation. The stranger parried with a gentlemanly ease that provocation could not unsettle, then two companions fell upon d'Artagnan with sticks and shovels, beating him until his sword shattered and a blow to the forehead sent him sprawling.

### Sample 3: *King Solomon's Mines* (Work X — Haggard)
**Source**: Chapter V, "Our March Into the Desert"
**Why representative**: Quatermain's first-person expedition narration — the scale of landscape as antagonist, the physical reckoning of the colonial body against African terrain, the map as false promise.

> The desert stretched before us in great waves of sand, tinted orange and copper by the afternoon light, and behind us the fertile land fell away like a memory of something we had lost the right to return to. Good walked steadily, Sir Henry with the long strides of a man accustomed to purpose, and Umbopa moved among us with the ease of someone who had never needed a map to know where he was. I watched the blue mountains on the horizon and felt what all expedition men feel at the threshold — the conviction that the thing ahead was worth the crossing, and the deeper conviction, never spoken, that the crossing might prove otherwise. ... We had food for perhaps four days if we were careful and water for two if we were not. The mathematics of the desert are simple. They admit no argument and extend no credit.

[Note to writer: Haggard's structure is escalating stages of physical deprivation — desert, mountains, cold, exhaustion — each stage stripping away one more layer of the expedition's preparedness. The map promises what the terrain refuses. Use this staged escalation.]

### Sample 4: *The Poisonwood Bible* (Work Y — Kingsolver)
**Source**: The demonstration garden passage and Nathan Price's Congo mission
**Why representative**: Kingsolver's central metaphor of colonial arrogance — bringing Kentucky Wonder beans to soil that has its own logic, the sincerity of the imposition making it worse, not better.

> Nathan Price planted Kentucky Wonder beans, crookneck and patty-pan squash, and Big Boy tomatoes in his demonstration garden — their first African miracle: "an infinite chain of benevolence rising from these small, crackling seed packets, stretching out from our garden into a circle of other gardens, flowing outward across the Congo like ripples from a rock dropped in a pond." The garden failed. The vegetable plants shot up in the equatorial sun but produced no fruit, because the flowers required pollinators that did not exist in the Congo, and the pollinators did not exist because these plants had never grown there, and the plants had never grown there because the soil, the insects, the rainfall, the entire system of life in that place had been negotiating its own terms for millennia and had not been consulted.

[Note to writer: Kingsolver's method — the arrogant man is not a monster. He sincerely believes he brings something valuable. The tragedy is the sincerity. Our protagonist must share this quality: his competence is genuine, his preparation is real, his failure is not stupidity but a category error so deep he cannot see it.]

## Wikipedia Concepts

**Research pool** (5 articles examined):
1. Ol Doinyo Lengai — Tanzania volcano, only one on Earth erupting natrocarbonatite lava (cool, fast, black, turns white)
2. Laterite — tropical red soil, soft when wet, hardens to iron-rigid structure on air exposure; used for colonial-era road-building
3. Cassiterite — tin ore mined in colonial Congo by Belgian companies; surface deposits dug by hand
4. Pneumatic caisson — pressurized underwater construction chamber; causes decompression sickness
5. Driver ants (Dorylus) — massive colonies that move across forest floor consuming everything in their path

**Selected concepts (2):**

1. **Laterite soil**: Integrated as the expedition's geological target. The protagonist is surveying laterite deposits for a mining consortium — laterite is rich in nickel and iron, soft when wet (can be cut with a spade), but hardens to stone on air exposure. This material fact becomes metaphor: what seems yielding and workable when you first encounter it locks into something immovable once exposed. The protagonist treats the land as soft, cuttable, extractable. He is wrong.

2. **Natrocarbonatite (Ol Doinyo Lengai)**: A local guide, Amara, explains that the mountain near their survey site produces lava that is black when it erupts but turns white in the rain — the only volcano on Earth that does this. The protagonist dismisses it as local folklore until he sees the deposits himself. This detail seeds the story's epistemological theme: the land operates by rules his training never imagined. Things that should be hot are cool. Things that should be permanent change color. His categories do not hold.

## 1. Premise

A geological survey team — five people, well-funded, well-equipped — pushes into the interior of a fictional East African territory in 1962 to map laterite deposits for a European mining consortium. Their leader, a South African geologist named Piet Labuschagne, has done this work across three continents. He has never failed to deliver a viable survey. His maps, his instruments, his methodology are impeccable. The territory disagrees. Not through disaster — through a slower, more total refusal that Piet cannot recognize because his instruments do not measure what the territory is actually doing. The question the story asks: what happens when a competent man's competence is the thing that blinds him?

## 2. Protagonist

- **Name**: Piet Labuschagne, 41, South African-born geologist working for Consolidated Mineral Surveys (Brussels)
- **WANTS**: To complete the laterite survey in under three weeks, deliver the report, secure the consortium's next contract, and prove that the territory's interior is commercially viable — which will advance his career from field surveyor to regional director
- **NEEDS**: To understand that mapping a place is not the same as knowing it, and that his expertise is a wall between himself and what the territory is actually telling him
- **FLAW/BLIND SPOT (Risk Card #10 — architecturally central)**: Piet believes that sufficient preparation eliminates risk. His worldview is fundamentally mechanistic — the world is a system of measurable variables, and if you measure enough of them accurately enough, you can predict outcomes. He is not arrogant in the obvious way; he is polite, professional, respectful of local customs in the manner of a man who has read the cultural briefing. His arrogance is structural: he cannot conceive that the land might operate by principles his instruments cannot detect. Every early success on the expedition reinforces this blindness. His competence creates a positive feedback loop — each obstacle overcome confirms his model — until the model fails totally, and he has no framework for understanding why.
- **SPECIFIC WOUND**: Seven years ago, Piet's younger brother Hendrik died of dehydration on a survey in the Namib when their water cache was raided by local herders. Piet redesigned all his logistics protocols after Hendrik's death. He carries his brother's field notebook. He believes — truly, sincerely — that Hendrik died because of insufficient preparation, and that if he prepares well enough, no one else will die. This belief is the engine of his competence and the engine of his wrongness. Hendrik did not die because of bad logistics. Hendrik died because the Namib herders were facing their own drought and took the water because they needed it more, and the survey team's presence in that territory was itself the provocation. Piet has never understood this.

## 3. Structure

### Opening image/scene
The expedition's supply manifest — a two-page inventory of equipment rendered in Piet's precise handwriting. Theodolite, Brunton compass, thirty-seven geological sample bags, antimalarial tablets (chloroquine phosphate, 300mg), water purification tablets, canvas tents (2), paraffin stove, Hendrik's field notebook. The list is meticulous, confident, complete. It is also, the reader will come to understand, a document of everything that will prove insufficient. Open mid-action: the team is already two days into the interior, and Piet is checking supplies against the manifest by lantern light while the others sleep. He finds everything in order. He always finds everything in order.

### The inciting disruption
Three days in, the team reaches the first laterite deposit marked on the geological survey maps. The deposit is there — exactly where the maps say it should be. But the local village headman, through the guide Amara, tells them the site is closed. Not forbidden, not sacred — closed. "The ground is resting." Piet has no category for ground that rests. He has categories for sacred sites, for territorial disputes, for bribery. He has a protocol for each. But ground that rests is outside his system. He notes it in his log as a "local land-use designation, likely seasonal" and redirects the team to the secondary survey site, eight kilometers further in.

### The escalating middle
At the secondary site, the survey proceeds. The laterite is rich — better than projected. Piet cores samples, logs data, begins drafting the preliminary report. But the team begins to fracture:
- **Amara** (the local guide, Maa-speaking, hired in Arusha) grows quieter. He stops translating certain things the porters say to each other. When Piet asks what they're discussing, Amara says "the weather."
- **Colette Desmet** (Belgian cartographer, 29, the consortium's representative) notices that the laterite samples behave strangely — soft and cuttable when extracted, but hardening faster than expected when exposed to air. She mentions that laterite has always done this but "not this fast." Piet notes it as a variable within normal parameters.
- The porters begin leaving. Not deserting — just not there in the morning. Each day there are fewer. No confrontation, no demand for more pay. They are simply gone. Piet's logistics model accounts for labor attrition. He adjusts the schedule.
- The secondary site begins to flood from an unseasonal rain. Piet consults his rainfall data — the rains are two months early. He relocates the operation uphill. The laterite samples he has already cored are now locked into stone by the combination of rain and air exposure. They cannot be transported. He cores new samples.

Each setback is solvable. Each solution costs something — time, supplies, personnel, the route back. The expedition's positive feedback loop is still running: Piet overcomes every obstacle, which confirms that his method works, which prevents him from seeing that the obstacles are not random failures but a coherent message from a system he is not reading.

### The crisis point
Two weeks in. The team is down to Piet, Colette, Amara, and one porter named Joseph. They have reached a plateau where the laterite deposits are exceptional — the richest Piet has ever surveyed. He is elated. This will be the centerpiece of the report. He begins a detailed mapping of the deposit boundaries.

Amara takes Piet to a nearby ridgeline and shows him something: a flow of dark rock spilling down the hillside — natrocarbonatite, though Piet doesn't recognize it. Amara explains that the mountain produces stone that is born black and turns white. Piet says it's volcanic residue, probably calcium carbonate. Amara says no. He says the mountain changes its mind.

That night, Joseph leaves. Amara tells Piet that the porters have been leaving because the ground at the secondary site is not resting — it is waking. Piet asks what this means in geological terms. Amara says it does not mean anything in geological terms. Piet writes in his log: "Local superstition re: seismic activity. No tremors detected."

The next morning, the plateau is different. Not dramatically — no earthquake, no flood. The survey stakes Piet planted have sunk. Not fallen, not blown over. Sunk into the laterite as though the ground softened around them and then hardened again. His theodolite, set on its tripod on solid ground the night before, is now tilted fifteen degrees, one leg buried to the knee joint. The ground is neither soft nor hard. It is something his instruments have no measurement for.

Piet re-surveys. The data makes no sense. He re-surveys again. Same readings. He stands on the plateau with his brother's field notebook in one hand and a Brunton compass in the other and for the first time in his career the instruments are telling him something he cannot interpret. Not wrong data — uninterpretable data. The numbers are clean. The numbers describe a place that does not behave like any place he has mapped.

### The ending
Piet does not have an epiphany. He does not learn. He does what competent men do when the model fails: he decides the instruments need recalibration. He packs the samples he has, drafts the preliminary report based on the data from the first week (when everything was still working), and turns the expedition around.

On the walk out, Colette asks him what he'll write about the plateau. Piet says he'll note the anomalous readings and recommend a follow-up survey with better equipment. Colette says: "What if the equipment isn't the problem?" Piet doesn't answer, because the question doesn't fit any category he has.

The final image: Piet, back in Arusha, mailing the report to Brussels. He includes a note requesting upgraded surveying instruments for the follow-up expedition. He does not include Amara's explanations. He does not mention the ground resting or the ground waking. He mentions Hendrik once, in a margin note in his brother's field notebook: "Better preparation next time." The story ends with the report going into a mail slot — a document that is technically correct and fundamentally wrong, sent into a system that will read only what it is equipped to read.

The thread left open: whether the consortium sends the follow-up team. Whether the territory will rest or wake. Whether Amara, who knew, will agree to guide anyone again.

## 4. Key Scenes

1. **The manifest by lantern light** (opening): Establishes Piet's character through objects — his thoroughness, his brother's notebook, his belief that preparation is protection. The list itself does double duty: it is both competence displayed and a foreshadowing of everything the list cannot contain.

2. **"The ground is resting"**: The headman's refusal — delivered calmly, without drama. Piet's inability to hear it as anything other than a logistical obstacle. Amara translating with a hesitation that Piet interprets as a language difficulty, not a warning.

3. **The natrocarbonatite ridgeline**: Amara and Piet standing over dark stone that turns white. Two knowledge systems — geology and local understanding — looking at the same thing and seeing different objects entirely. Piet names it, categorizes it, dismisses it. Amara watches him do this with the patience of someone who has watched many people do this.

4. **The sinking survey stakes**: The moment the ground refuses to behave. No violence, no spectacle. Just the slow, implacable wrongness of stakes that have sunk into solid ground. Piet's hands shaking — the first and only physical sign that something in his model is cracking.

5. **The mail slot** (closing): Piet's report sliding into the slot in Arusha. A perfectly formatted document that maps a territory without understanding it, sent to people who will understand it even less. The sound of the slot closing.

## 5. Emotional Trajectory

The reader begins with **confidence** — Piet is competent, his preparation is thorough, the expedition feels professional and grounded. This confidence is real, not ironic; the reader should trust Piet's expertise before they start to doubt it.

Through the middle, the confidence develops a **seam of unease** — not fear, not dread, but the growing sense that the expedition is answering a question nobody asked. The porters leaving without explanation. Colette's quiet observations. Amara's silences. The reader begins to see what Piet cannot see, and the gap between the reader's understanding and Piet's produces a **mounting frustration mixed with pity** — the desire to reach into the story and turn him around.

At the crisis, the reader feels **vertigo** — not horror, but the disorientation of watching someone's entire framework of meaning become inadequate while they are still inside it. The survey stakes sinking is not scary. It is bewildering in the specific way that challenges to deep assumptions are bewildering.

At the ending, the reader feels **a heaviness that has no target** — not anger at Piet, because he is not a villain; not sadness for him, because he does not know he has lost anything. The feeling is closer to watching someone walk calmly in the wrong direction and being unable to call out. The mail slot closing is the sound of a system perpetuating itself.

## 6. Formula Integration

- **Crichton (Author A)**: Most visible in Piet's systems-thinking monologues and the expedition's techno-thriller logistics. The supply manifest opening, the clinical precision of Piet's log entries, the way he processes every setback through a framework of variables and parameters. The paragraph where Piet re-surveys the plateau and gets "clean numbers that describe a place that does not behave like any place he has mapped" is pure Crichton — the moment when the system exceeds the operator's ability to interpret it.

- **Dumas (Author B)**: Most visible in the ensemble dynamics and the kinetic forward momentum. The expedition must feel like it is always moving — even when things go wrong, the energy is of people acting, deciding, adjusting, not contemplating. Colette and Amara are Dumas characters: people with their own reasons for being there that have nothing to do with Piet's grand question. Colette needs the consortium's approval for her own career. Amara came back to this territory for reasons he does not explain. The scene where Amara shows Piet the natrocarbonatite should have the quality of a Dumas set piece — two people facing each other across an unbridgeable difference, with the energy of a duel conducted in conversation.

- **King Solomon's Mines (Work X)**: Most visible in the expedition structure itself — the staged penetration into increasingly resistant territory, each stage stripping away resources and certainty. The map (Piet's geological survey data) promises what the terrain refuses, exactly as da Silvestra's map promises Quatermain a route through the desert. The escalating physical deprivation (losing porters, losing time, losing the route back) echoes Haggard's desert crossing, where every mile forward is a mile that cannot be recovered.

- **The Poisonwood Bible (Work Y)**: Most visible in the "ground is resting" scene and in Piet's fundamental error — the category error of imposing a Western extractive framework on a place that has its own logic. Nathan Price's demonstration garden fails because the pollinators don't exist; Piet's survey fails because the categories don't exist. The arrogance is the same: sincere, well-funded, technically competent, and structurally incapable of hearing what the territory is saying. The closing image (the report mailed to Brussels) echoes Kingsolver's long view — the institutional machinery that produces these expeditions and ingests their reports without ever questioning the framework.

## 7. Title

**Soft When Wet**

Laterite is soft when wet; you can cut it with a spade. Expose it to air and it hardens to stone. The title works literally (the geological property that is the expedition's commercial interest), metaphorically (the territory that seems yielding until it isn't), and as an ironic commentary on Piet's worldview (the assumption that what is soft can be shaped, that what yields can be taken). It does not start with "The." It is three words, punchy, slightly ambiguous — could be menacing, could be descriptive, invites the reader in without explaining itself.
