# Story Plan: mr-south-asian-001

**Genre**: Magical Realism / South Asian Magical Realism

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Salman Rushdie — Midnight's Children (Work Y primary source)

**Source**: Midnight's Children (1981), opening chapter "The Perforated Sheet"
**Why representative**: Demonstrates Rushdie's maximalist accumulation of clauses, the fusion of personal body with national history, the comic energy in tragedy, and the unreliable narrator who announces his unreliability as a feature, not a bug.

> "I was born in the city of Bombay ... once upon a time. No, that won't do, there's no getting away from the date: I was born in Doctor Narlikar's Nursing Home on August 15th, 1947. And the time? The time matters, too. Well then: at night. No, it's important to be more ... On the stroke of midnight, as a matter of fact. Clock-hands joined palms in respectful greeting as I came. Oh, spell it out, spell it out: at the precise instant of India's arrival at independence, I tumbled forth into the world."

> "I am the sum total of everything that went before me, of all I have been seen done, of everything done-to-me. I am everyone everything whose being-in-the-world affected was affected by mine. I am anything that happens after I've gone which would not have happened if I had not come. Nor am I particularly exceptional in this matter; each 'I', every one of the now-six-hundred-million-plus of us, contains a similar multitude."

> "I was linked to history both literally and metaphorically, both actively and passively ... actively-literally, passively-metaphorically, actively-metaphorically and passively-literally, I was inextricably entwined with my world."

> "She fed us the birianis of dissension and the nargisi koftas of discord; and little by little, even the harmonies of my parents' autumnal love went out of tune."

> "Midnight has many children: the offspring of Independence were not all human. Violence, corruption, poverty, generals, chaos, greed and pepperpots ..."

**Style notes**: Rushdie's sentences accumulate like bazaar stalls. The narrator corrects himself, doubles back, insists on precision that becomes its own form of distortion. History is not something that happened but something being told, and the telling is always an act of self-aggrandizement that the narrator simultaneously believes and mocks. The magical (telepathy, nose-as-instrument) is treated not as wonder but as medical condition. Comedy and catastrophe are inseparable. The body is always both personal flesh and national territory.

### Sample 2: Arundhati Roy — The God of Small Things (Work X primary source)

**Source**: The God of Small Things (1997), opening chapter "Paradise Pickles & Preserves" and Chapter 21 "The Cost of Living"
**Why representative**: Demonstrates Roy's microscopic sensory attention, her fractured chronology that circles the known catastrophe, her compound-word inventions, her children's-eye perspective, and the devastating precision of her political-intimate sentences.

> "May in Ayemenem is a hot, brooding month. The days are long and humid. The river shrinks and black crows gorge on bright mangoes in still, dustgreen trees."

> "And the air was full of Thoughts and Things to Say. But at times like these, only the Small Things are ever said. Big Things lurk unsaid inside."

> "Little events, ordinary things, smashed and reconstituted. Imbued with new meaning. Suddenly they became the bleached bones of a story."

> "She wore flowers in her hair and carried magic secrets in her eyes. She smelled of nail polish and new clothes. Her manner was that of someone who had been taken apart, cool and reassembled."

> "Only that there were tears. Only that Quietness and Emptiness fitted together like stacked spoons. Only that there was a snuffling in the hollows at the base of a lovely throat. Only that a hard honey-colored shoulder had a semicircle of teethmarks on it. Only that they held each other close, long after it was over. Only that what they shared that night was not happiness, but hideous grief. Only that once again they broke the Love Laws. That lay down who should be loved. And how. And how much."

> "The way her body existed only where he touched her. The rest of her was smoke."

> "If he touched her, he couldn't talk to her, if he loved her he couldn't leave, if he spoke he couldn't listen, if he fought he couldn't win."

> "That's what careless words do. They make people love you a little less."

Roy's signature moves: compound inventions ("dustgreen," "furrywhirring," "sariflapping"), capitalized abstractions that children give weight to ("Things to Say," "Love Laws"), the anaphoric "Only that" building emotional pressure through repetition, and the fracturing of chronology so that the reader knows the catastrophe from page one and spends the novel learning not what happened but how the small violations accumulated to make it inevitable.

**The Love Laws**: "the laws that lay down who should be loved, and how. And how much. The laws that make grandmothers grandmothers, uncles uncles, mothers mothers, cousins cousins, jam jam, and jelly jelly." These aren't metaphors. They are the architecture people live inside. Caste, propriety, Christianity, colonialism — all collapsed into a set of unwritten rules enforced through violence disguised as tradition.

### Sample 3: Rushdie — The Perforated Sheet as Structural Device

**Source**: Midnight's Children, Book One: The Perforated Sheet
**Why representative**: Shows Rushdie's technique of using a physical object (the sheet with a hole) as both literal plot device and structural metaphor for partial knowledge, for how we fall in love with fragments, for how narrative itself reveals by concealment.

Aadam Aziz, Saleem's grandfather, is summoned to treat the daughter of a landowner. She is hidden behind a perforated sheet — he may examine her only through a seven-inch hole, one body part at a time. Over months and visits, he falls in love with her piece by piece: a throat, a kneecap, a shoulder. He "came to think of the perforated sheet as something sacred and magical, because through it he had seen the things which had filled up the hole inside him."

This is Rushdie's great structural lesson: revelation by concealment. The reader of Midnight's Children learns the story through perforations — through digressions, corrections, lies, admissions. The truth is never seen whole. The sheet is never removed. Love, like history, is assembled from glimpses.

### Sample 4: Roy — Sensory Precision as Political Act

**Source**: The God of Small Things, various passages on the Meenachal river and the History House
**Why representative**: Shows how Roy's sensory writing is never merely atmospheric — every texture, smell, and color carries political weight.

The Meenachal river runs through the novel as a living body that changes with the political climate. In childhood, it is wild and full; as the children return as adults, the river has been "dammed and diverted and reduced to a thin, dirty trickle." Roy describes a river that "smelled of shit and pesticides." The History House — the ruined colonial bungatory where Velutha meets Ammu, where the Love Laws are broken — is described with the same microscopic attention Roy gives to pickles and grasshoppers. It is "waist-deep in water. Water in the pots. Water buoyed the bedbugs."

Roy's technique: the sensory world is the political world. The smell of the river IS the collapse of Kerala's ecology. The texture of the History House IS the residue of colonialism. She never says "colonialism." She says "waist-deep in water" and the reader understands.

### Prose Target for the Writer

**Sentence-level**: Aim for Roy's sensory density and compound inventions ("dustgreen," "honeycolored") when describing domestic space, food, bodies, rooms. Use Rushdie's accumulating clauses and comic self-correction when the narrative moves to the panoramic — the history of the town, the logic of the caste system, the absurdities of bureaucracy. The narrator should have Rushdie's unreliable energy (correcting themselves, insisting on details, admitting gaps) but Roy's commitment to staying in the body, in the room, until the reader can't stand it.

**The magic**: Must be ordinary. Must be resented. Must require bureaucratic patience. A room that appears in a house is not a wonder — it is a problem. A wall that remembers is not a metaphor — it is a condition. Characters should complain about the magic the way people complain about damp.

**Time**: The author meeting identifies a key question: how to handle time when combining Rushdie's branching, proliferating temporality with Roy's circular, returning-to-the-wound temporality. The solution proposed in the meeting: "friction." The story lives in the friction between the two kinds of time. The narrative should fracture chronologically (Roy) while also accumulating and branching outward (Rushdie). The reader should feel both the circling and the sprawl simultaneously.

### Selected Concepts

**Concept 1: Land subsidence** — The irreversible sinking of ground beneath Indian cities due to over-extraction of groundwater. Parts of Delhi are sinking 51mm per year. The process is irreversible: once sediments compress, they do not spring back even when water returns. This becomes the magical-realist engine of the story. The house doesn't grow rooms (as Rushdie's grandmother's story suggested in the author meeting) — it sinks. Each floor descends as the family's transgressions accumulate. The subsidence is both literal (the neighborhood is genuinely sinking due to borewells) and magically accelerated around this particular house. The family treats it not as wonder but as a maintenance problem — they measure the sinking with a pencil mark on the doorframe, they file complaints with the municipal corporation, they blame the borewells.

**Concept 2: Cadastral survey** — The official measurement and recording of land boundaries in India, a practice dating back centuries but formalized under British colonialism. Cadastral surveys define what belongs to whom on paper, creating official ownership records. The protagonist's father was a cadastral surveyor — a man whose entire profession was drawing lines on maps, defining boundaries of belonging. The irony: a Dalit man whose job was to measure and certify the boundaries of land he could never own. The cadastral maps become a recurring image — official documents that define belonging, that create the illusion that ownership is natural rather than constructed. When the house sinks, the survey maps become inaccurate. The land the family stands on no longer matches the land the government says they stand on. The official boundary is up there; the actual ground is down here.

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

A house in a South Indian town has been sinking for thirty-one years — one centimeter for every month since a forbidden marriage broke the caste line — and now the eldest daughter must decide whether to sell it to a developer (ending the sinking but erasing the family) or let it continue descending into the earth with her father's body still inside, uncremated, sitting upright in a chair in what used to be the ground floor and is now a kind of cellar. The developer has already built around every other holdout; the house is the last nail.

### 2. Protagonist

**Lata Karunakaran**, 34. Eldest daughter. Works as a hydrological engineer for the Thiruvananthapuram municipal corporation, specializing in groundwater monitoring. She maps aquifers for a living — she knows exactly why the earth sinks when you drain it. She has not been home to Muvattupuzha in three years, not since her father stopped answering the phone and her younger brother Sajan stopped returning her calls.

**What she WANTS**: To sell the house, divide the money with Sajan, and be done with it. The developer has offered 14 lakhs. The house is worth maybe 6. The premium is for the location — or rather, for the removal of the house from the location.

**What she NEEDS**: To understand that her father's refusal to leave (and now his refusal to be dead in the conventional sense — sitting up, eyes open, hair still growing) is not stubbornness but the only form of protest available to a man whose profession was drawing other people's boundaries. He is occupying the land. The house is his body is the land is the claim.

**Her flaw**: Lata believes that if she can name a thing precisely enough — measure it, map it, assign it coordinates — she can control it. Her entire career is the application of this belief. She maps groundwater depletion the way her father mapped land boundaries: with instruments, with confidence, with the conviction that what the instruments say is what is real. She cannot accept that some things are real without being measurable, that the house's sinking has a logic that her instruments do not capture, that her father's condition (alive? dead? something the Malayalam language has a word for but the English-language municipal death certificate does not?) is not a problem to be solved but a fact to be lived inside.

**Her wound**: When she was eleven, her mother Devaki — who was Nair, who married down across the caste line to Lata's Ezhava father — walked out of the house during the monsoon of 1995 and didn't come back. Not death. Not dramatic departure. Just a woman walking down a road in the rain with one bag. Lata was in the doorway. She didn't call out. She has told herself for twenty-three years that she didn't call out because she was a child and didn't understand what was happening, but the truth — the wound — is that she understood perfectly and chose not to call out because part of her (the part trained by her Nair grandmother, the Love Laws inscribed on a child's bones) believed her mother was right to leave. The marriage was wrong. The caste line should not have been crossed. Lata has never forgiven herself for this belief, which is why she became someone who draws lines for a living — always on the side of the line that says the line is necessary.

### 3. Structure

**Opening image/scene**: Lata arrives in Muvattupuzha by bus in the late afternoon. The town looks normal from the road — but the family house, when she turns the corner, is wrong. The roofline is at hip height. The front door requires her to descend eight concrete steps that weren't there three years ago — steps her brother Sajan has been building, one every few months, to keep the entrance accessible as the house sinks. From the street, it looks like a house that has been partially buried. The neighbors' houses are level. Only this one descends. Lata stands at the top of the steps and looks down at the front door the way a person looks into a well.

**The inciting disruption**: Inside, Sajan (28, unemployed, lives in the house) informs her that their father has been dead for six weeks — or rather, that he stopped breathing six weeks ago, which is not the same thing. Karunakaran sits in his chair in what used to be the sitting room, which is now the lowest room because the house sinks unevenly — the sitting room corner has dropped an additional 40cm. He is sitting upright. His eyes are open. His hair and fingernails continue to grow. Sajan has been trimming them. On his lap is his old cadastral survey kit — the theodolite, the chain, the ranging rods — arranged with a care that suggests ritual. Sajan has not called the authorities because (a) the death certificate would trigger the sale — the developer has a clause — and (b) because Karunakaran told him, before the breathing stopped, that when he finished sinking, he would have reached the depth where the water used to be, before the borewells took it, and then he would know what to do.

**The escalating middle**: Over three days, Lata tries to manage the situation — calling the municipal corporation, consulting a lawyer about the death certificate, negotiating with the developer's agent (a cheerful man named Pillai who keeps emphasizing the word "opportunity"). Each practical step fails or produces an absurd complication. The death certificate requires a doctor's examination, but the doctor (who comes, who descends the steps, who checks for vitals) cannot in good conscience declare dead a man whose hair is growing. The lawyer says the property survey must be updated before sale, but the surveyor's measurements don't match the land records because the house has sunk below the recorded elevation. The developer's offer, it turns out, is contingent on the house being demolished — but the house is below grade, which means demolition would require excavation, which means environmental clearance, which means a geological survey, which means the government would discover the borewells and the whole neighborhood would face penalties.

Meanwhile, Lata discovers her father's survey notebooks — decades of cadastral work, the official mapping of other people's land. In the margins of the last notebook, mixed in with the professional measurements, are different calculations: the rate of the house's descent, measured daily in pencil. The numbers reveal that the sinking is not uniform. It accelerates during certain events: the 2002 Gujarat riots, the 2004 tsunami, the 2008 Kandhamal violence, the 2019 abrogation of Article 370. The house sinks faster when the nation breaks its own laws. Karunakaran has been tracking this for decades. He has mapped the correlation with the same precision he brought to land boundaries — except this survey has no official status. No one commissioned it. No one will file it. It is the cadastral survey of a nation's transgressions, measured from the body of a house that was never supposed to exist because the marriage that made it was never supposed to happen.

**The crisis point**: Pillai, the developer's agent, arrives with a revised offer: 18 lakhs, cash, if Lata signs this week. He also, casually, reveals something that changes everything: Lata's mother Devaki didn't just leave in 1995. She went to the developer's family — the Nair family that owns the company. She has been living in their compound in Kochi for twenty-three years. Devaki, it turns out, is the one who initiated the development project. She wants the house gone. Not for money — for erasure. She wants the evidence of her transgression removed from the landscape. The sinking house is a monument to her shame, visible from the road, descending year by year like a slow confession. Lata must now decide: sign the paper (which means siding with her mother's erasure, with the Love Laws, with the principle that the marriage was wrong and the house should never have been); or refuse (which means siding with her father's body, with the surveyor's impossible map, with the claim that the ground you stand on is yours even when the ground is sinking).

**The ending**: Lata does not sign. But she does not refuse heroically or triumphantly. She goes down into the lowest room, where Karunakaran sits. She picks up the theodolite — her father's old survey instrument — and she carries it outside, up the eight steps, and sets it up on the street. She takes a measurement. She records the angle, the elevation, the distance. She writes it in the margin of his last notebook, continuing the survey. Then she calls Sajan and tells him to build another step. The house will continue sinking. She is not saving it. She is not selling it. She is measuring it. She is choosing to be the person who records what is happening, even when what is happening cannot be stopped, even when the record will have no official status, even when the surveyor and the surveyed are the same body, the same ground, the same sinking.

The unresolved thread: We never learn what happens when the house reaches the water table. Karunakaran said he would know what to do. Lata does not know. The reader does not know. The house is still sinking.

### 4. Key Scenes

- **The descent**: Lata walks down the eight steps for the first time. The house from inside looks normal — the same furniture, the same calendar on the wall (still showing November 2023), the same kitchen — except the windows at street level now show only the cross-section of earth. Soil and roots where sky used to be. Lata realizes the house hasn't just sunk — the windows have continued to show what was outside when the house was at that elevation. The front-facing window shows the street from 2019. The kitchen window shows the backyard from 2007. Each window is a different year. The house is sinking through time as well as earth.

- **The notebooks**: Lata reads her father's survey notebooks at the kitchen table (the kitchen window showing a mango tree that was cut down in 2011). The margins contain his secret survey — the house's descent correlated with national violence. This is the scene where Rushdie's national-allegory-through-personal-body meets Roy's microscopic attention to domestic space. The prose should accumulate like Rushdie (listing the dates, the events, the corresponding measurements) while staying anchored to Roy's sensory precision (the pencil graphite, the smell of old paper, the way the numbers get smaller as they approach the present).

- **The doctor's visit**: Dr. Prasad descends the steps, examines Karunakaran, and cannot declare him dead. "His hair is growing," she says, as if this settles something. "Dead men's hair does not grow." Lata says, "Dead men's hair does grow. It's a common misconception. The skin dehydrates and retracts, exposing more of the shaft." Dr. Prasad looks at her. "Then let me put it differently. His hair is growing too fast." This scene should have Rushdie's comic energy — the absurdity of bureaucracy confronting the impossible — while grounding the comedy in Roy's bodily specificity (the actual length of the hair, measured; the nails; the angle of the head).

- **The revelation about Devaki**: Pillai sits on the steps eating a banana (he is always eating, always cheerful, always saying "what a wonderful opportunity"). He mentions Devaki the way one mentions weather. Lata's response is not dramatic — it is the quiet collapse of a load-bearing wall. Everything she believed about her mother's departure (that it was chaotic, that it was a breakdown, that it was escape) reorganizes into something worse: it was planned. It was a return. The Love Laws didn't break — they snapped back. This scene should be mostly Roy: the precision of small devastation, the way a single piece of information can rearrange twenty-three years of memory.

- **The measurement**: The final scene. Lata with the theodolite on the street, taking readings. Sajan watches from the top of the steps. The neighbors, who have long ago stopped remarking on the house, glance and look away. Lata's hands on the instrument — the same hands that map aquifers, that chart the underground — are now mapping her own family's descent. The prose should find the "third time" that Rushdie challenged the narrator to find in the author meeting: not branching (Rushdie) and not circular (Roy) but something else. Accumulative. A survey. Each measurement adds to the record without resolving anything. The story ends with a measurement and a pencil mark.

### 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning**: The reader feels dislocation and dark comedy — a woman returning to a house that's been swallowed halfway into the ground, a dead father who won't lie down, a brother who's been living with a corpse and trimming its fingernails. The tone is wry, Rushdian, the absurdity of the situation masking what's underneath.

**Middle**: The comedy gives way to compression and claustrophobia. Each failed attempt to resolve the situation (doctor, lawyer, developer) reveals another layer of entrapment. The house is sinking, but so is Lata — into the family's history, into her own complicity with the Love Laws, into the recognition that her career in measurement has been an elaborate evasion of the unmeasurable. The reader should feel the walls closing in — literally, as the house descends, and emotionally, as Lata's rational defenses erode.

**End**: The reader should feel the particular ache of watching someone choose a form of devotion that will cost them everything and resolve nothing. Lata is not saved. The house is not saved. The father is not resurrected. But something has shifted — Lata has stopped trying to solve the house and started trying to witness it. The reader should feel the weight of ongoing obligation: not the catharsis of a resolved story but the dull, necessary ache of choosing to stay present to something that will not stop.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Author A (Rushdie) — most visible in**: The scene where Lata reads her father's notebooks and discovers the secret survey. The prose should go maximalist here — accumulating dates and events and measurements in long, clause-laden sentences that mirror Rushdie's technique of making personal history into national allegory. Karunakaran's body-as-nation (the house-as-body-as-nation), the comic energy of a man who responded to the Gujarat riots by noting "4.2cm, twelve days, unprecedented" in a surveyor's notebook, the unreliable self-importance of believing your house is connected to History. The narrative voice should also borrow Rushdie's technique of self-correction: "The house began sinking in 1993 — or was it 1994? No, it was definitely 1993, because the Babri Masjid had been demolished the previous December, and the first measurement was taken during the aftermath, which is to say the first measurement was taken during the beginning, which in India are the same thing."

**Author B (Roy) — most visible in**: The scene of Lata's memory of her mother's departure. This should be rendered with Roy's sensory microscopy — the specific rain, the specific bag (blue, vinyl, with one broken zipper), the specific doorway, the specific silence of an eleven-year-old who understood. The compound-word inventions should emerge naturally here: "raindarked," "halfpacked," the mother's "goodbyewalk." The children's-eye perspective (even though Lata is remembering it as an adult, the register should drop into the child's vision) and the devastating precision of Roy's political-intimate fusion: the Love Laws as something the child can feel in her own body, not as something she has read about.

**Work X (The God of Small Things) — most visible in**: The overall structure. The catastrophe is known from the beginning — the house is sinking, the father is dead/not-dead, the marriage was wrong. The story circles the wound, revealing not what happened but how the small violations accumulated. The non-linear chronology: windows showing different years, the notebooks moving backward through time, Lata's memories triggered by objects. The Love Laws as the story's gravitational field — who was allowed to love whom, and what happened when those laws were broken, and how the punishment was not spectacular violence but the slow, ordinary sinking of everything built on the transgression.

**Work Y (Midnight's Children) — most visible in**: The personal-as-national allegory. The house sinks with the nation. Karunakaran's body contains the history of post-Independence India, not through telepathy or midnight-birth, but through the surveyor's measurement — the most bureaucratic, the most boring, the most official form of knowledge, turned into a chronicle of national trauma. The house's windows-into-the-past mirror Saleem Sinai's ability to hear the thoughts of the midnight's children — both are impossible access to the collective, both are treated as conditions to be managed rather than miracles to be celebrated. The narrative excess: this is a story that tries to contain too much (a sinking house, a dead father, a missing mother, the Love Laws, the nation's violence, the water table, the developer, the brother, the survey notebooks) and the excess is the point.

### 7. Title

**Subsidence and Survey**

This title avoids "The," uses a compound form (two nouns joined by "and"), and captures the story's dual engine: the physical phenomenon (subsidence) and the human response (survey/measurement). It echoes the technical language both Lata and her father speak, and it carries the double meaning of "survey" — both the professional act of mapping land and the broader act of looking over, taking stock, witnessing.

Alternative titles considered:
- *Cadastral Lines and Love Laws* (too on-the-nose)
- *Eight Steps Down* (too literal)
- *Pencil Marks on the Doorframe* (too long, too domestic without the political)
- *Groundwater* (too generic, could be any story)
- *How the House Went Under* (too narrative, too Rushdian without the Roy)
