# Story Plan: cnf-braided-001

**Genre**: Creative Nonfiction / Braided-Hermit-Crab Essay
**Combo ID**: cnf-braided-001
**Target Word Count**: 6,800 words (6,500-7,100)
**Risk Card**: None

### Style Sources

**Author A -- John McPhee**: Structural innovation in nonfiction; geological patience; the architecture of the long piece. Long, precise descriptive sentences. Specificity as a form of reverence. The careful accumulation of factual detail until it becomes lyrical. Trust in the reader's patience.

**Author B -- Roxane Gay**: Confessional, vulnerable, politically engaged; the body and culture; accessible intellectual voice. Short declarative sentences that land like blows. The refusal to aestheticize suffering. The body as both subject and site of knowledge.

**Work X -- *Citizen: An American Lyric* (Claudia Rankine)**: Hybrid form that moves between genres, registers, and modes of address. Second person as weapon and mirror. Fragment and accumulation as primary structural tools. The seams between modes are not smoothed -- each shift is a disruption, and the accumulation of disruptions becomes the argument.

**Work Y -- *Underland* (Robert Macfarlane)**: Deep time, underground spaces, the hidden worlds beneath the surface. Descent as a way of knowing. The three tasks of the underland: to shelter what is precious, to yield what is valuable, and to dispose of what is harmful.

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: John McPhee (Author A)
**Source**: *Basin and Range* / *Annals of the Former World*
**Origin**: Various passages, compiled from published excerpts
**Why representative**: Shows McPhee's signature technique of collapsing geological time into human-scale observation, his precise geological vocabulary, and his ability to make scientific description feel like poetry.

> "The poles of the earth have wandered. The equator has apparently moved. The continents, perched on their plates, are thought to have been carried so very far and to be going in so many directions that it seems an act of almost pure hubris to assert that some landmark of our world is fixed at 73 degrees 57 minutes and 53 seconds west longitude and 40 degrees 51 minutes and 14 seconds north latitude -- a temporary description, at any rate, as if for a boat on the sea."

> "As we moved along besides the screaming trucks, we were averaging about ten thousand years per step."

> "Human time, regarded in the perspective of geologic time, is much too thin to be observed: the mark invisible at the end of a ruler."

> "Geology was called a descriptive science, and with its pitted outwash plains and drowned rivers, its hanging tributaries and starved coastlines, it was nothing if not descriptive. It was a fountain of metaphor -- of isostatic adjustments and degraded channels, of angular unconformities and shifting divides, of rootless mountains and bitter lakes... Geologists communicated in English; and they could name things in a manner that sent shivers through the bones."

> "The summit of Mount Everest is marine limestone."

**Voice notes for writer**: McPhee's sentences are long, patient, structurally elaborate but never ornamental. He earns his length. He trusts the specific noun -- "dolomite" over "ancient stone," "Marcellus Shale" over "the deep past." His humor is dry, often embedded in juxtaposition (screaming trucks / ten thousand years). He never announces what's interesting -- he describes until the interest is self-evident.

### Sample 2: Roxane Gay (Author B)
**Source**: *Hunger: A Memoir of (My) Body*
**Origin**: Opening chapters and widely excerpted passages
**Why representative**: Shows Gay's confessional directness, her short declarative sentences, the way she makes the body both subject and argument, and her refusal to let vulnerability become performance.

> "I ate and ate and ate in the hopes that if I made myself big, my body would be safe. I buried the girl I was because she ran into all kinds of trouble. I tried to erase every memory of her, but she is still there, somewhere."

> "I was trapped in my body, one that I barely recognized or understood, but at least I was safe."

> "This is a book about my body, about my hunger, and ultimately, this is a book about disappearing and being lost and wanting so very much, wanting to be seen and understood."

> "Writing about the body is a very vulnerable thing. I just didn't want to do it."

**Voice notes for writer**: Gay's sentences are short, blunt, rhythmic. She repeats words ("ate and ate and ate") for accumulative force. She does not metaphorize the body -- she states what happened to it. Her vulnerability is not performed; it is the cost of precision. She acknowledges the difficulty of her own project without retreating from it. Contrasts with McPhee's patience: Gay is urgent, present-tense in feeling even when past-tense in grammar.

### Sample 3: Claudia Rankine (Work X -- *Citizen: An American Lyric*)
**Source**: *Citizen: An American Lyric*
**Origin**: Published excerpts from Sections I and V
**Why representative**: Shows Rankine's use of second person as structural weapon, the shift between registers without transition, and the way fragment accumulates into argument.

> "The rain this morning pours from the gutters and everywhere else it is lost in the trees. You need your glasses to single out what you know is there because doubt is inexplicable; you put on your glasses."

> "Because of your elite status from a year's worth of travel, you have already settled into your window seat on United Airlines, when the girl and her mother arrive at your row."

> "And when the woman with the multiple degrees says, I didn't know Black women could get cancer, instinctively you take two steps back though all urgency leaves the possibility of any kind of relationship as you realize nowhere is where you will get from here."

**Voice notes for writer**: Rankine collapses distance between reader and subject using "you." The second person is not hypothetical -- it is documentary. Her sentences can be long and sinuous or short and percussive. She moves between lyric and documentary without signaling the transition, and that absence of signal is itself the argument: that the shift from ordinary life to violation is not announced. The fragment is a unit of meaning, not a broken sentence.

### Sample 4: Robert Macfarlane (Work Y -- *Underland*)
**Source**: *Underland: A Deep Time Journey*
**Origin**: Published excerpts and Goodreads quotes
**Why representative**: Shows Macfarlane's poetic-scientific prose, his feel for descent and underground spaces, his handling of deep time as both concept and sensation.

> "The way into the underland is through the riven trunk of an old ash tree... Near the ash's base its trunk splits into a rough rift, just wide enough that a person might slip into the tree's hollow heart -- and there drop into the dark space that opens below."

> "Something that has come from so deep down in time that it has lost all colour, and we are dancing and swearing and shouting, appalled and thrilled to have seen this repulsive, exquisite thing rise up that should never have surfaced, this star-dropped berg-surge that has taken three minutes and 100,000 years to conclude."

> "Time moves differently here in the underland. It thickens, pools, flows, rushes, slows."

> "We all carry trace fossils within us -- the marks that the dead and the missed leave behind."

> "We are part mineral beings too -- our teeth are reefs, our bones are stones."

> "The same three tasks recur across cultures and epochs: to shelter what is precious, to yield what is valuable, and to dispose of what is harmful."

> "What will survive of us is plastic, swine bones and lead-207, the stable isotope at the end of the uranium-235 decay chain."

> "Time isn't deep, it is always already all around us. The past ghosts us, lies all about us less as layers, more as drift."

**Voice notes for writer**: Macfarlane's prose is more overtly lyrical than McPhee's -- he lets the metaphor surface where McPhee keeps it submerged. He is precise about the sensory experience of underground spaces (texture, temperature, claustrophobia, wonder). His deep time passages mix scientific terminology with emotional register. His weakness (identified by Gay in the meeting) is managed vulnerability -- he descends into physical spaces more readily than into personal ones. The essay should use his style for descent passages but push beyond his emotional remove.

### Selected Concepts

**1. Ancient Roman units of measurement**
The Romans measured the earth using the body. The *digitus* (finger-width, 18.5mm) was the smallest unit. Sixteen *digiti* made a *pes* (foot). The *cubitum* was elbow-to-fingertip. The *uncia* -- from which we get "inch" and "ounce" -- was 1/12 of a foot.

**Integration**: This becomes a motif in the personal strands of the essay. The narrator, undergoing a medical procedure (a bone density scan, specifically), reflects on how all measurement once began with the body. The technician positions her arm, measures her wrist, her hip, her spine -- and the narrator thinks about the *digitus*, the *uncia*, the ancient assumption that the body is the baseline from which all distance is calculated. This connects to Gay's insistence that the body is not metaphor but ground truth, and to the geological theme: we measure the earth's layers the same way we once measured everything, starting from ourselves and extending outward. The concept also creates a structural echo -- the essay's sections can be titled with measurement terms that slide between geological and anatomical registers.

**2. Balcones Canyonlands / Balcones Fault Zone**
The Balcones Fault Zone runs through central Texas -- a series of en echelon faults where the Edwards Plateau dropped relative to the Gulf Coastal Plain. Displacement along the zone is 1,200-1,600 feet. The rock is Cretaceous limestone formed in shallow warm seas, now riddled with karst features: sinkholes, springs, underground rivers. The fault zone marks where Texas literally split.

**Integration**: This becomes the specific geological terrain of the essay. Instead of a generic or metaphorical landscape, the narrator visits the Balcones Fault Zone -- drives along the fault line, walks the exposed limestone, descends into a karst sinkhole. The specificity grounds the geological strands in McPhee's tradition (actual formations, actual ages, actual mineral compositions). The fault zone also provides structural metaphor that earns its place: a fault is a fracture along which one side has moved relative to the other. The narrator's personal material -- her relationship to her own body, a medical diagnosis, the gap between who she was and who she became -- maps onto the geology not as analogy but as structural echo. The Cretaceous limestone, deposited in warm shallow seas 100 million years ago, is now exposed by the fault. What was buried is now visible. What was level is now displaced.

## 1. Premise

A woman drives to central Texas to see the Balcones Fault Zone -- a geological fracture where one side of the earth dropped 1,200 feet relative to the other. She has recently received results from a bone density scan that revealed early osteopenia: her bones are thinning, losing mineral, becoming less themselves. The essay braids three strands -- a geological survey of the fault zone's stratigraphic column, the narrator's bodily experience of being measured and found diminishing, and lyric fragments in second person that implicate the reader in the act of looking beneath surfaces. The borrowed form is a geological field report, but the report keeps breaking. The question the essay asks: when you drill down through the layers of a place and a body, do you eventually reach bedrock, or do you discover that what you thought was solid is just another surface, slower than the others?

## 2. Protagonist / Narrator

- **Nia Okafor**, 41, a science journalist who writes about geology for a regional magazine. She is not a geologist -- she is a translator, someone who makes rock legible to people who will never touch it. Half-Nigerian, half-Irish-American, raised in New Jersey, now living in Austin, Texas. She moved to Texas six years ago after her marriage ended. She has not been back to New Jersey. She has not told her mother about the bone density results.
- **What she WANTS**: To write a definitive piece about the Balcones Fault Zone -- a McPhee-level geological essay that establishes her as more than a magazine writer. She wants to be taken seriously in the long form.
- **What she NEEDS**: To stop translating herself into geological metaphor and actually reckon with the body she's in -- its diagnosis, its history, the summer she was seventeen and stopped eating for three months because a boy she trusted told her she was too much.
- **Her flaw**: She believes precision is a form of courage. She uses geological vocabulary to describe her own experience because she thinks naming something accurately is the same as facing it. It is not. The accurate name for her condition is "osteopenia." The accurate name for what happened to her at seventeen is something she has not yet said aloud in the essay.

## 3. Structure

The essay borrows the form of a **geological field report / stratigraphic column** -- sections headed by layer names, depths, geological ages. But the form degrades as the essay descends. Upper layers are precise, scientific, patient (McPhee mode). Middle layers begin to buckle as personal material intrudes. Lower layers fragment into lyric and second person (Rankine mode). The deepest layer refuses to complete its description.

### Opening Image / Hook
The essay opens mid-report, clinical and precise: a description of the Glen Rose Formation, Lower Cretaceous, 112 million years old, exposed along a road cut on Ranch Road 1431 west of Austin. The language is McPhee-beautiful: exact mineral names, fossil identifications, the patience of someone who has learned to read rock. But in the second paragraph, without transition, a sentence appears: "The technician asks you to hold still." Then back to geology. The reader doesn't know yet what's happening. The disruption is tiny but felt.

### Inciting Disruption
Section 3 of the essay ("Edwards Formation, 97-100 Ma, dense gray limestone, porosity 15-30%") -- the geological description of karst porosity triggers the personal strand. Porosity: the percentage of a rock that is empty space. The narrator's bones have a porosity problem. Her T-score came back at -1.8. The medical explanation intrudes into the geological one, and for the first time the reader understands that the essay is not only about the earth. The narrator describes her bone density scan in clinical language that mirrors the geological precision: the DEXA machine, the X-ray attenuation coefficient, the way the technician measured her left hip, her lumbar spine, her distal radius. The body measured in *digiti* and *unciae*. The body as a stratigraphic column.

### Escalating Middle
The braids tighten. The geological strand continues its descent through the Balcones Fault Zone's column -- Walnut Formation, Comanche Peak, the moment of the fault itself (the unconformity where 40 million years of rock are missing because they were uplifted and eroded away). At the fault unconformity, the personal strand hits its own unconformity: the gap in the narrator's story. She was seventeen. She stopped eating. She does not say why, not yet. The geological gap and the personal gap sit side by side without commentary. The second-person fragments intensify: "You are in the waiting room. You are holding a magazine you have not opened. You are the only body here that is not behind a door." These fragments arrive without warning, disrupting the geological patience.

### Crisis Point
The narrator descends into an actual karst sinkhole near the fault zone -- a physical descent, Macfarlane-mode, with the sensory detail of underground spaces: the temperature drop, the smell of wet limestone, the way sound changes in enclosed rock. She reaches a point where the passage narrows and she cannot go further. This is both literal (the cave passage is too tight) and structural (the essay has reached the layer it cannot describe). Here, at maximum depth, the personal strand and the geological strand collide: the narrator, crouched in Cretaceous limestone formed in a sea that no longer exists, finally names what happened at seventeen. Not as metaphor. Not as geology. In Gay-mode: short, blunt, declarative. A boy. A car. Three months of not eating because if she could disappear her body, maybe what happened to her body would also disappear. The bones remember what the mind tries to erode.

### Ending
The narrator climbs back out. The essay's final section is headed "Surface" -- but unlike the earlier sections, it has no geological data. No formation name, no age, no mineral composition. Just the narrator standing at the lip of the sinkhole, back in Texas sunlight, looking at the exposed fault scarp where one side of the earth sits 1,200 feet lower than the other. She calls her mother. The call goes to voicemail. She does not leave a message. She does not know what she would say. The geological column is complete except for the surface layer, which the essay refuses to fill. An unconformity in the text itself: a gap the reader can feel but the writer will not close.

**What is NOT resolved**: Whether Nia tells her mother. Whether the essay she's writing -- this essay -- is the "definitive piece" she wanted or something else entirely. Whether naming the thing at seventeen changes anything. The geological column remains incomplete at the top. The surface layer has no data.

## 4. Key Scenes / Sections

1. **Road cut on Ranch Road 1431**: The narrator stops her car and examines the exposed Glen Rose Formation. She identifies fossil rudists (reef-building clams), notes the alternating beds of limestone and marl. McPhee precision at its fullest. But she is also aware of her own body against the rock -- her hand on the outcrop, the heat of the limestone, the way the fossil shapes press into her palm. The first hint that the essay will be about contact between body and earth.

2. **The bone density scan (rendered in fragments across multiple sections)**: Not told in one block but distributed through the essay, appearing at unconformities in the geological column. The DEXA machine. The technician's instructions. The way the results came printed on a graph that looked, Nia notes, like a stratigraphic column turned sideways -- density plotted against age, her data point falling in the yellow zone between normal and osteoporotic. The Romans measured everything from the body outward. Now the machines measure inward.

3. **The fault scarp at Balcones**: Nia stands where the Edwards Plateau drops away. She describes the displacement -- not gradually, not over millions of years, but in a single catastrophic adjustment. One side moved. The other didn't. The landscape that had been continuous was now severed. The narrator does not comment on the metaphor. She doesn't need to. The reader already knows.

4. **The sinkhole descent**: The physical and emotional center of the essay. Nia descends into a karst sinkhole (legally -- she has a guide, a Texas caver named Dolores who appears only in this section). The underground passage is Macfarlane territory: the change in light, the drip of water through limestone that has been filtering for 10,000 years, the fossils embedded in the walls (brachiopods, crinoid stems, the shadows of creatures that lived in the Cretaceous sea). At the narrowest point, the cave demands that Nia flatten her body against the rock. She feels her bones against stone that is made of bones -- the calcium carbonate of ancient shells. Her bones, thinning. Their bones, turned to rock. The temporal vertigo triggers the confession.

5. **The second-person interlude (a standalone section near the end)**: A Rankine-mode passage, 300-400 words, entirely in second person. You are in the doctor's office. You are seventeen. You are forty-one. You are neither of these. The passage collapses the temporal distance between the narrator's adolescence and her present, just as the geological column collapses the distance between the Cretaceous and now. This section should feel like a rupture -- the essay's controlled form breaking open.

## 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Opening**: Intellectual pleasure. The reader enters the essay as they'd enter a McPhee piece -- drawn by the beauty of geological description, the music of formation names, the satisfaction of precise observation. A sense of being in expert hands.

**Early middle**: Unease. The personal fragments begin to intrude, and the reader feels the essay's form destabilizing. Something is being withheld. The geological patience, which was pleasurable, starts to feel like avoidance.

**Deep middle**: Recognition. The reader understands that the geological essay is a shell the narrator is living inside -- a hermit crab essay in the most literal sense. The precision was never just precision. It was a strategy for approaching something the narrator could not approach directly.

**Crisis**: Exposed. The narrator's confession in the sinkhole is not cathartic. It is not a breakthrough. It is a person crouched in the dark saying something true without knowing if it will change anything. The reader should feel the vertigo of a private disclosure -- the discomfort of witnessing, the inability to look away, the sense that the essay has crossed into territory from which it cannot retreat.

**Ending**: Unsettled, but not desolate. The narrator is back on the surface, in sunlight, and the essay refuses its own completion. The reader is left with the feeling of a column that is missing its top layer -- a gap that is not silence but the evidence of something the writer has not yet found words for. The phone call that goes to voicemail. The surface that remains undescribed.

## 6. Formula Integration

**John McPhee (Author A)** -- Most visible in: **Sections 1-3 and the road cut scene.** The opening geological descriptions should feel like they could have been lifted from *Basin and Range*. Long, patient, structurally elaborate sentences. Exact formation names and ages. The trick of collapsing deep time into pedestrian scale ("ten thousand years per step"). McPhee's dry humor -- the screaming trucks on Ranch Road 1431 contrasted with the 112-million-year-old limestone. His trust in the specific noun: "rudist bivalve" not "ancient shell," "Glen Rose Formation" not "old rock." The writer should emulate McPhee's sentence architecture: the way a McPhee sentence begins in one place and arrives, after several carefully managed subordinate clauses, somewhere unexpected.

**Roxane Gay (Author B)** -- Most visible in: **The sinkhole confession and the personal fragments throughout.** Gay's voice should surface whenever the narrator stops translating her body into metaphor. Short, blunt, declarative sentences. "I stopped eating. I wanted to disappear. I did not disappear." The refusal to aestheticize what happened. Gay's confessional directness -- not performed vulnerability, but the specific, costly act of saying what happened in plain language. Also: the political dimension. The narrator's body is not just a site of personal history but a body in a world that has opinions about it -- a body in a doctor's office, a body being measured, a body that is always being told what it should be.

**Claudia Rankine / *Citizen* (Work X)** -- Most visible in: **The second-person interludes and the essay's structural grammar.** The second-person passages should mirror Rankine's technique: "you" as both the narrator and the reader, collapsing the distance. The shifts between registers should not be announced -- no transitional sentences, no "which reminds me of." The essay moves from geological description to personal confession to second-person lyric the way *Citizen* moves between prose, poetry, and image: each shift is a small disruption, and the disruptions accumulate into the essay's argument. Also: the hybrid form itself. The essay is part geological survey, part memoir, part lyric poem. It refuses to be one thing.

**Robert Macfarlane / *Underland* (Work Y)** -- Most visible in: **The sinkhole descent and the essay's relationship to depth and deep time.** The descent into the karst sinkhole should feel Macfarlane-inflected: the sensory detail of underground spaces, the way sound and light change, the temporality of underground ("time moves differently here"). Macfarlane's three tasks of the underland -- to shelter what is precious, to yield what is valuable, to dispose of what is harmful -- should echo through the essay without being stated. The narrator is doing all three: sheltering the memory of herself at seventeen (precious), yielding the medical information about her bones (valuable), and trying to dispose of the shame she has carried (harmful). Also: Macfarlane's idea that "we all carry trace fossils within us" should be literalized through the bone density scan -- the narrator's bones are a geological record, their mineral content a core sample of her life.

## 7. Title

**"Drilling Down to Where the Body Starts"**

This title does not start with "The." It echoes the geological drilling/borehole imagery. "Where the body starts" carries a double meaning: the depth at which the essay's personal subject begins, and the place where measurement originates (the body as the ancient baseline for all units). It sounds like something a geologist might say and also something a memoirist might say. It does not duplicate the structure of any existing title in the collection.

Alternative titles considered:
- "Core Sample with Unconformity" (too clever, too much like a workshop title)
- "Porosity" (too vague, single-word geological term lacks the essay's tension)
- "Measuring Inward" (decent but bland)
- "Fault and Formation" (paired nouns already common in the collection: "Salt and Mortar," "Peat and Testimony," etc.)

## Section-by-Section Outline (for writer reference)

The essay is structured as a geological field report with sections headed by stratigraphic units. The headers degrade as the essay descends.

1. **GLEN ROSE FORMATION | Lower Cretaceous, Aptian-Albian, 112 Ma | Alternating limestone and marl, rudist reef facies, thickness 300-500 ft**
   - Full McPhee geological description. Road cut scene. Narrator introduces herself through her relationship to rock. First micro-intrusion: "The technician asks you to hold still."

2. **EDWARDS FORMATION | Upper Albian to Cenomanian, 97-100 Ma | Dense gray limestone, karst porosity 15-30%**
   - Geological description of karst -- how limestone dissolves, how empty space forms inside solid rock. Porosity triggers the bone density strand. First sustained personal passage: the DEXA scan, the T-score, the graph. Roman measurement units: the body as baseline.

3. **WALNUT FORMATION | Lower Cretaceous, Albian, ~105 Ma | Nodular limestone with oyster biostromes**
   - Brief geological section. Narrator at the fault scarp. Description of the Balcones Fault displacement. Personal strand: the gap in the narrator's story. She was seventeen. She circles it. Second-person fragment: "You are in the waiting room."

4. **COMANCHE PEAK FORMATION | Lower Cretaceous, Albian, ~105-100 Ma | Massive limestone, abundant echinoids**
   - Geological description thins. Personal strand grows. Narrator drives along the fault, thinking about displacement -- how one side of a landscape can shift relative to the other without the surface showing it. The marriage. The move to Texas. The body she arrived in. The body she has now.

5. **UNCONFORMITY | ~40 million years absent | No record**
   - The essay's structural center. This section has almost no geological content -- just the header noting the gap. Instead: the personal unconformity. Narrator approaches but does not yet name what happened at seventeen. The absence is compositional.

6. **BASEMENT ROCK | Precambrian, >1 Ga | Metamorphic gneiss, no fossils**
   - The sinkhole descent. Macfarlane-mode sensory detail. Narrator with Dolores the caver. The passage narrows. The confession. Gay-mode: plain, declarative, blunt. Bones against bones.

7. **[second-person interlude, no geological header]**
   - Rankine-mode. 300-400 words. You at seventeen, you at forty-one, the collapse of temporal distance. The essay's form is fully broken now.

8. **SURFACE | Holocene, present | [no data]**
   - Narrator back in sunlight. The phone call. The voicemail. The column left incomplete. The surface layer the essay refuses to describe.
