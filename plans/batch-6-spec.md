## Work 1: dystopian/techno-dystopia

- **Title**: Kindness Engine
- **Slug**: kindness-engine
- **AuthorA**: Aldous Huxley (style contribution: satirical intellectualism, pleasure-as-control, the dystopia that feels like paradise)
- **AuthorB**: Octavia Butler (style contribution: visceral survival prose, unflinching power dynamics, the body as political territory)
- **WorkX**: *Minority Report* by Philip K. Dick (structural contribution: a predictive system that creates the crimes it claims to prevent; the procedural unraveling of an algorithm's logic)
- **WorkY**: *Brave New World* by Aldous Huxley (thematic contribution: engineered happiness, consent manufactured through comfort, the horror of a world where no one wants to be free)
- **Target word count**: 5200 (window: 4900-5500)
- **Reading time**: 21 min
- **Risk card**: STORY-AS-DOCUMENT
- **Premise**: A municipality's AI-driven "Kindness Engine" predicts antisocial behavior and intervenes with mandatory wellness protocols before any offense occurs. The story takes the form of official documents -- intake assessments, compliance reports, appeal transcripts -- that trace a social worker's growing realization that the system's definition of kindness has become indistinguishable from coercion. The documents tell one story; the silences between them tell another.
- **Tags**: [techno-dystopia, predictive-policing, algorithmic-control, wellness-as-coercion, found-documents, consent, surveillance, social-work]
- **Formula summary**: Huxley's satirical precision about pleasure-as-control fused with Butler's visceral depiction of bodies under institutional power. Minority Report provides the architecture of a predictive system unraveling from within; Brave New World provides the thematic horror of a populace that has been made to love its own subjugation. The story-as-document form means the reader must reconstruct the human cost from bureaucratic language.

## Work 2: creative-nonfiction/literary-journalism

- **Title**: Sixty Versions of Juarez
- **Slug**: sixty-versions-of-juarez
- **AuthorA**: Gay Talese (style contribution: novelistic scene-setting, immersive observation, the journalist disappearing into the reported world)
- **AuthorB**: Rebecca Solnit (style contribution: digressive, associative feminist essay, walking and thinking as method, structural wandering that arrives somewhere unexpected)
- **WorkX**: *In Cold Blood* by Truman Capote (structural contribution: the true-crime narrative as literary architecture, multiple perspectives converging on a single event, the reporter's impossible proximity)
- **WorkY**: *The Journalist and the Murderer* by Janet Malcolm (thematic contribution: the moral corruption inherent in telling someone else's story, the betrayal at the heart of all journalism, the writer as con artist)
- **Target word count**: 3800 (window: 3500-4100)
- **Reading time**: 15 min
- **Risk card**: none
- **Premise**: A journalist returns to a small Texas border city to write about the demolition of its last independent newspaper building, only to find that every person she interviews remembers a different city -- and that her own early career there was built on a story she got wrong. The piece braids reportage about the building's final days with the journalist's reckoning with her younger self's certainties, asking whether any act of journalism can be anything other than a selective betrayal.
- **Tags**: [literary-journalism, border-city, newspapers, memory, journalism-ethics, demolition, texas, self-reckoning]
- **Formula summary**: Talese's immersive, novelistic scene-building fused with Solnit's associative, digressive essayistic intelligence. In Cold Blood provides the structural blueprint of multiple perspectives converging on a single event rendered with literary density; The Journalist and the Murderer provides the thematic DNA of journalism's inherent moral compromise and the reporter's complicity in shaping truth.

## Work 3: humor-satire/picaresque

- **Title**: Liability Abroad
- **Slug**: liability-abroad
- **AuthorA**: Oscar Wilde (style contribution: epigrammatic wit, paradox as worldview, the comedy of surfaces and performances)
- **AuthorB**: David Sedaris (style contribution: self-deprecating memoir-comedy, family dysfunction mined for laughs, precise comic timing in conversational voice)
- **WorkX**: *A Confederacy of Dunces* by John Kennedy Toole (structural contribution: a magnificently deluded protagonist careening through a series of comic disasters, each scheme more catastrophically ill-conceived than the last)
- **WorkY**: *The Importance of Being Earnest* by Oscar Wilde (thematic contribution: the absurdity of social performance, identity as costume, the liberating terror of being found out)
- **Target word count**: 3200 (window: 2900-3500)
- **Reading time**: 13 min
- **Risk card**: none
- **Premise**: A middling British cultural attache, posted to a fictional Eastern European republic to organize a literary festival, discovers that the celebrated local author she is meant to honor does not exist -- he is a collective pseudonym maintained by an entire village. Rather than report the fraud, she decides to impersonate the author at the festival's keynote, setting off a chain of increasingly absurd deceptions as the village scrambles to keep the fiction alive and she discovers she is better at being someone else than she ever was at being herself.
- **Tags**: [picaresque, cultural-diplomacy, literary-fraud, identity, farce, eastern-europe, performance, comic-misadventure]
- **Formula summary**: Wilde's epigrammatic wit and delight in performative identity fused with Sedaris's self-deprecating, confessional comic voice. A Confederacy of Dunces provides the structural engine of a deluded protagonist stumbling through escalating disasters of their own making; The Importance of Being Earnest provides the thematic architecture of identity as performance, where the fake self turns out to be more real than the original.

## Work 4: crime-noir/tartan-noir

- **Title**: Peat and Testimony
- **Slug**: peat-and-testimony
- **AuthorA**: Derek Raymond (style contribution: unflinching British noir, existential despair, violence rendered without glamour or redemption)
- **AuthorB**: Chester Himes (style contribution: absurdist violence, institutional racism exposed through crime narrative, rage and dark humor coexisting)
- **WorkX**: *Laidlaw* by William McIlvanney (structural contribution: the foundational tartan noir -- a Glasgow detective investigating a murder while confronting the city's class violence, institutional rot, and his own contradictions)
- **WorkY**: *In a Lonely Place* by Dorothy B. Hughes (thematic contribution: the investigation that turns inward, the detective who discovers complicity, the noir of realizing you are closer to the criminal than to justice)
- **Target word count**: 5700 (window: 5400-6000)
- **Reading time**: 23 min
- **Risk card**: REVERSE CHRONOLOGY
- **Premise**: A police sergeant in a decaying Scottish port town investigates the death of a migrant worker found in a disused peat processing plant. The story moves backward in time -- from the body's discovery through the institutional indifference that made the death inevitable, the sergeant's own small compromises, and finally to the moment the dead man first arrived in the town, full of the future he would not get. Each step backward reveals not whodunit but who allowed it.
- **Tags**: [tartan-noir, scotland, migrant-workers, institutional-corruption, reverse-chronology, class-violence, peat-industry, complicity]
- **Formula summary**: Derek Raymond's unflinching, existentially bleak British noir fused with Chester Himes's ability to render institutional racism through crime narrative with coexisting rage and dark absurdist humor. Laidlaw provides the structural DNA of Scottish crime fiction -- class consciousness, urban decay, a detective at war with his own institution; In a Lonely Place provides the thematic revelation that the investigator is implicated in what he investigates. The reverse chronology means the reader understands consequences before causes, and complicity before innocence.

## Work 5: horror/body-horror

- **Title**: Giving Graciously
- **Slug**: giving-graciously
- **AuthorA**: Carmen Maria Machado (style contribution: surreal, formally inventive horror, the body as site of terror and desire, fairy-tale structures lurking beneath contemporary surfaces)
- **AuthorB**: Thomas Ligotti (style contribution: philosophical pessimism rendered as fiction, nightmarish corporate/institutional settings, anti-natalist dread, Lovecraft meets Schopenhauer)
- **WorkX**: *The Vegetarian* by Han Kang (structural contribution: a character's radical bodily transformation as refusal of the social contract, three perspectives on one person's dissolution, the body as protest)
- **WorkY**: *Annihilation* by Jeff VanderMeer (thematic contribution: biological transformation as encounter with the unknowable, mutation without malice, the body becoming something that is not wrong but is no longer human)
- **Target word count**: 3400 (window: 3100-3700)
- **Reading time**: 14 min
- **Risk card**: none
- **Premise**: An employee at a corporate organ-sharing startup discovers that the company's flagship "reciprocal donation" program -- in which employees voluntarily donate tissue samples in exchange for equity -- has begun triggering biological changes that the donors cannot reverse and the company cannot explain. Her own body is changing in ways that feel less like illness and more like becoming something she was always supposed to be, which is the most frightening possibility of all.
- **Tags**: [body-horror, corporate-horror, organ-donation, biological-transformation, biotech, consent, identity, mutation]
- **Formula summary**: Machado's surreal, body-centered horror and fairy-tale undertow fused with Ligotti's nightmarish institutional pessimism and the dread of consciousness trapped in a system it cannot escape. The Vegetarian provides the structural architecture of radical bodily transformation as refusal -- a character dissolving out of the social contract through the flesh; Annihilation provides the thematic DNA of mutation without malice, where becoming something inhuman is not punishment but revelation, and the horror is that it might be welcome.
