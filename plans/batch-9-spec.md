# Batch 9 Specification

## Overview
5 works targeting the 3 genres at 5 works (gothic-fiction, mystery-thriller, romance) plus creative-nonfiction and science-fiction.

## Work 1: goth-queergothic-001
- **Genre**: gothic-fiction
- **Subgenre**: queer-gothic
- **Worker**: 1
- **AuthorA**: Emily Brontë (wild elemental passion; the moors as emotional landscape; love as destruction)
- **AuthorB**: Sarah Waters (Victorian/period settings; queer narratives; Dickensian plotting with modern sensibility)
- **WorkX**: *The Picture of Dorian Gray* by Oscar Wilde (the portrait/double as structural conceit; split between public self and hidden truth; aesthetic philosophy as narrative frame)
- **WorkY**: *Carmilla* by Sheridan Le Fanu (predatory desire disguised as intimacy; the female vampire as queer figure; isolation enabling transgression; the guest who devours the host)
- **Target word count**: 5,800 (reading time: 23 min)
- **Risk card**: None
- **Direction**: Two women in a remote country house, 1890s. One is the new companion hired after the other's breakdown. The house shifts — rooms that can't be found again, a locked gallery with an inexplicable portrait. A cache of letters suggests the woman may not be who the household claims. The gothic machinery maps onto queer subtext: what's hidden, what's revealed, what the house refuses to release. Brontë's elemental passion meets Waters' period precision. Dorian Gray's portrait conceit provides structure; Carmilla's predatory intimacy gives the relationship its dangerous edge. No tidy resolution.

## Work 2: mys-espionage-001
- **Genre**: mystery-thriller
- **Subgenre**: espionage-spy-thriller
- **Worker**: 2
- **AuthorA**: John le Carré (dense bureaucratic realism; moral exhaustion of espionage; layered literary prose)
- **AuthorB**: Patricia Highsmith (cold clinical suspense; moral ambiguity; the banality of evil; unsettling intimacy)
- **WorkX**: *Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy* by John le Carré (institutional investigation; the mole hunt; loyalty tested by betrayal from within; debriefing as narrative mode)
- **WorkY**: *The Remains of the Day* by Kazuo Ishiguro (a life spent in service to the wrong cause; dignity as self-deception; the reckoning that comes too late)
- **Target word count**: 6,200 (reading time: 25 min)
- **Risk card**: #10 — THE PROTAGONIST IS WRONG (protagonist's worldview is fundamentally flawed; revealed through consequences, not epiphany)
- **Direction**: A retired intelligence officer reviews a cold case file from decades ago. As they reconstruct events from documents and memory fragments, the reader gradually understands what the protagonist cannot: the operation succeeded exactly as designed, and they were its instrument, not its author. Le Carré's bureaucratic prose; Highsmith's clinical moral gaze. Tinker Tailor's institutional investigation structure meets Remains of the Day's wasted devotion. Protagonist remains sympathetic as the reader grasps the truth first. No confession scene, no dramatic reveal — just accumulating weight.

## Work 3: rom-paranormal-001
- **Genre**: romance
- **Subgenre**: paranormal-romance
- **Worker**: 3
- **AuthorA**: Beverly Jenkins (rich historical context; meticulous cultural detail; community and pride; emotional directness)
- **AuthorB**: Casey McQuiston (queer rom-com voice; exuberant optimism; pop-culture fluency; millennial sensibility)
- **WorkX**: *Interview with the Vampire* by Anne Rice (confession/interview frame; immortality as burden; intimate bond between vampire and human; sensuality laced with melancholy)
- **WorkY**: *The Time Traveler's Wife* by Audrey Niffenegger (love across temporal dislocation; one partner aging while the other doesn't; logistics of impossible devotion; domesticity strained by the supernatural)
- **Target word count**: 4,900 (reading time: 20 min)
- **Risk card**: None
- **Direction**: Contemporary setting. A Black woman in her 30s runs a community bookstore in a gentrifying neighborhood. Her new regular customer is charming, funny, suspiciously knowledgeable about obscure local history, and does not age. She is building roots, legacy, community; he has watched communities rise and fall for centuries and doesn't trust permanence. Jenkins' warmth and community-centered storytelling meets McQuiston's witty voice. Interview with the Vampire frame (confession, centuries' weight) provides structure. Time Traveler's Wife themes (love across incompatible timescales) give the emotional engine. HFN ending — not by solving the impossible, but by choosing love despite it.

## Work 4: cnf-travel-001
- **Genre**: creative-nonfiction
- **Subgenre**: travel-writing
- **Worker**: 4
- **AuthorA**: Joan Didion (cool precise observation; the sentence as scalpel; recursive controlled prose)
- **AuthorB**: Hunter S. Thompson (gonzo first-person; the reporter as chaotic protagonist; excess as method)
- **WorkX**: *In Patagonia* by Bruce Chatwin (mosaic travelogue structure; vignettes accumulating into landscape portrait; digression as narrative strategy; the journey that keeps fragmenting)
- **WorkY**: *The Rings of Saturn* by W.G. Sebald (walking and melancholy; landscapes as palimpsests of history; decay observed with clinical tenderness; the traveler as witness to entropy)
- **Target word count**: 5,100 (reading time: 20 min)
- **Risk card**: #3 — STORY-AS-DOCUMENT (takes the form of a non-literary document — annotated travel receipts, boarding passes, and hotel bills)
- **Direction**: A narrator traveling alone through a region in decline — an industrial coast, rust belt corridor, or depopulating rural zone. Structured as annotated receipts and travel ephemera: a gas station receipt becomes meditation on the town; a hotel bill annotates overheard conversation; a highway toll stub triggers historical digression. Didion's surgical precision meets Thompson's first-person chaos. Chatwin's mosaic structure gives the form; Sebald's landscape-as-entropy gives philosophical underpinning. The document conceit means the essay never settles into conventional narrative.

## Work 5: sf-softsf-001
- **Genre**: science-fiction
- **Subgenre**: soft-sf-social-sf
- **Worker**: 5
- **AuthorA**: Ursula K. Le Guin (anthropological worldbuilding; lyrical philosophical prose; gender and social experimentation)
- **AuthorB**: Becky Chambers (warm character-focused SF; found-family dynamics; hopeful futures; cozy SF)
- **WorkX**: *The Left Hand of Darkness* by Ursula K. Le Guin (anthropological field report as narrative frame; society organized around radically different social principle; outsider-observer whose certainties crumble)
- **WorkY**: *Parable of the Sower* by Octavia Butler (community-building amid collapse; new social philosophy from necessity; tension between survival pragmatism and utopian vision; leadership as burden)
- **Target word count**: 7,400 (reading time: 30 min)
- **Risk card**: None
- **Direction**: A social anthropologist arrives at a human colony isolated for three generations, founded on a radical experiment: elimination of individual property, privacy, and fixed identity (people rotate names, roles, households on a regular cycle). Her field reports shift from clinical observation to personal crisis as she realizes the colony's model is working — and her own society's assumptions about selfhood are the aberration. Le Guin's anthropological rigor meets Chambers' warmth. Left Hand of Darkness gives structural DNA (outsider report, alien social logic); Parable of the Sower gives thematic engine (community from necessity, cost of building new). Resist conversion narrative — she should leave changed but not converted, carrying a question.
