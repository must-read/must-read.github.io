## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Franz Kafka — *The Trial*, Chapter 1 (Author A + Work X)

**Source**: Author A (Franz Kafka) and Work X (*The Trial*)
**Origin**: *The Trial*, Chapter 1, "Arrest — Conversation with Mrs. Grubach — Then Miss Burstner"
**Why representative**: This passage establishes Kafka's signature method — a surreal premise reported in flat, bureaucratic language, where the protagonist's bewilderment is met by officials who are polite, unhelpful, and operating within rules they themselves barely understand.

> Someone must have been telling lies about Josef K., he knew he had done nothing wrong but, one morning, he was arrested. Every day at eight in the morning he was brought his breakfast by Mrs. Grubach's cook — Mrs. Grubach was his landlady — but today she didn't come.

K. waits in bed, rings for service, and a mysterious man enters his room. When K. asks who the visitor is, the man ignores the question and reports to someone in the next room that "He wants Anna to bring him his breakfast," receiving the response "It is not possible."

K. jumps from bed, demanding to know who is in the adjoining room. Upon entering the living room, he encounters another man by a window who informs him: "You can't go away when you're under arrest."

When K. asks why he's being arrested, the man explains: "That's something we're not allowed to tell you. Go into your room and wait there. Proceedings are underway and you'll learn about everything all in good time."

The officers examine K.'s clothing and discuss taking his possessions, explaining that items stored in warehouses often go missing over time. K. considers whether this might be an elaborate joke. Yet he decides to take the matter seriously.

The supervisor, sitting casually with crossed legs, appears indifferent to K.'s protests about proper procedure and documentation. He explains that the court doesn't search for guilt among the populace — rather, guilt draws the court's attention, as the law states. The officers are merely low-ranking functionaries.

K. questions the authority and identity of those present, noting the absence of uniforms. The supervisor responds curtly that they have nothing to do with K.'s actual case and know almost nothing about him.

The supervisor then lectures K.: "Think less about us and what's going to happen to you, and think a bit more about yourself." He advises K. to express his innocence less vociferously, noting that excessive talking has worked against rather than for K.'s case.

**Key techniques to channel**: (1) The matter-of-fact reportage of impossible situations. (2) Officials who are individually reasonable and collectively insane. (3) The protagonist's insistence on rational procedure in an irrational system. (4) The paradox of being formally accused but practically free — the arrest that changes nothing and everything.

### Sample 2: Franz Kafka — *The Metamorphosis*, Opening (Author A)

**Source**: Author A (Franz Kafka)
**Origin**: *The Metamorphosis* (1915), opening pages
**Why representative**: Shows Kafka's technique of treating the extraordinary as a mundane problem — Gregor's first thoughts after becoming an insect are about missing his train.

> One morning, when Gregor Samsa woke from troubled dreams, he found himself transformed in his bed into a horrible vermin.

Gregor's initial reaction: confusion and denial. He observes his familiar room — textile samples from his job as a traveling salesman and a framed picture of a woman in furs — and wonders if this could be merely a dream. However, he recognizes the reality of his situation: "It wasn't a dream."

Despite his physical transformation, Gregor's mind remains on practical concerns. His alarm clock shows nearly 6:30 AM, and he's already missed his early train. He fears his boss will hear of his absence — his perfect attendance record now broken. His family knocks; his voice has changed dramatically — a "painful and uncontrollable squeaking." His sister pleads for him to open the door. A visitor arrives — the chief clerk from his company, indicating that his absence has already triggered concern at work.

**Key technique**: The horror is not the metamorphosis. The horror is that Gregor's first thought is about being late to work. The system's hold on him is so complete that becoming an insect is less alarming than missing a shift. This is the rhythm the greengrocer story needs — the practical concern swallowing the existential one.

### Sample 3: George Orwell — "Politics and the English Language" (Author B)

**Source**: Author B (George Orwell)
**Origin**: "Politics and the English Language" (1946), key passages
**Why representative**: Orwell's argument about how political language manufactures compliance — how euphemism transforms atrocity into procedure — is the intellectual substrate of the greengrocer's world.

> "Political language has to consist largely of euphemism, question-begging and sheer cloudy vagueness."

> "Defenceless villages are bombarded from the air, the inhabitants driven out into the countryside, the cattle machine-gunned, the huts set on fire with incendiary bullets: this is called pacification."

> "If thought corrupts language, language can also corrupt thought. A bad usage can spread by tradition and imitation, even among people who should and do know better."

> "What is above all needed is to let the meaning choose the word, and not the other way about."

> Political language is "designed to make lies sound truthful and murder respectable, and to give an appearance of solidity to pure wind."

**Key technique**: Orwell's prose is transparent — every sentence could be read aloud to a twelve-year-old. The horror comes not from obfuscation but from clarity: stating plainly what euphemism hides. In the greengrocer story, this manifests as the narrator's refusal to mystify the bureaucracy. Each office, each form, each redirect is described with lucid specificity. The absurdity is not in the prose but in the facts the prose reports.

### Sample 4: Milan Kundera — *The Unbearable Lightness of Being*, Opening (Work Y)

**Source**: Work Y (*The Unbearable Lightness of Being*)
**Origin**: Part 1, "Lightness and Weight," opening philosophical passage
**Why representative**: Kundera's framework of lightness versus weight — the idea that actions gain gravity only through repetition, and that our unrepeatable lives are therefore weightless — provides the thematic architecture for the greengrocer's crisis.

> "The idea of eternal return is a mysterious one, and Nietzsche has often perplexed other philosophers with it: to think that everything recurs as we once experienced it, and that the recurrence itself recurs ad infinitum!"

> "If every second of our lives recurs an infinite number of times, we are nailed to eternity as Jesus Christ was nailed to the cross. It is a terrifying prospect. In the world of eternal return the weight of unbearable responsibility lies heavy on every move we make. That is why Nietzsche called the idea of eternal return the heaviest of burdens (das schwerste Gewicht)."

> "But is heaviness truly deplorable and lightness splendid?"

> A life which disappears once and for all, which does not return, is like a shadow, without weight, dead in advance.

Kundera's framework, applied to the greengrocer: The sign has been displayed 4,015 mornings (11 years x 365). Each display was identical, meaningless, weightless. But the single morning of its absence — the one morning it is NOT there — carries enormous weight. Lightness (the meaningless sign) versus weight (the meaningful absence). This inversion is the thematic core.

### Sample 5: Vaclav Havel — *The Power of the Powerless* (Contextual Source)

**Source**: Contextual / thematic background
**Origin**: *The Power of the Powerless* (1978), the greengrocer passage
**Why representative**: The original philosophical thought experiment that inspired the story's premise.

> The manager of a fruit-and-vegetable shop places in his window, among the onions and carrots, the slogan: "Workers of the world, unite!"

> He put them all into the window simply because it has been done that way for years, because everyone does it, and because that is the way it has to be. If he were to refuse, there could be trouble.

> The slogan is really a sign, and as such it contains a subliminal but very definite message. Verbally, it might be expressed this way: "I, the greengrocer XY, live here and I know what I must do. I behave in the manner expected of me. I can be depended upon and am beyond reproach. I am obedient and therefore I have the right to be left in peace."

> The greengrocer would be embarrassed and ashamed to put such an unequivocal statement of his own degradation in the shop window, and quite naturally so, for he is a human being and thus has a sense of his own dignity. To overcome this complication, his expression of loyalty must take the form of a sign which, at least on its textual surface, indicates a level of disinterested conviction.

**Key technique**: Havel is NOT a source to channel stylistically. But his analytical framework — the sign as performance of compliance, the semiotic content as alibi for the structural function — is what the story dramatizes. The story must never explain what Havel explains. It must SHOW it, through the greengrocer's day.

### 2 Selected Concepts

**1. The Concert by Vermeer (stolen painting)**

A Vermeer painting stolen from the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum in 1990. Never recovered. Valued at $250 million. The museum still hangs the empty frame where the painting once hung — a deliberate display of absence. The frame without its content has become more famous than the painting ever was. Visitors come specifically to see the empty frame.

**Integration**: The greengrocer, during his day of office visits, passes a municipal gallery or lobby where a framed space on the wall holds nothing — just a label that reads "On loan" or "Under restoration." This image echoes his own situation: a frame (the window, the lamination, the form of compliance) that has lost its content. The parallel is never stated. It is simply there — a passing detail during the compliance office visit. The greengrocer notices it without thinking about it, the way one notices things in waiting rooms. But the reader registers the rhyme: empty frame, empty sign. Additionally, the concept of an absence being more valuable/significant than the presence connects directly to Kundera's lightness-weight framework. The sign's absence has more weight than eleven years of its presence.

**2. Restrictor plate (engine limiter)**

A device installed at the intake of an engine to limit its power. The engine is capable of more, but the restrictor ensures it operates within prescribed bounds. Used in motorsport so no car has an unfair advantage — everyone is equally limited.

**Integration**: The greengrocer's neighbor, or a customer in the shop, is a mechanic or works at the local motor pool. During a brief conversation (perhaps while the greengrocer is deciding what to do about the window), this person mentions restrictor plates — how engines are built to produce more power than they're allowed to use, how the limiter is not a flaw but a design feature, how removing it would not make the engine better, just uncontrolled. The greengrocer hears this without connecting it to his own situation, but the reader does: the sign in the window is his restrictor plate. It limits him to a known, manageable output. Without it, he is capable of more — but "more" is not freedom, it is just unregulated power with no framework to contain it. The conversation should feel natural — shop talk, nothing symbolic — and should occur in the evening section, before the night of decision.

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

A greengrocer in an unnamed Central European city has been displaying a sign in his shop window every morning for eleven years. The sign is in a language he does not speak; he does not remember who told him to display it. On Tuesday, the sign falls and the glass cracks. He spends the day navigating the municipal apparatus to obtain a replacement — sign office, lamination bureau, compliance office, archives — and discovers that no one in the system knows what the sign says, either. By evening he stands before an empty window and must decide what to put in it, a decision that should be trivial but feels, for reasons he cannot articulate, like it will determine the shape of every morning that follows.

### 2. Protagonist

- **Name**: Ivo Polak, 52
- **Situation**: Widower, five years. Runs a small fruit and vegetable shop on Zeleznicni Street, inherited from his wife's father. The shop was always hers in spirit; he was an electrician before she got sick. He learned the trade of vegetables to keep the shop alive after she died — a form of mourning that looks, from the outside, like moving on.
- **What he WANTS**: A replacement sign. Specifically: to restore his window to the state it was in yesterday morning, so that tomorrow morning can proceed the same way yesterday morning did.
- **What he NEEDS**: To recognize that his eleven-year ritual of displaying the sign has been a form of grief management — a way of performing normalcy in a life that stopped being normal when his wife died. The sign is not political compliance (though it functions as such). It is his restrictor plate: the thing that keeps each day identical to the last, which is the only way he knows to survive.
- **Flaw/blind spot**: Ivo cannot distinguish between routine and meaning. He believes that if he can replicate the form of his life (the sign in the window, the shop open at 7, the vegetables arranged by color), the content will take care of itself. He confuses continuation with purpose. This is why the blank sign appeals to him in the end — form without content is, he discovers, what he has been living all along.

### 3. Structure

**Opening image/scene**: Ivo is arranging plums in the window display when the sign falls. Not dramatically — it simply lets go of the adhesive that has held it for years, as adhesive does. The sound is small: a crack, like stepping on a walnut shell. He picks it up. The glass is fractured across the middle, bisecting the foreign text. He cannot read the text, but he can see that it is now two things instead of one, and this bothers him in a way he cannot name.

**The inciting disruption**: The sign has fallen before — once, three years ago — and he simply pressed it back. This time the glass is broken, the text obscured by fracture lines. It cannot be repaired. He must replace it. He knows there is a sign office because he has seen it mentioned on a municipal directory in the post office. He closes the shop (a thing he has not done on a Tuesday in eleven years) and walks to the municipal building.

**The escalating middle (four office visits)**:

1. **Sign Office (Amt fur Beschilderung)** — A clerk asks for his original requisition number. Ivo has no requisition number. He has no memory of a requisition. The clerk is sympathetic, even kind, but explains that replacement signs require the original requisition as proof of authorized display. Without it, the office cannot determine what the sign said, and therefore cannot reproduce it. She suggests the archives, which maintain copies of all requisitions. She also notes that the sign's lamination must be handled separately, at the lamination bureau.

2. **Lamination Bureau** — In a different building, on the third floor. The bureau handles only the physical encasement, not the content. The clerk here is younger, more bored. He explains that lamination orders must reference an active sign requisition. Without the requisition, he cannot laminate. He has a form — 14b — that can be used to request retroactive requisition status, but it must be submitted at the compliance office.

3. **Compliance Office** — The compliance officer is the most senior person Ivo meets all day. She is efficient, not unkind, and handles Ivo's case with genuine attention. She pulls up his address on the register and confirms that, yes, a sign is recorded as being displayed at his premises. The record shows that the sign was classified under "Category 7: Civic Participation (Voluntary)." The word "voluntary" gives Ivo pause — he has never thought of the sign as voluntary, nor as involuntary, nor as anything with a category at all. The compliance officer explains that she can authorize a replacement, but the content of the sign must be verified against the original in the archives.

4. **Archives** — Closed on Tuesdays. A note on the door, handwritten in ink that has faded to the color of weak tea, reads: "Closed Tuesdays (pursuant to Directive 7, 1987)." Beneath it, someone has taped a second note: "For Tuesday inquiries, please visit Wednesday." Ivo stands in the corridor and reads both notes several times. He does not try the door.

**The crisis point**: Evening. Ivo returns to the shop. The window is empty — not just without the sign, but without the display he normally builds around it. He has been gone all day; the vegetables were never arranged. The window is dark glass reflecting the street. A neighbor, Brada (the mechanic), stops by to pick up onions and comments on the empty window. They talk. Brada mentions something about restrictor plates — a car he is working on. Ivo half-listens. After Brada leaves, Ivo stands alone in the shop, holding the broken sign in both hands, looking at the empty window. He has three options and the night to decide.

**The ending**: Wednesday morning. Ivo arrives at the shop at 6:45, as always. He unlocks the door, turns on the lights, begins arranging the vegetables. The narrative describes his routine in meticulous detail — the plums, the peppers, the parsley in the metal bucket. When he reaches the window, we learn what he has decided: he has placed a blank piece of card stock — the same dimensions as the old sign, inserted into the old frame (which he has cleaned and re-glued) — in the window. White card, no text. The frame is there, the glass is there, the shape of the sign is there. From across the street, it looks like a sign. Up close, it is nothing.

The story's final beat: Ivo's first customer of the day enters the shop. She glances at the window, says nothing about the sign. She buys tomatoes. After she leaves, Ivo looks at the window again. The blank sign catches the morning light and becomes, briefly, a mirror — reflecting the street, the other shops, their signs (which he has also never read). He turns back to his vegetables.

**What is left open**: Whether anyone will ever notice. Whether the archives, when he visits on Wednesday, will have the original requisition. Whether "Category 7: Civic Participation (Voluntary)" is truly voluntary. Whether the blank sign is an act of freedom or the most complete form of compliance yet — displaying the pure form of obedience, stripped of even the pretense of content. The story does not answer any of these questions. Ivo does not ask them. He sells vegetables.

### 4. Key Scenes

1. **The sign falling** (opening): The physical break that starts everything. Must be described with Kafka's matter-of-fact precision — no symbolism, no portent. A thing that was stuck becomes unstuck. The adhesive failed. This is what adhesive does eventually. The greengrocer's reaction is practical, not philosophical: "I will need a new one."

2. **The compliance office encounter**: The most important office visit. This is where Ivo learns the sign is classified as "Category 7: Civic Participation (Voluntary)" — a designation that reframes his entire eleven-year routine. The compliance officer is the story's most complex minor character: competent, genuinely helpful, and operating within a system she does not question because it works. She sees no contradiction between "voluntary" and "required for compliance." Neither did Ivo, until this moment. The scene does double duty: advances the plot (he still cannot get a replacement) and reveals the system's core absurdity (voluntary compliance that is, functionally, mandatory).

3. **The closed archives**: The shortest scene but the most Kafkaesque. Ivo reads the note. He reads the second note. He stands in the corridor. He does not try the door. This is the moment where the system reveals itself as complete — it has accounted for his visit by redirecting him to a day that has not happened yet. The archives are not denying him access; they are deferring it infinitely. "For Tuesday inquiries, please visit Wednesday." But on Wednesday, will there be a note that says "For Wednesday inquiries, please visit Thursday"? The story does not answer this. Ivo does not wonder about it. He simply accepts that today is Tuesday and the archives are closed on Tuesdays.

4. **The conversation with Brada**: Evening, in the shop. Brada is a practical man, a mechanic. He talks about engines the way Ivo talks about vegetables — with the familiarity of daily handling. The restrictor plate enters the conversation naturally: Brada is working on a Skoda whose owner wants more power, but the plate is factory-installed and removing it voids the warranty. "The engine can do more," Brada says, "but doing more isn't the point. The point is doing what's expected, reliably, every day." Ivo nods. He is thinking about onions. But the reader is thinking about the sign.

5. **Wednesday morning / the blank sign**: The final scene. No epiphany, no announcement of theme. Just the meticulous description of Ivo's routine, the vegetables, and then the white card in the frame. The customer who doesn't notice. The window catching the light. The story ends in the middle of a day, not at its end — because the story is about what continues, not what resolves.

### 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Opening**: Mild irritation shading into resignation. The reader should feel what Ivo feels: the annoyance of a disrupted routine, the kind of thing that happens on Tuesdays. Not anxiety, not dread — just the familiar sag of a day that is going to be more complicated than expected.

**Middle (office visits)**: A slowly accumulating bewilderment that the reader recognizes before Ivo does. Each office is individually reasonable. Each clerk is polite. But the reader begins to sense the pattern — the circular referrals, the missing original, the categories that classify without explaining. The humor is bone-dry: the absurdity of "Category 7: Civic Participation (Voluntary)" should land like a deadpan joke that is also, somehow, the thesis of the story. The reader should feel the specific unease of recognizing a system that works perfectly while accomplishing nothing.

**Evening (the crisis)**: A strange, empty calm. Not dramatic tension but a kind of vertigo — the feeling of standing at the edge of a decision that should be trivial but isn't. The reader should feel the weight of the empty window, the way its blankness is more demanding than the sign ever was. The lightness of the meaningless sign versus the weight of its absence — Kundera's framework, felt rather than stated.

**Ending**: Something between recognition and discomfort. The blank sign should feel like both the most honest and most disturbing possible choice. The reader should understand, without being told, that Ivo has discovered that the content of the sign never mattered — that what mattered was the display, the form, the daily performance. And that this discovery changes nothing. He will continue. The vegetables will be arranged. The blank sign will remain. The reader should put the story down and, perhaps much later — while passing a shop window, or reading a sign they've never bothered to translate — feel something shift.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Author A — Franz Kafka (prose style)**: The entire narrative voice. Every sentence should be precise, declarative, and matter-of-fact, even when reporting absurdity. The sign falls; Ivo picks it up. The archives are closed on Tuesdays; he reads the note. No melodrama, no ironic distance, no winking at the reader. The prose treats the bureaucratic nightmare as an ordinary inconvenience, which makes it worse. The office visits in particular should channel Kafka's *Trial* — each encounter a layer of polite incomprehension, each official helpful and useless simultaneously.

**Author B — George Orwell (political clarity)**: Concentrated in two places: (1) the compliance office scene, where the language of "Category 7: Civic Participation (Voluntary)" reveals the political architecture beneath the bureaucratic surface — the Orwellian insight that language can make coercion look consensual; and (2) the overall narrative strategy of transparent prose. Orwell's influence means the story never mystifies. The reader always knows exactly what is happening. The horror is not in confusion but in comprehension.

**Work X — *The Trial* (structure)**: The day's architecture. Four offices, each deferring to the next, each revealing another layer of the system without revealing its center. Like Josef K., Ivo is caught in a process whose rules are partially visible and wholly inaccessible. Unlike K., Ivo does not protest or demand his rights. He simply proceeds to the next office. This passivity is its own form of Kafkaesque dread — the man who cooperates so completely with the system that the system has nothing to punish.

**Work Y — *The Unbearable Lightness of Being* (themes)**: The lightness/weight inversion. For eleven years the sign was light — meaningless, unread, part of the furniture. Its absence is heavy — a gap that demands attention, decision, action. The story dramatizes Kundera's question: if nothing recurs, if each morning is unique and unrepeatable, then the sign's presence on 4,015 mornings has no accumulated weight. But its absence on a single morning — on THIS morning — suddenly weighs everything. The blank sign Ivo chooses is the lightest possible object (empty card) carrying the heaviest possible meaning (the form of compliance without content, the admission that content never mattered). Kundera's themes also surface in the political register: the way private decisions (what to put in a shop window) become existentially significant under systems of power.

### 7. Title

**Working title**: "The Greengrocer" -- starts with "The." While the current percentage (0/98 = 0%) is well under the 30% threshold, the title is also too direct a reference to Havel's essay and sounds like a fable title. It lacks the specificity the story deserves.

**Recommended title**: **Sign, Frame, Glass**

**Rationale**: Three concrete nouns, no article, no verb. It names the physical components of what the greengrocer is trying to replace — the elements that constitute the "form" of compliance. It echoes the story's discovery: that the sign was never about the words. It was about the sign (the display), the frame (the structure), and the glass (the barrier between interior and exterior, private and public). The title is specific enough to intrigue, vague enough to resist summary, and structurally distinct from every existing title in the catalog.

**Alternative titles considered**:
- "Requisition" — too clinical
- "Closed on Tuesdays" — good but too cute, sounds like a comedy
- "Category Seven" — intriguing but opaque; reader won't know what it means until deep into the story
- "Voluntary and Has Always Been Completed" — already taken (similar: "Compliance Is Voluntary and Has Always Been Completed")
- "Display Window" — too literal
- "What the Sign Said" — starts with "What" (okay per rules) but feels like a children's book title

## Notes for the Writer

1. **Language of the sign**: The sign is in a real language the greengrocer does not speak. The story should specify the language — suggest Georgian (mkhedruli script), which is visually distinctive, geographically plausible for a Central European border-confusion scenario, and carries no easy political coding for the Western reader. The greengrocer calls it "the foreign writing" or "the other language." He has never tried to have it translated. This is important: it is not that translation was impossible, but that it never occurred to him as necessary.

2. **Setting**: Unnamed Central European city. Small enough that Ivo knows his neighbors, large enough to have a municipal building with multiple departments. Think Brno, Olomouc, Kosice — places with Habsburg-era bureaucratic infrastructure overlaid with postwar administration overlaid with post-1989 municipal governance. The architecture should feel layered, like the bureaucracy itself.

3. **Tone**: Bone-dry. The humor is cumulative, not punchline-based. Each office visit is slightly more absurd than the last, but none is individually outrageous. A reader should find themselves smiling at "For Tuesday inquiries, please visit Wednesday" not because it's a joke, but because it is the most natural thing in the world and also completely insane.

4. **Pacing**: The four office visits should occupy roughly 60% of the story. The evening scene with Brada: 15%. The opening (sign falling): 10%. The Wednesday morning ending: 15%. The office visits should not be equal in length — the compliance office scene is the longest and most important; the archives scene is the shortest.

5. **What NOT to do**: Do not have Ivo reflect on the meaning of his situation. Do not have him draw parallels between the sign and his life. Do not have him think about his dead wife while staring at the empty window (this connection should be available to the reader through the structure of Ivo's behavior — his devotion to routine, his need to restore the window to its previous state — but never stated). Do not explain what the blank sign means. Do not give the compliance officer a speech about the nature of systems. Let the system speak for itself through its procedures.

6. **The Vermeer frame**: During one of the office visits (suggest the compliance office, which has a waiting area), Ivo notices a framed space on the wall — empty, or containing only a label. "Awaiting installation" or "Under restoration." A small detail. He does not think about it. But it should be there, visible to the reader: another frame without its content, another display of absence.

7. **Restrictor plate conversation**: Must feel completely natural — two men talking about engines, not about metaphors. Brada should not be wise or symbolic. He should be a mechanic who talks about engines because that's what he does. The connection to the sign is the reader's to make, not the character's.
