# Story Plan: Stripped Country

**Combo ID**: adv-expedition-journey-002
**Genre**: Adventure
**Subgenre**: Expedition/Journey Narrative
**Slug**: `stripped-country`
**Target Word Count**: 4,400 words (range: 4,100–4,700)

---

## 1. Premise

A woman bush pilot in late-1950s Kenya, hired to fly a geological survey team along a corridor of abandoned colonial stations between Lake Magadi and the Tanzanian border, makes an unscheduled landing at a decommissioned airstrip she once used during the war. On the ground, she encounters a former telegraph operator she thought was dead and a landscape that refuses to be merely scenery. The flight that should take a day stretches into three as mechanical trouble, weather, and the pilot's own compulsion to verify what she sees from above against what she finds on the ground force a series of landings at ghost stations along the Rift Valley floor.

## 2. Protagonist

**Name**: Eda Lowe
**Age**: 42
**Situation**: British-born, Kenya-raised bush pilot operating a single-engine Cessna 180 out of Wilson Airport, Nairobi. She flies charter — geological surveys, supply runs, the occasional emergency medical evacuation. She has been in Kenya since age four, when her father came to manage a sisal estate near Thika. She flew reconnaissance for the RAF in East Africa during the war, mapping terrain for military operations. She has never left Kenya for longer than eleven days.

**Want**: To complete the survey flight on schedule, collect her fee, and keep her reputation for reliability intact.

**Need**: To reconcile the two versions of the country she carries — the aerial one (patterns, grids, cleared land, the beautiful indifference of altitude) and the ground one (names, faces, the taste of tea at a station that no longer exists). She needs to stop treating the aerial view as the real one.

**Flaw**: Eda treats precision as a substitute for honesty. She is meticulous about fuel calculations, weather reports, engine maintenance — and uses this operational competence to avoid examining her own position within the colonial machinery she has served. She flew reconnaissance that helped the British suppress Mau Mau. She has never articulated, even to herself, what that means. Her precision is a form of moral evasion — she knows exactly where she is on the map at all times, but she has never located herself in the history that the map records.

## 3. Structure

### Opening (approx. 600 words)
Eda in the air, flying the first leg from Wilson Airport south toward Magadi. The geological survey team — two British geologists and their Kenyan field assistant, Josephat — are behind her. She narrates the landscape below in the spare, competent voice of someone who has flown this corridor hundreds of times: the Ngong Hills, the drop into the Rift Valley, the soda flats of Magadi shimmering white. The passage establishes the aerial view — beautiful, ordered, legible. She mentions landmarks by their operational names (the "Magadi spur," the "southern transit"), a private cartography that strips the country to its navigational essentials. She notes, in passing, the ruins of a station below — corrugated iron catching light — and does not slow down.

### Inciting Disruption (approx. 800 words)
The survey team's first ground stop at Magadi. While the geologists take measurements at the soda lake, Eda inspects the aircraft and discovers an oil pressure anomaly — not dangerous yet, but she'll need to monitor it. Josephat, the field assistant, asks her if she knows the abandoned station they flew over. She does. He tells her his uncle was the last telegraph operator there — a man named Odongo — before the station was decommissioned in 1953. Eda remembers Odongo. She once landed at that strip during the war to collect dispatches. She remembers the particular sound his telegraph key made, a detail so specific it startles her. Josephat says Odongo is still alive, living in a village near the old station. The information lands like a change in air pressure — not dramatic, but the instruments register it.

### Escalating Middle (approx. 1,800 words)
The survey continues south along the Rift Valley floor. At each stop, the pattern repeats with variation: Eda sees something from the air (a cleared rectangle, a line of planted trees, a road ending in nothing), then encounters its ground-level reality during survey stops. The gap between what the aerial view shows and what the ground reveals widens.

**Key escalation points:**

*Stop 2 — An abandoned pyrethrum drying shed.* From the air it was a rectangle of corrugated iron. On the ground, Eda finds the shed still standing, the drying racks intact, a calendar on the wall stopped at March 1954. The pyrethrum flowers that once grew for miles — Kenya's "white gold," insecticide for the war effort — are gone. The fields have returned to scrub. She finds a brass survey benchmark embedded in the concrete floor, stamped 1938, the year her father's sisal estate was at peak production. She pries at it with her boot and it does not move. The survey team takes no notice.

*Stop 3 — A decommissioned weather station.* The Stevenson screen is still standing, its louvered walls bleached white, but the instruments are gone. Inside, the mounting brackets are visible — the shapes of absent thermometers, absent barometers. Eda recognizes the layout; she used to radio weather reports from stations like this during her reconnaissance flights. She collected data that helped plan operations. The station recorded rain and wind; she transmitted the numbers; someone in Nairobi decided what to do with them. The chain of use is clear but she has never before stood at the beginning of it, in the place where the data was made.

*Stop 4 — The trig pillar.* A concrete triangulation pillar on a ridge overlooking the valley — one of the pillars erected in the 1930s to triangulate British East Africa. The brass plate on top is still there, stamped with coordinates. From this point, the colonial surveyors could see fifty miles in every direction. Eda realizes she has used these coordinates her entire flying career. Her charts are built on this network of pillars, each one placed on high ground by African labor, each one a claim expressed as measurement. Dead reckoning, she thinks. Navigation by estimation from a known point. But who decided the known point?

### Crisis (approx. 700 words)
The oil pressure problem worsens. Eda must land at the nearest viable strip — which turns out to be the old airstrip near Odongo's former station, the one Josephat mentioned. She lands. The strip is marginal — cracked earth, thorn scrub encroaching from both sides — but usable. While the geologists set up camp and Eda works on the engine, Josephat walks to the nearby village and returns with Odongo.

Odongo is old but sharp. He remembers Eda. He remembers the dispatches she collected. He remembers the sound of her aircraft — not the Cessna she flies now, but the Tiger Moth she flew during the war. He tells her, without rancor, what happened after the station closed: the telegraph wire was pulled down for scrap, the corrugated iron was taken for roofing in the village, the termites ate the doorframes, the windsock pole fell in a storm and no one set it upright. He tells her the names of the people who worked at the station — not just himself, but the track clearers, the water carriers, the woman who cooked for the stationmaster. Names Eda never knew despite landing there six or seven times during the war. She took the dispatches and left. She saw the station from the air as a rectangle of cleared ground with a windsock. She saw it from the ground as a place to refuel and collect paper. She did not see it as a place where people had lives.

Odongo does not accuse her of anything. He simply names the people and what they did, and the naming is enough.

### Ending (approx. 500 words)
The next morning, Eda fixes the oil pressure problem — a worn gasket, replaceable from her spares kit. Before takeoff, she walks the old airstrip alone. The wind is blowing and there is no windsock to read it, so she picks up a handful of dust and lets it fall — the oldest method, before instruments, before measurement. She watches which way it drifts. She can feel the wind on her face and she can see the dust trail and the two readings do not quite agree and she takes off anyway, navigating the discrepancy the way she has always navigated: by carrying both pieces of information and trusting neither completely.

In the air, climbing out over the Rift Valley, she looks down at the old station. From altitude it is what it has always been — a rectangle of cleared ground, already blurring back into scrub. She can see the village nearby, the paths between the houses, the shapes of people moving. She cannot see their faces. She cannot see Odongo. The aerial view gives her the pattern; the ground gave her the names. She carries both. Neither is the country. The country is what happens between them, in the gap she cannot close, and she is running out of fuel, and the next strip is forty minutes south, and the oil pressure gauge is holding, and the wind has shifted, and she corrects for it, and flies on.

The ending does NOT resolve the moral question. Eda does not have an epiphany about colonialism. She does not decide to change her life. She adjusts her heading and continues flying, carrying the new weight of Odongo's names alongside her old aerial certainties, and the story ends with the instrument panel and the horizon line and the gap between them.

## 4. Key Scenes (Must-Have)

1. **The aerial opening** — Eda flying, narrating the landscape in operational language. Establishes the voice (Markham's spare precision) and the core tension (the aerial view as a kind of willful blindness).

2. **The pyrethrum shed** — The first ground-level encounter with ghost infrastructure. The brass benchmark embedded in the floor. The calendar stopped at 1954. The absent flowers. This scene does the Sebald work: a specific ruin saturated with the economic history it represents.

3. **The trig pillar** — Eda standing at a colonial survey marker and realizing her entire navigational framework is built on it. The dead reckoning meditation. This is the structural/thematic pivot where the story's ideas come into focus without being announced.

4. **Odongo names the people** — The crisis scene. The ground-level truth that the aerial view cannot contain. Not accusation, just enumeration. Names, tasks, the texture of work. Conrad's method inverted: instead of Marlow encountering Kurtz at the end of the river, Eda encounters an ordinary man who simply remembers.

5. **The dust-reading and takeoff** — Eda reading the wind without instruments, carrying two kinds of knowledge, flying on. The story's refusal to resolve.

## 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Opening**: Competence, control, the pleasure of precision. The reader should feel the appeal of the aerial view — its clarity, its beauty, its legibility. Eda is good at what she does and the prose should transmit that skill.

**Middle escalation**: Growing unease. Each ground stop introduces a specific detail that the aerial view missed. The unease is not dramatic — it is cumulative, like Sebald's accumulation of historical losses during his Suffolk walk. The pyrethrum shed, the weather station, the trig pillar: each one adds a layer of history that Eda's operational language cannot contain.

**Crisis**: Not shock but recognition. Odongo does not tell Eda anything she did not already know at some level. He simply forces the knowledge from the aerial register (pattern, grid, coordinates) into the ground register (names, faces, work). The emotion is not guilt — it is the vertigo of seeing the same place from two altitudes simultaneously and being unable to make a single picture.

**Ending**: Unresolved tension carried forward. Eda flies on. The fuel gauge, the oil pressure, the wind correction — the operational details resume, but they no longer carry the same reassurance. The competence remains, but the comfort of competence is gone. The reader should feel the difference between a pilot who does not think about what is below and a pilot who cannot stop thinking about it but flies anyway.

## 6. Formula Integration Plan

### Author A — Beryl Markham (lyrical spare memoir-adventure prose, precision without decoration, the solo journey as self-portrait)
**Most visible in**: The entire narrative voice, but especially the opening aerial sequence and the dust-reading scene at the end. The prose should be spare, precise, grounded in physical sensation. Markham's gift is making technical competence lyrical without decoration — the fuel calculation as a form of poetry. Eda's voice should carry this: she notices the shimmer of the soda flats, the way the aircraft responds to thermals, the exact color of the oil on the dipstick, and these observations should feel both functional and beautiful. The solo journey as self-portrait: Eda is most herself in the cockpit, and the story is her self-portrait, and the portrait is incomplete because she has been leaving out everything below.

### Author B — Joseph Conrad (psychological depth beneath adventure surfaces, the unreliable retrospective narrator, colonial landscapes rendered with critical ambivalence)
**Most visible in**: The Odongo scene and the trig pillar meditation. Conrad's influence operates through the story's psychological undertow: the surface is an adventure (a bush pilot on a survey flight with mechanical trouble), but beneath it runs the question of complicity. The retrospective quality: Eda narrates in a voice that knows more than it says, that circles around the colonial question without landing on it directly — until Odongo's naming forces a landing. The critical ambivalence: the colonial infrastructure is both the thing that gave Eda her freedom (she could not fly without the airstrips, the charts, the weather stations) and the thing that extracted labor and land from the people she now flies over. This ambivalence is never resolved, only held.

### Work X — The Rings of Saturn (Sebald) (the walking journey as digressive meditation, landscape saturated with decay/memory/vanished lives)
**Most visible in**: The escalating middle — the series of ground stops where each ruin opens into its history. The pyrethrum shed that connects to the wartime insecticide industry that connects to the colonial agriculture that connects to the labor that cleared the fields. The weather station where absent instruments leave bracket-shaped ghosts on the wall. The trig pillar where coordinates become claims. This is Sebald's method: a journey that keeps falling through the surface of the present into the past, each specific object becoming a portal to a larger system of loss. The melancholy of places that have outlived their purpose. But — per Markham's correction in the author meeting — the mourning is complicated by the protagonist's presence during the operational period. Eda is not Sebald's walker arriving after the fact. She was there.

### Work Y — West with the Night (Markham) (flight as freedom and solitude, Africa as both home and colonized territory, the competence narrative)
**Most visible in**: The opening and closing sequences, and throughout Eda's relationship with flying. Flight as freedom: the cockpit is where Eda is most free, most competent, most herself. But the freedom is conditional — it was earned by serving the colonial administration, and the story makes this condition visible without moralizing about it. Africa as home and colonized territory: Eda is not a visitor. Kenya is the only place she has ever lived. Her relationship with the landscape is not touristic or exploitative in the obvious sense — it is deep, specific, grounded in decades of flying over it. But "home" and "colonized territory" are not different places for her; they are the same place seen from different altitudes.

## 7. Title

**"Stripped Country"** — The title operates on multiple levels. "Stripped country" is a bush-flying term for terrain that has been cleared of vegetation (natural or deliberate), making it visible and legible from the air. It also evokes the colonial stripping of the land — its resources, its names, its people's labor. And it suggests the protagonist's condition: a country (both the physical land and Eda's internal landscape) that has been stripped to its operational essentials, leaving the infrastructure visible but the human life removed. The title does not start with "The."

## Wikipedia Concepts Section

### 20 Random Wikipedia Articles Consulted

1. **Triangulation station** — Fixed surveying stations used in geodetic surveying, including concrete trig pillars with brass plates placed on high ground across colonial Africa.
2. **Heliograph** — Solar telegraph system signaling by flashes of sunlight reflected by a mirror, used extensively in colonial military campaigns.
3. **Pyrethrum** — Chrysanthemum-derived natural insecticide; Kenya was the world's largest producer by the 1950s, introduced by colonial agricultural services in 1928.
4. **Rift Valley fever** — Mosquito-borne viral disease of livestock, first identified in Kenya's Rift Valley in 1931, with cyclical outbreaks linked to rainfall.
5. **Veld fire / controlled burn** — Grassland fire ecology in African savannas, indigenous fire management practices predating colonial intervention.
6. **Windsock** — Conical textile tube indicating wind speed and direction at airfields; early aviation windsocks were rudimentary fabric, becoming standard by the 1920s.
7. **Uganda Railway ("Lunatic Express")** — Metre-gauge railway from Mombasa to Lake Victoria, built 1896–1901 with 32,000 imported Indian laborers.
8. **Lake Magadi** — Saline alkaline soda lake in the southernmost Kenya Rift Valley, mined for trona (sodium sesquicarbonate) since 1911.
9. **Duka** — Small retail shop in Kenya, originated with Indian "dukawallas" who stayed after railway construction; backbone of colonial-era and modern trade.
10. **Corrugated galvanized iron** — Patented 1830, exported to colonies; lightweight, portable roofing material that became ubiquitous in colonial Africa.
11. **Olduvai Gorge / Laetoli footprints** — Prehistoric archaeological sites in the Great Rift Valley; the Leakey excavations established East Africa as the cradle of humanity.
12. **Solar compass** — Navigation instrument using the sun's position rather than magnetism; Bagnold's sun-compass revolutionized desert exploration in the 1930s.
13. **Drift netting** — Pelagic fishing nets ("curtains of death") killing millions of non-target species; the concept of "ghost nets" — synthetic nets lost at sea that continue catching for years.
14. **Phantom limb syndrome** — Sensation that an amputated limb is still present; the brain's body map does not update after removal, creating pain from what is no longer there.
15. **Singing sand** — Desert dunes producing booming sounds (up to 105 dB) when sand avalanches trigger the dune's natural resonance frequency.
16. **Dead reckoning** — Navigation by estimating current position from a previously determined position, using speed and direction; errors are cumulative.
17. **Survey marker / benchmark** — Brass disks stamped with coordinates, embedded in rock or concrete as permanent position indicators; disturbing them is prohibited by law.
18. **Lake Natron** — Highly alkaline lake in the Rift Valley, the only regular breeding area for 2.5 million lesser flamingoes; its caustic environment protects nests from predators.
19. **Palimpsest** — Manuscript from which text has been scraped off for reuse; faint remains of former writing reappear over time.
20. **Termite mound architecture** — Meter-sized porous structures with sophisticated ventilation systems; in African savannas, mounds form fertile "islands" with higher tree density.

### 2 Selected Concepts for Fusion

**Selection 1: Phantom limb syndrome** — The concept of a body map that does not update after loss provides the story's deepest metaphor (operating beneath the surface, never stated explicitly). Eda's internal map of Kenya still contains stations, airstrips, and infrastructure that no longer exist. She navigates by a phantom country — the operational Kenya of the 1940s, with its functioning telegraph stations and manned weather outposts — that her body remembers even though the ground has changed. When she lands at the abandoned stations, she experiences a version of phantom limb pain: the expectation of a functioning place meeting the reality of an empty one. The termite-eaten doorframes, the absent instruments, the fallen windsock — these are the sensations of a phantom country. This integrates at the level of experience, not metaphor — Eda does not think "this is like a phantom limb." She simply feels the wrongness of landing at a strip that should have a windsock and does not.

**Integration**: The pyrethrum shed scene. Eda's hand reaches for a door latch that is no longer there — the muscle memory of a gesture repeated during the war. The body remembers the station as it was. The eyes see it as it is. The gap is physical, not philosophical.

**Selection 2: Dead reckoning** — Navigation by estimation from a known point, where errors accumulate over distance. This becomes the story's structural principle. Each stop in the journey is a "known point" from which Eda calculates her next position — but the known points (colonial survey markers, decommissioned stations, coordinates on her chart) are themselves relics of a system that is disappearing. She is navigating by dead reckoning through a landscape where the reference points are dying. The term itself — "dead" reckoning — carries the weight of the story's themes: reckoning with what is dead, navigating from the dead, the calculation that begins from loss.

**Integration**: The trig pillar scene. Eda stands at a triangulation point and thinks about dead reckoning — how her entire navigational practice depends on reference points established by colonial surveyors, how each flight is a calculation from a "known" position that is itself a product of empire. The meditation does not moralize; it simply lays out the mechanics of navigation and lets the reader hear the echo.

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Beryl Markham — from *West with the Night*

**Source**: Markham, *West with the Night* (1942). Collected via Goodreads and The Marginalian.

**Why representative**: Shows Markham's spare lyrical precision, her ability to render technical competence as poetry, her economy of language, and her treatment of solitude as a form of self-knowledge.

> "There are all kinds of silences and each of them means a different thing. There is the silence that comes with morning in a forest, and this is different from the silence of a sleeping city. There is silence after a rainstorm, and before a rainstorm, and these are not the same. There is the silence of emptiness, the silence of fear, the silence of doubt. There is a certain silence that can emanate from a lifeless object as from a chair lately used, or from a piano with old music on it, or from anything that has answered to the need of a man, for pleasure or for work. This kind of silence can speak. Its voice may be melancholy, but it is not always so; for the chair may have been left by a child or a lover or a dog. It is a soundless echo."

> "Being alone in an aeroplane for even so short a time as a night and a day, irrevocably alone, with nothing to observe but your instruments and your own hands in semi-darkness, nothing to contemplate but the size of your small courage, nothing to wonder about but the beliefs, the faces, and the hopes rooted in your mind — such an experience can be as startling as the first awareness of a stranger walking by your side at night. You are the stranger."

> "We fly, but we have not 'conquered' the air. Nature presides in all her dignity, permitting us the study and the use of such of her forces as we may understand. It is when we presume to intimacy, having been granted only tolerance, that the harsh stick falls across our impudent knuckles and we rub the pain, staring upward, startled by our ignorance."

> "There are as many Africas as there are books about Africa — and as many books about it as you could read in a leisurely lifetime. Whoever writes a new one can afford a certain complacency in the knowledge that his is a new picture agreeing with no one else's, but likely to be haughtily disagreed with by all those who believed in some other Africa... Being thus all things to all authors, it follows, I suppose, that Africa must be all things to all readers. Africa is mystic; it is wild; it is a sweltering inferno; it is a photographer's paradise, a hunter's Valhalla, an escapist's Utopia. It is what you will, and it withstands all interpretations. It is the last vestige of a dead world or the cradle of a shiny new one. To a lot of people, as to myself, it is just 'home.'"

> "I have learned that if you must leave a place that you have lived in and loved and where all your yesteryears are buried deep, leave it any way except a slow way, leave it the fastest way you can. Never turn back and never believe that an hour you remember is a better hour because it is dead. Passed years seem safe ones, vanquished ones, while the future lives in a cloud, formidable from a distance."

> "I saw how a man can be master of a craft, and how a craft can be master of an element. I saw the alchemy of perspective reduce my world, and all my other life, to grains in a cup."

### Sample 3: W.G. Sebald — from *The Rings of Saturn*

**Source**: Sebald, *The Rings of Saturn* (1995, trans. Michael Hulse 1998). Collected via published excerpts, Goodreads, and critical essays.

**Why representative**: Shows Sebald's digressive, accumulative prose style, his method of saturating landscape with historical memory, and his melancholy precision.

> "In August 1992, when the dog days were drawing to an end, I set off to walk the county of Suffolk, in the hope of dispelling the emptiness that takes hold of me whenever I have completed a long stint of work. And in fact my hope was realized, up to a point; for I have seldom felt so carefree as I did then, walking for hours in the day through the thinly populated countryside, which stretches inland from the coast."

> "I wonder now, however, whether there might be something in the old superstition that certain ailments of the spirit and of the body are particularly likely to beset us under the sign of the Dog Star. At all events, in retrospect I became preoccupied not only with the unaccustomed sense of freedom but also with the paralysing horror that had come over me at various times when confronted with the traces of destruction, reaching far back into the past, that were evident even in that remote place."

> "It takes just one awful second, and an entire epoch passes."

> "On every new thing there lies already the shadow of annihilation."

> "We lose our sense of reality proportionally to our engrossment in our own work."

> "From above, we see only the things people made, not the people themselves hiding within."

> "[He wrote of] the immense power of emptiness" [describing the sea over Dunwich — a town that fell into the sea over centuries, its churches, markets, and streets now under water.]

> "[It seemed as if we were] amidst the remains of our own civilization after its extinction in some future catastrophe."

**Sebald's structural method** (paraphrased from critical sources): Each chapter of *The Rings of Saturn* follows the same pattern — the walker encounters a place, and the place opens into its history through a series of digressions. A manor house leads to the family that built it, which leads to the silk trade that funded it, which leads to the weavers who fled religious persecution, which leads to the industry that consumed them. The connections are real but the structure is associative, not chronological. The melancholy comes from the accumulation of specific losses, each one documented with archival precision.

### Sample 4: Additional Markham — from *West with the Night*

**Source**: Markham, *West with the Night* (1942). Via The Marginalian and published reviews.

**Why representative**: Shows Markham's treatment of night flying, the desert, and the relationship between the pilot and the element.

> "Night flying over the charted country by the aid of instruments and radio guidance can still be a lonely business."

> "To me, desert has the quality of darkness; none of the shapes you see in it are real or permanent."

> "Night... was a world as old as Time, but as new as Creation's hour had left it."

> "You were alone when you sat and talked with the others — and they were alone."

**Markham's prose method** (from critical analysis): Markham writes in a voice that is simultaneously technical and lyrical. She never decorates; every image earns its place by being also a piece of operational information. When she describes the African landscape from the air, she is also reading it — assessing wind, terrain, the viability of emergency landing sites. The precision is not cold; it is the form her intimacy with the landscape takes. Her Africa is not exotic or symbolically charged. It is home, described with the unsentimental specificity of someone who has lived there her entire life and knows exactly where the thermals rise and where the ground is too soft to land.

## Notes for the Writer

- **Voice**: First-person retrospective. Eda tells this story from some later point, looking back. The retrospection should be subtle — not "I did not know then what I know now" but rather the quiet precision of someone who has had time to think about what she saw. The retrospective distance is Conradian; the language is Markham.

- **Pacing**: Resist the temptation to make the ground stops equally weighted. The pyrethrum shed should be the longest and most Sebaldian (the history of the pyrethrum industry radiating outward from the stopped calendar). The weather station should be briefer, more atmospheric. The trig pillar should be the most internal — Eda thinking, not looking. The Odongo scene should feel different from the others because a person is present, not just a ruin.

- **Dialect and naming**: Eda uses Swahili place names and operational English interchangeably. Josephat and Odongo speak English with Eda but the story should acknowledge (through Eda's awareness) that they also speak Luo and that the English they use with her is a colonial inheritance.

- **What NOT to do**: Do not have Eda cry, confess, apologize, or undergo moral transformation. Do not have Odongo forgive her. Do not end with a beautiful image that resolves the tension. Do not use the word "tapestry" or "testament" or "delve." Do not make the landscape a symbol. Make it a landscape.

- **The gap**: The story is about the gap between the aerial view and the ground view, and the gap should remain open at the end. The writer's job is to make the reader feel the gap — the vertigo of seeing the same place from two altitudes — without closing it.
