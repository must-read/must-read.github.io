# Story Plan: mys-espionage-001

## Assignment

- **Genre**: mystery-thriller
- **Subgenre**: espionage-spy-thriller
- **Combination ID**: mys-espionage-001
- **AuthorA**: John le Carré (dense bureaucratic realism; moral exhaustion of espionage; layered literary prose)
- **AuthorB**: Patricia Highsmith (cold clinical suspense; moral ambiguity; the banality of evil; unsettling intimacy)
- **WorkX**: *Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy* by John le Carré (institutional investigation; the mole hunt; loyalty tested by betrayal from within; debriefing as narrative mode)
- **WorkY**: *The Remains of the Day* by Kazuo Ishiguro (a life spent in service to the wrong cause; dignity as self-deception; the reckoning that comes too late)
- **Target word count**: 6,200 (range: 5,900-6,500)
- **Reading time**: 25 minutes
- **Risk card**: #10 — THE PROTAGONIST IS WRONG

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: John le Carré — *Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy* (Author A / Work X)

**Source**: Opening of the novel, Chapter 1
**Why representative**: Shows le Carré's signature method — the institutional world rendered through oblique, understated observation. The reader receives intelligence tradecraft through the mundane rhythms of a school, and the emotional weight arrives sideways, through telling omission rather than statement.

> The truth is, if old Major Dover hadn't dropped dead at Taunton races Jim would never have come to Thursgood's at all.

The narrative opens at a preparatory school where Jim Prideaux arrives mid-term as a replacement languages teacher. He drives an old red Alvis with a trailer, parks dramatically in the school grounds' depression called the Dip. Young Bill Roach, a lonely new boy, observes the arrival and becomes fascinated by this crooked, intense man with a limp and unusual mannerisms. Jim asks Roach about his abilities, and though the boy initially struggles emotionally with the question, Jim provides unexpected kindness — enlisting him to watch the trailer's levelness. Their bond begins: Roach becomes Jim's "regent-guardian," convinced they share an essential solitude.

Then the narrative shifts to George Smiley in London. Unlike Jim, Smiley appears unassuming: "Small, podgy, and at best middle-aged, he was by appearance one of London's meek who do not inherit the earth." Walking through rain, Smiley reflects on his miserable day, which culminated in a dinner with Roddy Martindale, a gossipy Foreign Office figure who pestered him with rumors about Control's death, Percy Alleline's leadership, and Bill Haydon's prominence — topics clearly painful for the recently retired intelligence officer.

**Key craft elements to absorb**: The withholding of direct statement. The way institutional knowledge is conveyed through social detail rather than exposition. The passive constructions. The careful rendering of "one account rendered" and lists — an oblique inventory of emotional damage delivered through bureaucratic paperwork.

### Sample 2: Patricia Highsmith — *The Talented Mr. Ripley* (Author B)

**Source**: Various key passages from the novel
**Why representative**: Shows Highsmith's clinical, unnerving intimacy with a morally compromised protagonist — the reader inhabits the psychology without moral guardrails. The prose is neutral, direct, almost flat, which makes the transgressive content more disturbing.

> It struck Tom like a horrible truth, true for all time, true for the people he had known in the past and for those he would know in the future: each had stood and would stand before him, and he would know time and time again that he would never know them, and the worst was that there would always be the illusion, for a time, that he did know them, and that he and they were completely in harmony and alike.

> He loved possessions, not masses of them, but a select few that he did not part with. They gave a man self-respect. Not ostentation but quality, and the love that cherished the quality. Possessions reminded him that he existed, and made him enjoy his existence.

> This was the real annihilation of his past and of himself, Tom Ripley, who was made up of that past, and his rebirth as a completely new person.

> The main thing about impersonation, Tom thought, was to maintain the mood and temperament of the person one was impersonating, and to assume the facial expressions that went with them. The rest fell into place.

**Key craft elements to absorb**: The cool, almost clinical observation of the self. The way moral transgression is rendered in the same flat register as breakfast. The sentences that spiral inward toward a philosophical abyss but never raise their voice. The absence of judgment in the prose — the reader must supply it. Highsmith's influence on le Carré's work is documented; the two share a preoccupation with impersonation, identity constructed through performance, and complicity that feels like competence.

### Sample 3: *Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy* — Structural/Narrative DNA (Work X)

**Source**: Plot structure and debriefing methodology
**Why representative**: The novel's engine is not action but reconstruction. Smiley interviews witnesses, reviews documents, traces circulation lists and cross-references institutional memory against personal testimony. The mole is found through patient reassembly of what was always visible.

The key structural elements to carry forward:
- **Debriefing as narrative mode**: Characters reconstruct events through questioning, memory, and document review. The reader assembles the picture from fragments the narrator provides without understanding their full significance.
- **Institutional investigation**: Power is readable in the circulation lists. Who signed what, who routed what memo, who was excluded from what meeting. The mole hunt is conducted through paperwork, not gunplay.
- **Loyalty tested by betrayal from within**: The deepest betrayal comes not from the enemy but from within the institution one has served. The mole is a colleague, a friend.
- **The investigator's own implication**: Smiley is not outside the story he investigates. He was married to a woman who slept with the traitor. The investigation is also a self-examination.

### Sample 4: *The Remains of the Day* by Kazuo Ishiguro — Thematic/Structural DNA (Work Y)

**Source**: Key passages and structural method
**Why representative**: Stevens' voice is the novel's engine — a man of absolute professional discipline who reveals everything the reader needs to understand his wasted life while maintaining perfect dignity. The dignity IS the blindness.

> I would say that it is the very lack of obvious drama or spectacle that sets the beauty of our land apart. What is pertinent is the calmness of that beauty, its sense of restraint. It is as though the land knows of its own beauty, of its own greatness, and feels no need to shout it.

> 'Dignity' has to do crucially with a butler's ability not to abandon the professional being he inhabits. Lesser butlers will abandon the professional being for the private one at the least provocation. For such persons, being a butler is like playing some pantomime role; a small push, a slight stumble, and the facade will drop off to reveal the actor underneath.

> You see, I trusted. I trusted in his lordship's wisdom. All those years I served him, I trusted I was doing something worthwhile. I can't even say I made my own mistakes. Really — one has to ask oneself — what dignity is there in that?

> After all, what can we ever gain in forever looking back and blaming ourselves if our lives have not turned out quite as we might have wished?

> What a terrible mistake I've made with my life.

**Key structural elements to carry forward**:
- **Self-deception as narrative voice**: The protagonist tells you everything you need to know while failing to know it himself. The gap between what he says and what the reader understands is the entire architecture.
- **Dignity as prison**: Professional competence substitutes for moral agency. The protagonist's pride in his work is inseparable from his complicity.
- **The late reckoning that never fully arrives**: Stevens comes closest to understanding at the pier scene but immediately retreats into professional purpose. The "remains of the day" — making the evening pleasant — becomes his defense against grief.
- **Service to the wrong cause**: Lord Darlington was a fascist sympathizer. Stevens devoted his life to enabling this man's agenda. The novel asks: what dignity is there in perfect service to a disgraced cause?

### Selected Concepts (2):

**1. Iron gall ink** — The protagonist, reviewing decades-old handwritten annotations in the cold case file, notices that his own notes from the 1970s have begun to eat through the paper. The iron in the ink oxidizes over decades and literally destroys the document from within. This is not a metaphor — it is a physical fact that the protagonist observes with mild professional irritation, noting that the Archives Section should have transferred these to acid-free folders years ago. **2. This memory surfaces when he's looking at a circulation list and sees a name he'd forgotten — a junior officer who was the fixed reference point in the operation, the benchmark against which other positions were measured. The benchmark was not the thing itself; it existed so that other things could be located. Like the junior officer. Like, the reader understands, the protagonist himself.

## 1. Premise

A retired MI6 officer named Aldous Foyle, seventy-three, has obtained a cold case file — the operational records of DOVETAIL, a 1974 intelligence operation in which he was a middle-ranking desk officer. The operation was officially classified as a partial failure: an East German asset was compromised and a British officer was recalled under a cloud. Foyle, who has never stopped believing the operation was mishandled by superiors, is reviewing the file methodically in his study, making notes, cross-referencing his own memory against the documentary record. He wants to establish, conclusively, that DOVETAIL's failure was attributable to a specific decision by his then-superior, Gerald Innes.

What Foyle cannot see — and what the reader gradually assembles — is that DOVETAIL succeeded exactly as designed. The East German asset was not "compromised" but deliberately sacrificed. The recalled officer was not the victim of incompetence but the fall guy in a trade engineered between London and Moscow. And Foyle himself — through his meticulous desk work, his faithful routing of memos, his tidy initialing of documents — was the mechanism that made the sacrifice possible. He was not the author of the betrayal. He was its instrument. And the file, read correctly, tells him so on every page.

The story asks: what happens when a man has built his identity on professional competence, and the evidence that his competence served someone else's purpose is sitting in front of him in his own handwriting — and he still can't read it?

---

## 2. Protagonist

**Aldous Foyle**, 73, retired MI6 officer (Grade 4, Europe Controllerate, 1966-1998). Lives alone in a semi-detached house in Camberley, Surrey, since his wife Vivian relocated to Salisbury three years ago. They speak on the phone Sundays — cordial, topical, never about anything that matters. Two grown children who visit at Christmas.

- **What he WANTS**: To prove that DOVETAIL's failure was caused by Gerald Innes's premature recall of the Bonn case officer. He wants to write a paper — nothing public, just an internal memo to the Histories Section — establishing the operational record correctly. This is about institutional truth.
- **What he NEEDS**: To confront the possibility that his entire career was built on performing tasks whose consequences were kept deliberately invisible to him. That his professional dignity — the thing he is most proud of — is indistinguishable from his complicity.
- **His flaw/blind spot**: Foyle genuinely believes that if you execute your role competently and within the rules, moral responsibility belongs to those who made the decisions. He separates execution from consequence with the rigor of a man sorting cutlery. This is not cynicism — it is a sincere conviction that the institution distributes agency, and his agency was limited to his desk, his files, his initials. The reader sees what he cannot: the institution designed itself so that men like Foyle would believe exactly this.

## 3. Structure

### Opening image/scene
Foyle at his desk in the study, mid-morning, with the DOVETAIL file open. He is making notes in a ruled pad. The study is compulsively ordered — banker's boxes labeled by year on the shelving, reference books alphabetized, his reading glasses set precisely on the right of the desk. He has been working since seven. The prose establishes his method: left to right, top to bottom, each document read in sequence, cross-referenced against a timeline he has constructed on a folding table near the window. The prose rhythm here is le Carré: dense, institutional, the language of a man who thinks in circulation lists and clearance grades. One detail early: he notices that his handwritten annotations from 1974 have begun to corrode the paper. He makes a note to mention it to Archives. The iron gall ink is eating through from the other side.

### The inciting disruption
On page forty-seven of the file, Foyle encounters a routing slip he does not remember signing. The document is a request to transfer one Thomas Harbird — the Bonn case officer — from Bonn Station to London on short notice, dated April 1974. The signature is clearly his. The routing is through his desk. But Foyle's memory of DOVETAIL is that Harbird was recalled by Innes over Foyle's objections. He checks his timeline. He had no meeting with Innes on that date. The request originated from his own desk. He did this.

His response is not shock but professional confusion. He assumes a clerical error, or that Innes gave a verbal instruction he's forgotten. He makes a note. He turns to the next document.

### The escalating middle
Foyle continues through the file, finding more evidence that the reader can interpret but he cannot:

- A letter from the East German asset, Rudi Kirschwasser, expressing concern about a security leak in Bonn — routed through Foyle's desk, initialed by Foyle, but never forwarded to the case officer. Foyle remembers this letter arriving. He remembers deciding it was "alarmist" — Kirschwasser was known for nervous exaggeration. He initialed it and filed it.

- An internal assessment of Kirschwasser's intelligence product, authored by Foyle himself, downgrading the asset's reliability rating from B2 to C3. This assessment was used to justify not investigating the security concerns. Foyle remembers writing it. He still believes it was accurate.

- A phone log showing a call between Foyle and a liaison officer in the Joint Intelligence Committee. The call lasted forty minutes. Foyle cannot remember what they discussed. He finds the gap unsettling but not suspicious.

Between file sessions, Foyle calls Vivian on Sunday. The conversation is polite, empty. She mentions finding an old Ordnance Survey benchmark on a church wall in Salisbury — did he remember when they used to notice those things? He says he does. After hanging up, he sits for a moment before returning to the file. The domestic thread runs parallel to the professional one — two operations that succeeded on their own institutional terms and failed at everything else — but the prose never draws the connection.

### The crisis point
Foyle reaches the final section of the file: the aftermath. Kirschwasser was arrested by the Stasi in September 1974. The arrest report, obtained later, indicates that the East Germans had specific knowledge of his contact schedule — information that could only have come from the Bonn Station's internal records, which passed through Foyle's desk.

Then: a supplementary file, a memo dated 1978, four years after the operation. It is a retrospective assessment written by the Head of Europe Controllerate — not Innes, but Innes's successor, Margaret Lowe. The memo is classified STRAP 3, well above Foyle's clearance at the time. It notes that DOVETAIL "achieved its primary objective" — the phrase is underlined — and that "the operational costs, including the loss of DOVETAIL-7" (Kirschwasser's cryptonym) "were anticipated and accepted at the planning stage."

Foyle reads this. He reads it again. He makes a note: *Lowe's assessment contradicts the operational summary. Possible retrospective justification. Investigate.*

That is all. The reader understands that the operation was designed to sacrifice Kirschwasser — probably as part of a trade with Moscow, or to protect a more valuable asset. Foyle's downgrading of Kirschwasser's reliability, his filing of the warning letter, his recall of Harbird — all of it was managed. The institution arranged for Foyle to do exactly what he did, using his own professionalism as the mechanism. And Foyle, reading the memo that tells him this, classifies it as an error by someone else.

### The ending
Evening. Foyle closes the file. He writes in his notepad: *DOVETAIL — preliminary conclusions. The failure of the operation is attributable primarily to the premature withdrawal of the Bonn case officer (see routing analysis, pp. 34-51) and secondarily to inadequate security protocols regarding DOVETAIL-7's contact schedule. Recommend Histories Section re-examine Innes's decision-making in light of new documentation.*

He caps his pen. He folds his reading glasses and puts them in their case. He goes to the kitchen and makes tea — Earl Grey, no milk, which is what he has drunk since Vivian left and he no longer needs to accommodate her preference for builders' tea. He carries the cup back to his study and drinks it standing at the window, looking at the quiet street. A neighbour is walking a dog. The streetlight comes on.

He does not understand what he has read. The file is complete. His conclusions are finished. He is satisfied with a day's careful work.

The final paragraph: Foyle rinses the cup, returns to the study, and places the file back in its box. He reaches for the lid. One corner of the box is damp — condensation from the unheated room overnight. He notices the moisture has softened the cardboard. The label, in his own handwriting, is beginning to bleed. He'll need to relabel it tomorrow. He puts the lid on, aligns it properly, and switches off the desk lamp.

## 4. Key Scenes

1. **The routing slip** (pp. 2-3 of the story): Foyle finds his own signature on the transfer order he blamed on Innes for thirty years. He rationalizes it immediately — verbal instruction, forgotten meeting. But the prose stutters: one sentence is shorter than it should be, a hesitation in the rhythm that the reader registers as the body knowing before the mind. This is the Highsmith moment: the clinical observation of a man not reacting to something he should react to.

2. **The Sunday phone call with Vivian** (p. 4): A conversation that does everything twice — it reveals the emptied-out marriage AND mirrors the professional relationship with the institution. Vivian mentions the Ordnance Survey benchmark. They talk about a TV programme. Neither says anything real. After hanging up, Foyle sits with his hand on the receiver for a moment longer than necessary. The prose is pure Ishiguro: the unsaid doing all the work.

3. **Foyle's assessment of Kirschwasser** (pp. 4-5): A flashback embedded in the file review — Foyle remembering writing the downgrade assessment. He recalls it as a careful, professional judgment. The prose here shifts into the le Carré institutional voice: the grammar of intelligence work, where "subject" and "object" and "asset" are human beings rendered as technical categories. The reader understands that Foyle's professional language is the mechanism of his blindness — the grammar that makes certain questions unspeakable.

4. **Margaret Lowe's retrospective memo** (pp. 5-6): The crisis document. Foyle reads the memo that tells him, in institutional language, that DOVETAIL was designed to succeed at the cost of the asset. The prose here must carry enormous weight with absolute restraint: no dramatic music, no dawning horror. Foyle reads it. He disagrees with it. He makes a note. The reader's stomach drops.

5. **The closing of the file** (final page): Foyle writes his conclusions — blaming Innes, exactly as he has believed for thirty years. He drinks tea. He notices the iron-gall ink bleeding on the label. He switches off the lamp. The story ends in an act of meticulous filing. The reader carries the grenade home.

## 5. The Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning**: The reader feels the comfort of competence. Foyle's study is ordered, his method is systematic, his mind is sharp for seventy-three. There is something reassuring about watching a professional at work. The reader trusts him — as the institution once trusted him — because he seems so careful.

**Middle**: Growing unease. Small discrepancies emerge. The reader begins to see a pattern Foyle cannot. The discomfort is not dramatic — it is the slow crawl of something wrong in a room that looks right. The reader wants to warn him. The reader also begins to suspect that warning him would change nothing.

**Ending**: The reader feels the accumulating weight of a life organized around a premise that was false. Not grief exactly — more like the sensation of watching someone carefully lock a door to an empty house. The competence that was admirable in the opening has become, by the end, the precise instrument of the protagonist's tragedy. The reader leaves with the sensation that Foyle's careful handwriting, his tidy conclusions, his properly filed box, are all part of a system that used his virtues against him — and that he is still, in the evening of his life, performing the role he was designed to perform.

## 6. Formula Integration

### Author A — John le Carré
**Concentrated passage**: The entire institutional voice of the story — the circulation lists, clearance grades, routing slips, the grammar of intelligence work. But the most concentrated le Carré moment is **Foyle's reading of his own assessment of Kirschwasser**: the dense, bureaucratic language of an intelligence evaluation that describes the reduction of a human life to a reliability rating. The prose should inhabit the institutional register so completely that the reader feels the seduction of it — how natural it is to evaluate a person in these terms, how reasonable the downgrade from B2 to C3 seems, and how monstrous it is.

### Author B — Patricia Highsmith
**Concentrated passage**: **The routing slip scene** — the moment Foyle finds his own signature and does not react. Highsmith's signature is the protagonist who observes his own transgression with clinical detachment. Here, Foyle's non-reaction is not sociopathy but self-protection so deep it reads as calm. The prose should be flat, neutral, almost affectless — the temperature drops without the narrator acknowledging it. Also present in the **final scene**: the precision of Foyle's evening routine — tea, window, neighbour's dog — has the Highsmith quality of the monstrous concealed within the orderly.

### Work X — *Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy*
**Concentrated structural element**: **The debriefing-as-investigation method.** The entire story is a one-man debrief — Foyle interrogating the file, cross-referencing documents against memory, building a timeline. As in Tinker Tailor, the investigation succeeds in the sense that the reader can reconstruct what happened, but fails because the investigator is too close. The mole-hunt structure is inverted: instead of finding the traitor, the reader finds the unwitting instrument.

### Work Y — *The Remains of the Day*
**Concentrated thematic element**: **The Sunday phone call with Vivian**, where the emptied-out marriage mirrors the emptied-out career. The Ishiguro influence is strongest here: two people being cordial about nothing, the enormous gulf between what is said and what is felt, the reader understanding that this conversation — like Foyle's file review — is a performance of normality that conceals irretrievable loss. Also present structurally: Foyle's final reflections — his satisfaction with a day's careful work — echo Stevens's retreat into the "remains of the day," the conviction that making the evening pleasant is sufficient purpose.

## 7. Title

**Clearance and Provenance**

"Clearance" works on two levels: security clearance (the intelligence world) and the act of clearing — declassification, removal, making way. "Provenance" refers to the documented history of ownership or custody — used in archives, art, and property records. Together they suggest a story about tracing where something came from and who had authority over it. The title has an institutional, documentary quality that fits the story's register. It does not start with "The," and its compound structure (X and Y) is underrepresented in the existing title corpus.

## Risk Card Integration: #10 — THE PROTAGONIST IS WRONG

The risk card is not a feature of the story. It IS the story. The entire architecture is built to demonstrate that Foyle's worldview — that competent execution within institutional rules constitutes moral adequacy — is fundamentally flawed. The story reveals this through consequences, not epiphany:

- **Consequence 1**: Kirschwasser was arrested and presumably killed because Foyle's downgrade assessment removed the institutional obligation to investigate security concerns. Foyle executed the assessment competently. The competence was the weapon.

- **Consequence 2**: Harbird, the recalled officer, had his career destroyed. Foyle's signature on the routing slip was the administrative mechanism. Foyle does not remember signing it, which is itself the point: it was routine. One more piece of paper on a desk.

- **Consequence 3**: Foyle's marriage — which he attributes to Vivian's inability to understand the demands of the service — was in fact consumed by the same mechanism. His devotion to institutional procedure crowded out everything else. The marriage did not fail dramatically; it emptied out, like a house losing furniture one piece at a time.

- **Consequence 4 (structural)**: Foyle's careful review of the file — the very activity that constitutes the story — ends with him reaching the wrong conclusion. His method is impeccable. His conclusion is wrong. The precision of his work is what prevents him from seeing the truth, because the truth would require him to read his own competence as complicity, and his professional grammar does not contain that sentence.

Foyle does NOT have a dramatic realization. He does NOT confess. He does NOT break down. He closes the file, drinks his tea, and relabels the box. The reader sees everything. Foyle sees nothing. The protagonist is wrong, and will remain wrong, and the wrongness is not a flaw in his character but the purpose for which he was designed.

## Notes for the Writer

- **Prose register**: Dense, institutional, slightly formal. The narrator speaks in Foyle's voice — close third person that inhabits his professional consciousness. Le Carré's rhythm: compound sentences, subordinate clauses, qualifications and hedges that mirror institutional language. Highsmith's temperature: cool, neutral, clinical when the emotional content is highest.

- **Do NOT**: Announce the theme. Never have Foyle think "was I used?" Never have an internal monologue where he approaches and retreats from the truth. The gap between what Foyle understands and what the reader understands must be maintained entirely through the presentation of evidence. The reader does the arithmetic.

- **The physical details matter**: The iron gall ink corroding the paper. The moisture on the box. The reading glasses. The orderly desk. The neighbour's dog. These are not metaphors — they are things. If the reader draws parallels, that is the reader's intelligence. Never do the reader's work.

- **The ending must not resolve**: Foyle's conclusions are wrong. The marriage is not addressed. Kirschwasser's fate is noted in passing. The reader leaves carrying all of it. No catharsis. No confession scene. Just a man switching off a desk lamp in a quiet house in Camberley.

- **Pacing**: The story moves at the speed of document review. This is deliberate. The tension comes not from pace but from the accumulating distance between what the reader knows and what Foyle knows. Each document widens that gap. By the end, the gap is an abyss, and Foyle is calmly writing his conclusions on the other side of it.
