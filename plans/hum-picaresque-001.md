## Research: Writing Samples & Style Analysis

### Oscar Wilde — Prose Samples & Technique

**From The Importance of Being Earnest (Act 1) — The Bunburying Concept:**

Algernon and Jack's exchange establishes the play's central idea: respectable men creating fictional personas to escape social obligation. Algernon explains: "I have invented an invaluable permanent invalid called Bunbury, in order that I may be able to go down into the country whenever I choose." Jack maintains a fictional dissolute brother named Ernest in London, while being respectable Jack in the country. The fake identity is not merely useful — it is *necessary*. "A man who marries without knowing Bunbury has a very tedious time of it."

This is the core Wilde mechanism: the lie that makes civilized life possible. Not a moral failing but a social technology.

**Lady Bracknell's Interrogation:**

When Jack reveals he was found in a hand-bag at Victoria Station, Lady Bracknell responds: "To be born, or at any rate bred, in a hand-bag, whether it had handles or not, seems to me to display a contempt for the ordinary decencies of family life..." The comedy lies in treating a question of identity as a question of luggage quality. Origin is not about blood — it's about accessories.

**The Identity-as-Costume Resolution:**

Both Jack and Algernon arrange christenings to literally change their names. Gwendolen insists she can only love someone named Ernest: "There is something in that name that inspires absolute confidence." The name IS the person. When Jack is finally revealed to genuinely be named Ernest, the fake identity turns out to have been the real one all along. "I always told you, Gwendolen, my name was Ernest, didn't I? Well, it is Ernest after all."

**From The Decay of Lying (1891 essay in dialogue form):**

Vivian argues: "Life imitates Art far more than Art imitates Life." He dismisses nature and realism both: "Enjoy Nature! I am glad to say that I have entirely lost that faculty." On consistency: "Who wants to be consistent? The dullard and the doctrinaire." On truth: "Truth is entirely and absolutely a matter of style." On contemporary fiction: Henry James "writes fiction as if it were a painful duty."

**Key Wilde Techniques Identified:**
- The epigram: a complete idea in a single sentence that inverts conventional wisdom. "The only thing worse than being talked about is not being talked about."
- Paradox as worldview: contradictions are not errors but the actual structure of reality. "Every saint has a past and every sinner has a future."
- The comedy of surfaces: what appears trivial (a name, a handbag, a buttonhole) is treated as the only thing that matters, while what appears profound (identity, morality, love) is treated as costume.
- Conversational wit: characters compete to say the cleverest thing, and the winner of any exchange is the person with the best line, not the best argument.
- Social performance as the only reality: there is no "authentic self" beneath the performance. The performance is all there is, and recognizing this is not cynicism but liberation.

### David Sedaris — Prose Samples & Technique

**From "Me Talk Pretty One Day" (title essay, 2000):**

Sedaris describes his French teacher singling him out: "Every day spent with you is like having a cesarean section." And then the pivot: "And it struck me that, for the first time since arriving in France, I could understand every word that someone was saying. It's a small step, nothing more, yet its rewards are intoxicating and deceptive." The comedy lives in the gap between the insult and the achievement — comprehension arrives as a wound.

**From "Go Carolina" (opening essay of Me Talk Pretty One Day):**

"The agent came for me during a geography lesson. She entered the room and nodded at my fifth-grade teacher, who stood frowning at a map of Europe." Sedaris begins with a dry, matter-of-fact sentence, then lets the absurdity of a speech therapist arriving like a government operative unfold through precise observation rather than commentary. The humor is in the verb "frowning at a map of Europe" — as though Europe had done something wrong.

**From Barrel Fever (1994):**

Writing about his appearance on talk shows: "Growing up, my parents were so very into themselves that I got little love and attention. As a result, I would squeeze the life out of everyone I came into contact with... I would scare away my dates on the first night... By the second date, I would arrive at the boyfriend's apartment carrying a suitcase and a few small pieces of furniture." The escalation is precise — from abstract neediness to literal furniture. The comedy is in the specificity of "a few small pieces of furniture."

**Key Sedaris Techniques Identified:**
- The conversational setup: an anecdote that begins simply and branches into unexpected territory. A story about a dinner party becomes a meditation on shame.
- Self-deprecation as connection: Sedaris exposes his own worst instincts — pettiness, vanity, judgment — and invites the reader to laugh with him, not at him.
- Family as comic material: dysfunction presented with love, exasperation, and brutal honesty, never sentimentalized.
- Comic escalation through specificity: the detail that makes the joke (the kitchen timer, the pistachio shells, the furniture on a second date).
- Deadpan delivery: presenting absurd situations with the flatness of a police report. The narrator does not signal that something is funny; the reader is trusted to notice.
- The pause that does more work than the words: timing embedded in sentence structure. Short declarative sentences after long setups.
- Vulnerability beneath the comedy: a "deep vein of sadness, vulnerability, and existential dread" that strengthens rather than undermines the humor.

### A Confederacy of Dunces — Structure & Voice

**Opening:**

"A green hunting cap squeezed the top of the fleshy balloon of a head. The green earflaps, full of large ears and uncut hair and the fine bristles that grew in the ears themselves, stuck out on either side like turn signals indicating two directions at once." Ignatius J. Reilly is introduced as a physical grotesque whose body is a comic object — not a person but a "fleshy balloon" with "turn signals."

**Ignatius's Voice:**

"In addition, I am at the moment writing a lengthy indictment against our century. When my brain begins to reel from my literary labors, I make an occasional cheese dip." The juxtaposition of grandiose intellectual ambition and cheese dip is the novel's fundamental comic register — Ignatius is simultaneously the most pompous and the most pathetic person in New Orleans.

"I refuse to look up. Optimism nauseates me. It is perverse. Since man's fall, his proper position in the universe has been one of misery." Ignatius views his own sloth and failure as philosophical positions rather than personal failings.

**Escalating Disasters:**

At Levy Pants: Ignatius is hired as a file clerk. He throws out the files rather than filing them, writes an insulting letter to a distributor and forges his boss's name, then attempts to organize the Black workers into a political revolt. Each "improvement" is more catastrophically ill-conceived than the last.

As hot dog vendor: He eats more merchandise than he sells, unknowingly becomes a mule for pornographic pictures stored in his cart, and discovers that one of the pictures features a naked woman reading Boethius — which he takes as a personal message from the universe.

**The Wheel of Fortune:**

Ignatius believes in the rota Fortunae — the medieval wheel of fortune — as a cosmological explanation for his circumstances. When things go wrong, it is Fortuna's doing. When his schemes fail, it is the century's fault. His worldview is a masterpiece of self-exculpation dressed as philosophy.

**Key Structural Techniques:**
- The deluded protagonist who views each disaster as external persecution rather than self-inflicted consequence
- Escalation: each scheme is more ambitious and more doomed than the last
- The gap between the protagonist's self-image (medieval genius) and reality (unemployed slob living with his mother)
- Multiple plotlines that converge catastrophically — Ignatius's actions create chain reactions across New Orleans
- Comedy as the collision between the protagonist's grandiose inner life and the mundane, indifferent outer world

### The Importance of Being Earnest — Thematic Architecture

**Identity as Performance:**

Every character in the play maintains at least one fictional identity. Jack is Ernest in London and Jack in the country. Algernon becomes Ernest to pursue Cecily. Cecily has maintained a fictional three-month engagement in her diary, complete with letters she wrote herself. Even Miss Prism has a secret identity as a would-be novelist who lost her manuscript (and a baby) in a moment of confusion.

**The Bunbury Principle:**

Bunburying is not mere deception — it is the maintenance of a fictional person whose existence makes your real life tolerable. The fictional invalid Bunbury allows Algernon to escape whenever he needs to. Ernest allows Jack to be irresponsible in London while remaining respectable in the country. The play argues that this is not dishonesty but *architecture* — the structure that makes social life possible.

**The Liberating Terror of Being Found Out:**

When the double identities are discovered, the women are furious — but their fury is about the *names*, not the deception. They can forgive a lie but not the wrong name. The resolution is not confession and repentance but *re-christening* — literally changing your name to match the lie. The fake becomes real through institutional ratification.

**Key Thematic Elements:**
- Social identity is entirely a performance, and the performance is the only reality
- The fictional self is often more useful, more charming, and more "real" than the authentic self
- Being "found out" is not a moral crisis but a logistical problem to be solved
- Names, titles, and social markers are the substance of identity, not decorations on top of it
- The most absurd resolution is also the truest: you ARE who you successfully pretend to be

### Selected Concepts (2):

**1. John George Bartholomew (1860-1920) — "The Prince of Cartography"**
A British cartographer who held a royal warrant as "Cartographer to the King" and was responsible for *naming Antarctica* — giving the continent its official English name. He was part of the Bartholomew cartography dynasty, six generations of Edinburgh mapmakers. A man whose job was literally to decide what places were called.

**Integration:** The fictional republic where the story is set will have been mapped and named by a minor Bartholomew — a footnote cartographer from the same dynasty — who gave the region its name during a survey expedition in the 1890s and got it slightly wrong. The village's name on British maps differs from what the locals call it, and this gap between the official cartographic name and the local name becomes a running comic motif. It mirrors the story's larger theme: the gap between what something is called and what it actually is. The attache, as a representative of Britain, inherits the cartographer's presumption — the belief that naming something gives you authority over it.

**2. Ahaetulla laudankia — the Laudankia vine snake**
A rare Indian snake named after the Odia word for "dried bottle gourd stems" because of its extraordinary camouflage — it looks so much like a dried plant stem that it was named for the thing it imitates. An animal whose identity is its disguise.

**Integration:** The fictional celebrated author's most famous novel (the one that put the village on the literary map) will be titled *The Laudankia* — a word that sounds vaguely Eastern European but is actually borrowed from this obscure Indian etymology. In the novel-within-the-story, it refers to a type of person who becomes so skilled at pretending to be something else that the pretense becomes their nature. The attache will reference this concept during her keynote speech, using the laudankia as a metaphor for authorship itself — not realizing that she is, at that moment, the perfect embodiment of the concept. The vine snake that looks so much like a plant that even its name comes from the plant, not the snake.

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

A middling British cultural attache, dispatched to a fictional Eastern European micro-republic to honor a celebrated reclusive novelist at a literary festival, discovers that the author is a collective pseudonym maintained by an entire village. Instead of reporting the fraud, she decides she can perform the author more convincingly than the committee that invented him — and she is correct, which turns out to be the worst possible thing to be right about.

The engine: What happens when a woman who has spent her career performing other people's culture discovers she's better at being someone fictional than anyone real?

### 2. Protagonist

**Philippa Quist**, 41, Third Secretary (Cultural Affairs) at the British Embassy in Budapest. Divorced. Fifteen years into a career that peaked seven years ago when she organized a Kazuo Ishiguro reading in Ljubljana that was, by her own account, "adequately attended." She tells this story at parties with the practiced timing of someone who has calculated exactly how self-deprecating to be.

**What she WANTS:** To successfully deliver the literary festival without incident, receive a commendation in her annual review, and eventually transfer to Vienna, which she has been requesting for nine years.

**What she NEEDS:** To stop treating her own life as a performance she is watching from the audience. She has become so skilled at being the diplomatic version of herself — charming, competent, pleasantly self-effacing — that she has forgotten whether there is a version underneath. The impersonation of the author is not an aberration; it is the logical endpoint of a career spent being whoever the room required.

**Her flaw:** She believes competence is a form of identity. If she can do something well, she assumes she IS the person who should be doing it. This is the Ignatius Reilly delusion turned inside out: where Ignatius believes he is a genius and the world refuses to recognize it, Philippa believes that recognition IS genius — that performing the role is the same as deserving it.

**Her specific wound:** Three years ago, her ex-husband told her during the divorce proceedings that he had "never been entirely sure she was home" — meaning present, engaged, herself. She found this hurtful and inaccurate and has been silently composing her rebuttal ever since.

### 3. Structure

**Opening image/scene (the hook):**

Philippa is in a van on a road that her GPS insists does not exist, being driven by a man named Drago who speaks seven words of English, four of which are "no problem." She is rehearsing, under her breath, the pronunciation of the celebrated author's name — Aleksandar Bogataj — which she has been saying wrong for three months and only learned this morning when Drago corrected her. The van smells of cigarettes and sheep medicine. She is holding a gift-wrapped first edition of Bogataj's novel *The Laudankia*, which she purchased on the embassy's cultural engagement budget and which she has not read. She has read the Wikipedia summary and a review in the TLS and considers this sufficient preparation, which it would have been, had the author existed.

**Inciting disruption:**

Philippa arrives at the village of Korbejn (spelled Corbein on British maps, due to a Bartholomew cartographer's error in 1893 that no one has corrected because no one has cared) to discover, through a sequence of comic accidents, that Aleksandar Bogataj is not a person but a project. The pharmacist, Josip, does mornings. The baker, Slavica, does afternoons. The retired schoolteacher, Franjo, does evening events. Each has internalized a third of the author's personality. They bicker about continuity like actors in a long-running show.

The discovery happens when Philippa spills coffee on a manuscript page and two people instinctively reach for it — Slavica from the left and Josip from the right — and they argue, in front of her, about whose scene it is.

**Escalating middle:**

Rather than expose the fraud, Philippa makes a calculation that is both rational and catastrophic: these people are terrible at being Bogataj. Their version of the author is inconsistent, nervous, and prone to contradicting himself between morning and evening events. She, who performs identity for a living, can do this better. She proposes a collaboration: she becomes the public face, they feed her the details.

The collaboration works. It works *beautifully*. Philippa's Bogataj is charming, erudite, and possessed of an accent that several journalists describe as "placeless in the most sophisticated way." She gives an interview to a Croatian literary magazine in which she explains the laudankia concept — the creature so good at pretending to be something else that it becomes the thing — and the interviewer calls it "the most penetrating self-analysis I have ever encountered in a living author."

But each success creates a new problem requiring a bigger deception:
- A German publisher wants to discuss the next novel. Philippa has to conduct a business meeting about a book that does not exist, written by a person who does not exist, while Slavica feeds her plot points through an earpiece using a walkie-talkie from the village's volunteer fire brigade.
- A doctoral student from Zagreb arrives wanting to interview Bogataj for her thesis. She has read *The Laudankia* twelve times and notices that Philippa's interpretation of the third chapter contradicts the author's 2019 letter to the Slovenian literary review — a letter Franjo wrote and has since forgotten about.
- The novel is shortlisted for a regional literary prize. Philippa must attend the ceremony in Dubrovnik.

**Crisis point:**

At the prize ceremony, Philippa delivers a speech about the solitude of the creative life. She speaks about the terror of the blank page, the feeling of being inhabited by characters more real than yourself, the loneliness of creating something from nothing. Every word is a lie, and every word is also true — she IS, at that moment, inhabited by a character more real than herself. The speech is genuinely moving. It receives a standing ovation.

Then, in the reception line afterward, a woman says to her: "You remind me so much of my daughter. She also could never figure out who she was, so she just kept becoming other people until she ran out."

Philippa excuses herself, goes to the bathroom, and stands at the mirror for four minutes.

**Ending:**

Philippa returns to the reception. The novel does not win the prize (it comes second to a Croatian memoir about beekeeping). On the van ride back to Korbejn, Drago tells her — in one of his rare complete English sentences — that his grandmother was one of the original writers. "She did the love parts," he says. "She was very good at the love parts." Philippa asks why the village created Bogataj in the first place. Drago thinks about this for a long time. "Same reason anyone writes anything," he says. "We wanted someone to be from here."

Philippa returns to Budapest. She writes her annual review. Under "Notable Achievements," she writes: "Successfully delivered the Korbejn Literary Festival. The author was well received." She considers this the most honest sentence she has written in years.

She does not return to the village. She does not expose the fraud. She does not transfer to Vienna. The unresolved thread: she keeps saying Bogataj's name correctly now, even though there is no one to say it to, and she does not know why this feels like the most important thing she learned.

### 4. Key Scenes (5)

1. **The coffee spill discovery** — Philippa accidentally reveals the fraud through a physical accident (not investigation). Two people reach for the same manuscript. The comedy is in their argument about whose scene it was, conducted in front of Philippa as though she were not there. This scene does double duty: it exposes the fraud AND reveals the depth of the villagers' emotional investment in their creation.

2. **The walkie-talkie negotiation** — Philippa meets the German publisher while receiving information from Slavica through a fire brigade walkie-talkie hidden in her scarf. Slavica's instructions are delayed because she is simultaneously pulling bread from the oven. The comedy is in the collision of registers: Philippa speaking in elegant literary platitudes while Slavica hisses details about narrative structure between timer beeps. Advances plot (the deception deepens) AND reveals that Philippa is exhilarated, not stressed — she has never felt more competent in her life.

3. **The doctoral student's trap** — The Zagreb student asks Philippa about a specific passage in chapter three that describes a woman watching a snake in a garden. Philippa improvises brilliantly. Then the student pulls out the 2019 letter to the Slovenian review, in which the author says the snake scene was actually about his mother. Philippa must reconcile two incompatible interpretations on the spot. She does — she says "an author is not obligated to tell the truth about his own work" — and the student writes this down as though it were scripture. Advances plot (the deception nearly collapses) AND reveals Philippa's capacity for thinking on her feet, which is the same capacity that makes her unable to be still.

4. **The prize ceremony speech** — The climactic set piece. Philippa speaks about creative solitude to an audience of real writers, critics, and publishers. The speech must be genuinely beautiful — not a parody of literary speeches but the real thing, delivered by a fraud, which makes it both more moving and more terrible. The standing ovation is the worst moment of her life and the best, simultaneously. Advances the plot (peak deception) AND the emotional arc (she realizes the performance has consumed the performer).

5. **Drago's grandmother** — On the drive back, Drago reveals the human origin of the collective. His grandmother "did the love parts." The village created Bogataj not for tourism or literary prizes but because they "wanted someone to be from here." This reframes the entire story: the fraud was never about deception. It was about the same need that makes any community create its myths. Advances the emotional arc (Philippa glimpses something she cannot name) AND leaves the central question unresolved: is she any different from the village?

### 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning:** The reader feels amusement and mild superiority — Philippa is a recognizable type (the competent bureaucrat slightly out of her depth), and her situation (bad roads, language barriers, a gift-wrapped book she hasn't read) is comfortably funny.

**Middle:** The amusement sharpens into something more complicated. Philippa's skill at the impersonation is genuinely impressive, and the reader begins to root for the deception even while recognizing it as absurd. The comedy shifts from laughing at Philippa to laughing with her — she knows this is ridiculous, and she does it anyway, and she's *good at it*.

**Crisis:** At the prize ceremony speech, the reader should feel a lurch — the comedy turns on a dime into something that aches. The speech is too good. Philippa has stopped performing and started meaning it, and the gap between the fraud and the sincerity has closed, and the reader should feel the same vertigo she does: where does the costume end?

**Ending:** The reader should feel something they cannot quite name — not sadness, not satisfaction, not the warmth of resolution. Something closer to the feeling of recognizing a stranger's face in a crowd and realizing it is your own reflection in a shop window. The comedy remains — the final line is funny — but it sits on top of something that does not resolve.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Oscar Wilde (epigrammatic wit, paradox, comedy of surfaces):** Most visible in Philippa's internal narration and dialogue. Her observations should crackle with Wildean inversion: the idea that the performed self is the real self, that sincerity is just another form of acting, that the best liars are the ones who believe their own lies. Specific concentration: the prize ceremony speech, where every sentence should have the polished, paradoxical quality of Wilde's best epigrams — beautiful surfaces that conceal (and reveal) the void beneath. Also: Philippa's epigrammatic dismissals of her own life ("a career that peaked adequately," "the kind of marriage that ends with a helpful division of bookshelves").

**David Sedaris (self-deprecating memoir-comedy, comic timing):** Most visible in the story's first-person-adjacent narrative voice and in Philippa's self-awareness. The Sedaris influence is the tonal register: deadpan observation, the pause that does more work than the words, the detail that makes the joke (Drago's seven words of English, the walkie-talkie from the volunteer fire brigade, the sheep medicine smell). Specific concentration: the opening van ride and the coffee spill scene, where the comedy should live in Sedaris's territory of precise, conversational, self-deprecating observation. The narrator's voice should feel like someone telling you this story at a dinner party — aware of their own absurdity, timing the beats, delivering the punchline through specificity rather than exaggeration.

**A Confederacy of Dunces (deluded protagonist, escalating comic disasters):** Most visible in the plot architecture — each success generating a bigger problem requiring a bigger deception, in an escalating chain that Philippa cannot stop because she is too good at it. The Ignatius Reilly parallel is inverted: where Ignatius fails upward through delusion (he thinks he's a genius), Philippa succeeds downward through competence (she IS good at this, and it's destroying her). Specific concentration: the three escalating complications (German publisher, doctoral student, prize ceremony), each more absurdly high-stakes than the last, each requiring a more elaborate deception to sustain.

**The Importance of Being Earnest (social performance, identity as costume):** Most visible in the thematic spine — the Bunburying principle applied to an entire village. The fake author is a collective Bunbury: a fictional person whose non-existence makes everyone's real life better. Philippa becomes a second-order Bunbury — she is pretending to be someone who was already pretend. Specific concentration: the moment when the fake identity turns out to be more "real" than the real one (the prize ceremony, where Philippa's fraudulent speech is more sincere than anything she has said as herself), mirroring Jack's discovery that he actually WAS Ernest all along.

### 7. Title

**Liability Abroad**

The title works on multiple levels: Philippa is literally a liability for her embassy; she is "abroad" in every sense — geographically, professionally, and ontologically (abroad from herself). The phrase echoes the British diplomatic register ("liability" is the kind of word that appears in cables about embarrassing incidents), and its dry bureaucratic tone matches the story's voice. It does not start with "The." It is distinct from all existing titles in the collection.

## Writing Samples for the Writer Agent

### Sample 1: Oscar Wilde — The Importance of Being Earnest

**Source:** WorkY — The Importance of Being Earnest, Act 1
**Why representative:** Shows Wilde's paradoxical wit, his treatment of identity as performance, and his epigrammatic dialogue style.

ALGERNON: You have invented a very useful younger brother called Ernest, in order that you may be able to come up to town as often as you like. I have invented an invaluable permanent invalid called Bunbury, in order that I may be able to go down into the country whenever I choose. Bunbury is perfectly invaluable. If it wasn't for Bunbury's extraordinary bad health, for instance, I wouldn't be able to dine with you at Willis's to-night, for I have been really engaged to Aunt Augusta for more than a week.

JACK: I haven't asked you to dine with me anywhere to-night.

ALGERNON: I know. You are absurdly careless about sending out invitations. It is very foolish of you. Nothing annoys people so much as not receiving invitations.

[...]

LADY BRACKNELL: To be born, or at any rate bred, in a hand-bag, whether it had handles or not, seems to me to display a contempt for the ordinary decencies of family life that reminds one of the worst excesses of the French Revolution. And I presume you know what that unfortunate movement led to?

[...]

ALGERNON: The truth is rarely pure and never simple. Modern life would be very tedious if it were either, and modern literature a complete impossibility!

GWENDOLEN: My ideal has always been to love some one of the name of Ernest. There is something in that name that inspires absolute confidence.

ALGERNON: When I am in trouble, eating is the only thing that consoles me. Indeed, when I am in really great trouble... I refuse everything except food and drink.

JACK: [About muffins] How you can sit there, calmly eating muffins when we are in this horrible trouble, I can't make out. You seem to me to be perfectly heartless.

ALGERNON: Well, I can't eat muffins in an agitated manner. The butter would probably get on my cuffs. One should always eat muffins quite calmly. It is the only way to eat them.

**Key voice qualities:** The gap between Ignatius's self-image (medieval intellectual genius, the Boethian philosopher of New Orleans) and his reality (unemployed, living with his mother, addicted to cheese dip and Dr. Nut soda).
