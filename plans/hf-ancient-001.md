## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Mary Renault — *The Last of the Wine*

**Source**: AuthorA (Mary Renault)
**Origin**: *The Last of the Wine* (1956), various passages
**Why representative**: Shows Renault's hallmark fusion of physical precision with emotional depth; the body rendered as philosophical object; queer love expressed through athletic and martial imagery.

> "great muscles like twisted oakwood gnarling his body and arms"

> His hand "lay, palm up, in the dust" and "the blood-voices cheered"

> "Your heart is too great for your body, Alexias. That is the message of the god."

> "This, then, is why I ceased to run the long-race..." and "So I set things down while I remember them."

> Alexias' grandfather is described as "buried with his old trophies and his olive crown."

**Style notes from critics**: Renault never Latinized proper names and insisted on Greek forms. Her prose has been described as "deliberate, sure of its effects, and subtle with the many points she is making." Tom Holland wrote: "No other novelist has so successfully evoked the beauty, the charisma and the terror of ancient Greece." She combined "the scholarship of a historian with the imagination of a novelist," transforming "the historical into the fictive" with "authentic flavor and color." Her research is never visible as research — "you do not feel like you are reading the author's research notes."

**Voice characteristics the writer should absorb**: Precise physical description that doubles as characterization. Measured syntax with sudden moments of intensity. Scholarly exactness worn lightly. The body as the primary medium of thought and feeling — Greek bodies wrestling, running, aging, dying. Queer desire expressed through admiration of physical form, through shared exertion, through grief at physical decline. First-person narration that is reflective but never detached from the material world.

### Sample 2: Pat Barker — *The Silence of the Girls*

**Source**: AuthorB (Pat Barker), also WorkX
**Origin**: *The Silence of the Girls* (2018), various passages
**Why representative**: Barker's stripped-back, present-tense prose; the enslaved perspective rendered through bodily experience rather than philosophical reflection; the refusal of heroic epithets.

> "Great Achilles. Brilliant Achilles, shining Achilles, godlike Achilles … How the epithets pile up. We never called him any of those things; we called him 'the butcher.'"

> "I lay there, hating him, though of course he wasn't doing anything he didn't have the perfect right to do."

> "A slave isn't a person who's being treated as a thing. A slave *is* a thing, as much in her own estimation as in anybody else's."

> "living in a bubble, no past, no future, only an endless repetition of now and now and now."

> "Because, make no mistake, this was his story — *his* anger, *his* grief, *his* story."

> "Men carve meaning into women's faces; messages addressed to other men."

> "The defeated go down in history and disappear, and their stories die with them."

> "Silence becomes a woman.' Every woman I've ever known was brought up on that saying."

> "So many pebbles on that beach—millions—all of them worn smooth by the sea's relentless grinding..."

**Voice characteristics the writer should absorb**: Short, declarative sentences. Present tense creating immediacy. The body as the site of political violence — not metaphorically but literally. Unflinching observation without self-pity. Briseis' intelligence expressed through what she *notices*, not what she articulates philosophically. Anger held in check because it can't be safely expressed, but visible in rhythm and word choice. The constant awareness of physical danger as background hum to every interaction.

### Sample 3: Marguerite Yourcenar — *Memoirs of Hadrian*

**Source**: WorkY
**Origin**: *Memoirs of Hadrian* (1951), various passages
**Why representative**: Yourcenar's hallmark: the imperial consciousness examining itself with lapidary precision; the body politic and the physical body mirroring each other; philosophical reflection rendered as sensory experience.

> "Like a traveler sailing the Archipelago who sees the luminous mists lift toward evening, and little by little makes out the shore, I begin to discern the profile of my death."

> "Like everyone else I have at my disposal only three means of evaluating human existence: the study of self, which is the most difficult and most dangerous method, but also the most fruitful."

> "Already certain portions of my life are like dismantled rooms of a palace too vast for an impoverished owner to occupy in its entirety."

> "Our Greek and Latin lands, everywhere supported by bone-structure of rock, have the trim beauty of a male body; the heavy abundance of the Scythian earth was that of a reclining woman."

> "I have done much rebuilding … These walls which I reinforce are still warm from contact with vanished bodies; hands yet unborn will caress the shafts of these columns."

> "A being deeply wounded had thrown this proof of devotion at my very face; a boy fearful of losing all had found this means of binding me to him forever."

> "Of all our games, love's play is the only one which threatens to unsettle the soul, and is also the only one in which the player has to abandon himself to the body's ecstasy."

> "The founding of libraries was like constructing more public granaries, amassing reserves against a spiritual winter."

> "Catastrophe and ruin will come; disorder will triumph, but order will too, from time to time."

> "The truth which I intend to set forth here is not particularly scandalous, or is so only to the degree that any truth creates a scandal."

**Voice characteristics the writer should absorb**: Long, periodic sentences that accumulate weight through subordinate clauses. The metaphorical habit of mapping the body onto landscape and architecture. Self-examination that is simultaneously intimate and imperial. Restrained sensuality — desire described with the precision of administrative dispatch. Mortality accepted as fact but felt as texture — in every reflection, the body's limits press against the mind's ambitions. First-person retrospection that creates a double consciousness: the man remembering and the man remembered.

### 2 Selected Concepts

**Selection 1: Melaleuca pungens** — A prickly shrub endemic to southwest Western Australia, in the myrtle family, producing spherical yellow flower heads despite (or because of) being covered in sharp spines.

**Integration**: The slave, Philo, will have specialized knowledge of plants from his childhood in the eastern provinces before enslavement. He recognizes a thorny specimen in the emperor's gardens at Tibur — brought back from distant provinces, planted as an exotic — and knows its medicinal properties (antiseptic, wound-washing). He uses this knowledge to treat the emperor's worsening skin lesions when the physicians' remedies fail, creating a moment of dependency that terrifies him. The plant becomes an image the story returns to: something prickly, defensive, that produces beauty from its own inhospitability. The emperor notices Philo's knowledge and this is what provokes the question that the slave cannot safely interpret.

**Selection 2: Martina Silva de Gurruchaga** — An Argentine patriot who earned the rank of captain for her actions during the Battle of Salta, recognized for contributions in a war she entered without the conventional credentials to participate.

**Integration**: The concept of someone earning military rank through unconventional means — a woman given a captain's title in an era that didn't recognize women's combat roles — maps onto the story's central tension about who gets recognized and how. In the story, there is a freedwoman in the city, Daphne, who runs a dye-works. She managed the business after its owner died in a provincial campaign and gained a kind of informal authority the law doesn't quite recognize. She is Philo's tenuous connection to a life outside the imperial household. Through Daphne, the story explores what it means to earn a position that the structure wasn't built to give you — and whether that position is real or a prettier form of tolerance. She exists in a legal gray zone: not enslaved, not fully free, running a business she doesn't technically own, managing workers who obey her because she's competent, not because the law says they must.

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

An aging Emperor Hadrian is dying at his villa at Tibur in 137–138 AD. His body servant, a Greek slave named Philo, has bathed, dressed, and physically supported the emperor for nine years — long enough to have witnessed the death of Antinous, the paranoid purges of Hadrian's final years, and the slow dissolution of the body he handles daily. When Hadrian begins dictating his private reflections (the proto-memoir that will become his letter to Marcus), Philo realizes the emperor is constructing a version of his reign that omits, reshapes, and beautifies — and that Philo's continued existence depends on whether the emperor considers him a witness to be tolerated or a record to be destroyed.

### 2. Protagonist

**Philo**, 31 years old, born free in Bithynia (the same province as Antinous), enslaved at 14 after his father's debts were called in by a Roman creditor. Sold twice, ending up in the imperial household at 22. Has served as Hadrian's cubicularius (body servant) for nine years. Lean, careful in movement, with the watchful stillness of someone who has calibrated the distance between safety and danger a thousand times.

- **What he WANTS**: To survive Hadrian's death and the transition to Antoninus Pius, ideally with manumission. He has calculated that his best chance is to be so functionally invisible that no one remembers to punish him, but visible enough that someone remembers to free him.
- **What he NEEDS**: To acknowledge that his nine years of intimate proximity to Hadrian have constituted a relationship — not friendship, not love, but something the language of slave and master doesn't have a word for. Something that will leave a mark on him whether or not he's freed.
- **His flaw/blind spot**: He believes his survival depends on feeling nothing. He has trained himself to observe without interpreting, to touch without registering. But the emperor's declining body has begun to produce involuntary responses in him — tenderness, revulsion, grief — that he cannot suppress and cannot safely acknowledge. He mistakes emotional discipline for emotional absence.

### 3. Structure

**Opening image/scene**: Philo is preparing the emperor's bath. The water must be a precise temperature — too hot aggravates the skin lesions, too cold makes the joints seize. He tests it with his inner wrist, the same gesture a mother uses to check a child's bathwater. The emperor is sitting on a marble bench, thinner than last month, watching Philo's hands with an expression Philo has learned not to interpret. Hadrian says: "You have a surgeon's hands, Philo. I wonder what you would have been." Philo does not answer because the question has no safe answer.

**The inciting disruption**: Hadrian begins dictating his private reflections to a secretary, but also — increasingly — speaking fragments aloud when only Philo is present. Not dictation. Something looser. Thoughts that seem to require a body in the room but not a respondent. Philo realizes he is becoming the audience for a version of Hadrian's life that the emperor is constructing in real time — a version that softens certain cruelties, reframes Antinous's death, and casts the paranoid executions of the last two years as reluctant necessities. The disruption is not that Hadrian is lying, but that Philo cannot tell whether Hadrian knows he's lying, and whether Hadrian's awareness of Philo as a witness is accidental or deliberate.

**The escalating middle**: Three complications interweave. (1) Philo's visits to Daphne, the freedwoman who runs a dye-works in the town below the villa, become a lifeline — she is the only person who speaks to him as a person rather than a function — but their relationship is endangered by the tightening security around the dying emperor's household. (2) Hadrian's body deteriorates — the dropsy worsens, the nosebleeds become more frequent, he can no longer walk unaided — and Philo's role shifts from servant to something closer to nurse. The physical intimacy intensifies. Philo must now lift the emperor, carry him, hold cloths to his face during the bleeds. The emperor's body, which was once a thing to be efficiently managed, becomes inescapable. (3) A fellow slave, a young Cappadocian called Aster who serves in the kitchens, warns Philo that other servants are planning to petition the incoming Antoninus Pius for manumission, and that they want Philo to join — but joining means drawing attention to himself, and Philo fears that attention from any direction is dangerous.

**The crisis point**: Hadrian, in a moment of clarity between bouts of pain, asks Philo a direct question: "You were in Bithynia. You would have been — what, fourteen? Fifteen? — when they brought the boy to my attention. Did you know him?" Philo did not know Antinous. But the question is not really about Antinous. It is about whether Hadrian can, at the end, have a single honest exchange with someone who has no power to refuse him — and whether that exchange is possible or whether the power differential makes honesty a category error. Philo must decide: tell the truth (he did not know Antinous but he has watched Hadrian grieve and has formed his own understanding of what happened), or perform the expected response (deference, vague affirmation, safe nothing). For the first time, he wants to speak honestly — not for the emperor's sake but for his own. Because the alternative is to have spent nine years in proximity to another human being and to have been nothing but a function.

**The ending**: Philo tells a partial truth — he did not know Antinous, but he knew boys like Antinous, boys from Bithynia who were noticed for their beauty and taken. He does not say what he thinks about Antinous's death. The emperor is quiet for a long time. Then he says something that could be gratitude or dismissal — the ambiguity sits in Philo's chest "like a stone he can't swallow and can't spit out" (the image from the author meeting). The story ends not with Hadrian's death but with Philo leaving the emperor's chamber, walking through the villa's corridors at dusk, passing the garden where the thorny eastern plants are flowering despite the cooling weather — and going to find Daphne. Not because he has been freed, or because anything is resolved, but because his body is carrying him toward the one person who will ask him how he is and mean it. The last image is his hands — the surgeon's hands — reaching for something that is not a task.

### 4. Key Scenes (5)

1. **The bath preparation** (opening): Philo testing the water, the emperor's comment about surgeon's hands, the calculus of proximity. Establishes the physical grammar of their relationship — every touch is simultaneously intimate and professional, and neither man can acknowledge the intimacy. Reveals Philo's hyper-attentive intelligence expressed entirely through bodily awareness.

2. **The dye-works visit**: Philo's hour away from the villa, walking down to the town, entering Daphne's workshop where the air smells of urine and murex and the vats stain everything purple. Daphne's hands are permanently discolored. She talks to Philo about the business, about the cost of Tyrian purple versus cheaper substitutes, about a supplier who cheated her. The conversation is ordinary and that's the point — this is what a life sounds like. Advances plot (establishes the world outside the imperial bubble) and reveals character (Philo relaxes physically in a way he never does in the villa; his sentences, even his thoughts, become longer and less guarded).

3. **The nosebleed**: Hadrian suffers a severe epistaxis in the night. Philo is summoned. The physicians have not yet arrived. Philo holds cloths to the emperor's face, tilts his head, applies pressure. Blood on Philo's hands, on the linen, on the marble floor. Hadrian grips Philo's arm — not as emperor to slave but as a body in pain reaching for the nearest body. The grip is tight, frightened. Philo feels the thinness of the arm, the give of muscle that was once hard. Something shifts between them. Neither names it. The physicians arrive and Philo is dismissed, and when he is alone he looks at his stained hands and cannot make himself wash them immediately.

4. **Hadrian's monologue**: Hadrian, propped on cushions in the garden, speaks about Antinous — not to Philo, but in Philo's presence. The monologue is beautiful, self-aware, philosophical, and — Philo realizes — a performance. A rehearsal for posterity. The grief is real but the prose is polished. Hadrian is turning a dead boy into a monument. Philo listens and thinks of the boys from Bithynia he grew up with, boys who were beautiful and were noticed and were taken, and the mothers who stood in doorways and said nothing because nothing was the only thing to say.

5. **The direct question**: The crisis scene described above. Hadrian asks about Bithynia. Philo answers. The exchange lasts two or three minutes. It is the only genuine conversation they have ever had, and neither of them is sure it happened.

### 5. The Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning**: The reader feels the claustrophobia of surveillance — every gesture monitored, every thought a potential liability. Philo's competence is impressive but also sad, because his intelligence is entirely in service of someone else's comfort. The reader should feel the specific unease of being trapped in an intimate relationship they cannot leave.

**Middle**: As Hadrian's body fails, the reader begins to feel an uncomfortable dual sympathy — for the dying man losing his dignity, and for the servant forced to witness and manage that loss. The visits to Daphne open a window of warmth and ordinariness that makes the reader feel the deprivation of the villa more acutely. When the nosebleed scene happens, the reader should feel physically present — the heat of the blood, the weight of the emperor's grip — and should not know what they feel about it.

**End**: The reader should feel the ache of something almost-spoken. Not resolution but proximity to a truth that both men circled and neither could land on. The final image — Philo's hands reaching for something that isn't a duty — should produce a feeling of painful, tentative openness. Not hope exactly. The possibility of hope. The reader puts this down feeling that they have been inside two bodies simultaneously and that neither body's experience cancels the other's.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Mary Renault (AuthorA)** — Most visible in: Philo's Bithynian memories and his internal awareness of Hadrian's physical beauty diminished by age. Renault's signature is the rendering of male bodies with scholarly precision and erotic attentiveness. When Philo remembers the emperor as he was nine years ago — the powerful frame, the deliberate movements, the face that was handsome in the Roman way, which is different from the Greek way — this is Renault's lens. Also in the bath scene: the physical description of the aging body rendered with the same precision Renault brought to athletic bodies, but inverted — beauty's residue rather than beauty's peak.

**Pat Barker (AuthorB)** — Most visible in: Philo's sections throughout. The spare, present-tense-inflected prose. The intelligence expressed through bodily calibration rather than philosophical reflection. Barker is the engine of Philo's voice: short declarative sentences, the constant awareness of power's physical reality, the refusal of self-pity. Particularly concentrated in the nosebleed scene and the dye-works visit — moments where the body speaks and the mind follows.

**The Silence of the Girls / WorkX** — Most visible in: The structural architecture. The canonical figure (Hadrian, like Achilles) retold from the perspective of someone the canonical narrative treats as furniture. Philo is Briseis: the person in the room who the epic doesn't see. The structural echo is most pointed in Scene 4 (the monologue) — where Hadrian constructs his own myth and Philo watches, and the reader understands that "this was his story — his grief, his story" and the slave's experience exists in the margins of that grief, unmonumented.

**Memoirs of Hadrian / WorkY** — Most visible in: The passages where Hadrian speaks or thinks. His sections should carry Yourcenar's DNA: the long, elaborately subordinated sentences, the metaphorical habit of mapping the body onto landscape and empire, the self-examination that is simultaneously intimate and imperial. Concentrated in Scene 4 (the monologue about Antinous) and the crisis scene. The thematic footprint — power examined from its own vantage point, the body's decline as political metaphor — shapes the entire narrative. The story asks: what does it mean to rule the world and be unable to rule your own flesh?

### 7. Title

**Grip and Surrender**

Rationale: Drawn from the author meeting's central image — "the story lives in the grip." The title captures the dual meaning: the physical grip of servant holding emperor, and the larger question of who holds and who releases. It does not start with "The." It uses a compound form (X and Y) that echoes the story's dual-consciousness structure without being too neat about it. "Surrender" carries both military/imperial and intimate/physical connotations, fitting a story about an empire's limits and a body's limits simultaneously.
