# Story Plan: Unfinished Depositions

**Genre**: Literary Fiction / Epistolary-Fragmented Narrative
**Target**: ~5,500 words (5,200–5,800 acceptable)
**Reading Time**: 22 minutes
**Risk Card**: None

## Formula Sources

- **AuthorA — Javier Marías**: Long, digressive sentences circling secrets. Subordinate clauses that delay meaning. The narrator who knows more than they admit. A Heart So White's opening: "I did not want to know but I have since come to know..." — that construction of reluctant knowledge arriving anyway.
- **AuthorB — Rachel Cusk**: Austere observation. Characters revealed through what they say, not what the narrator tells us. Outline's method: other people's stories refract the narrator's unspoken situation. Minimal authorial commentary. The details that feel minor and are not.
- **WorkX — Austerlitz by Sebald**: Documents, architecture, photographs as evidence and silence simultaneously. The accumulation method — fragments accruing meaning through juxtaposition rather than argument. Non-linear, recursive, with the weight of history pressing through objects.
- **WorkY — A Visit from the Goon Squad by Egan**: Time transforms everything. The same document, the same event, means something entirely different across decades. Formal innovation in structure — PowerPoint chapters, temporal leaps. Each section could stand alone but accretes into a whole.

## Wikipedia Concepts

### Concept 1: Iron Gall Ink Degradation
Iron gall ink — the standard writing ink from the Middle Ages through the mid-20th century — slowly corrodes the paper it is written on. The iron sulfate reacts with cellulose over decades, causing the ink to eat through the document from the inside. A signature written in iron gall ink is literally destroying the paper that bears it. The darker the ink, the deeper the corrosion. Documents can develop holes exactly where the pen pressed hardest — where the signature was most emphatic.

**Integration**: The grandfather's signature on the execution orders. One of the siblings — Nuria, the archivist — notices that the ink on the oldest documents has begun to eat through the paper. The signatures are becoming holes. The metaphor writes itself, but it must arrive as physical observation, not as metaphor: Nuria sees the corrosion because she knows what iron gall ink does to cellulose, because she is a trained paper conservator. She describes it clinically. The reader feels the metaphor. The story never announces it.

### Concept 2: Spirit Duplicator (Hectograph) Copies
Spirit duplicators produced copies in distinctive aniline purple ink that faded over time. Each successive copy was lighter than the last. By the fortieth copy, the text was barely legible — a ghost of the original. Common in bureaucratic and educational settings in Spain through the 1960s.

**Integration**: The grandmother's second memoir attempt (1995) is typed, but attached to it are spirit-duplicated copies of what appear to be classroom attendance records from a village school — names of children in fading purple ink. The grandmother was a schoolteacher. These lists, buried among her memoir drafts, are never explained. One of the siblings notices that several names on the attendance records appear on the execution orders signed by the grandfather. The grandmother kept the children's names. She never says why. The purple ink has faded to near-invisibility, as if the document is erasing itself.

## 1. Premise

Three adult siblings — Nuria (45), Tomás (42), and Inés (38) — are clearing their grandmother Consuelo's apartment in Madrid after her death at 96. In a desk drawer lined with yellowed newspaper, they discover a folder of Franco-era judicial documents: execution orders, sentencing records, and procedural filings, all bearing the signature of their grandfather, Esteban Arrieta Goicoechea, a municipal judge in Teruel province from 1939 to 1952. He died in 1987 and was remembered by the family as a gentle man who grew tomatoes and never raised his voice. Alongside the judicial papers, they find three separate beginnings of Consuelo's memoir — from 1978, 1995, and 2018 — each one abandoned after a few pages. The story asks: what does a family owe to the dead whose names appear on the papers, and at what point does knowing become its own form of violence?

## 2. Protagonist

**Inés Arrieta Moreno**, 38, an environmental lawyer in Barcelona who handles EU regulatory compliance. She is the youngest, the least certain, the one still reading. She has a six-year-old daughter, Lucía, whose existence makes the question of what to pass down feel immediate rather than abstract.

- **What she WANTS**: To understand what the documents mean — not historically (she can Google that) but within the family. Why did her grandmother keep them? Why three memoirs? Why not destroy them or hand them to an archive?
- **What she NEEDS**: To accept that understanding may not be available — that her grandmother's three abandoned memoirs are not failures but the most honest response to an impossible situation, and that Inés's own compulsion to "figure it out" is another version of the control she exerts in every area of her life.
- **Her flaw**: Inés believes that sufficient evidence yields a verdict. She is a lawyer. She thinks the documents, properly arranged, will tell her what to feel. She is wrong. The documents will tell her facts, and facts are not the same as understanding.

## 3. Structure

The story is told entirely through documents, text messages, and memoir fragments. No conventional narration. No scene-setting. The reader assembles the narrative from:

- **Judicial documents** (1941–1951): Execution orders, sentencing records, procedural filings. Described with absolute physical precision — paper quality, typeface, ink, signature pressure. Presented as if being catalogued by someone trained to see paper, not just words.
- **Consuelo's three memoir fragments**: 1978 (five pages, handwritten, abandoned mid-sentence), 1995 (three pages, typed, with spirit-duplicated school records tucked between the pages), 2018 (one paragraph, dictated to a phone and transcribed, the closest she comes to saying the thing directly).
- **Text messages between the siblings**: Time-stamped. Real-time processing. Deletions noted. The immediacy of people who have not yet constructed a position.
- **One formal letter**: From Nuria to a paper conservation service, requesting an assessment of the judicial documents' condition. Professional, clinical. The most emotional document in the story, because what it does not say is louder than what it says.
- **One email draft**: From Tomás to a journalist at El País. Never sent. Saved in drafts for three weeks before being deleted. Recovered by Inés from his laptop's trash folder.

### Beat Structure:

**Beat 1 — The Drawer (Discovery)**
Opens with Nuria's text to the sibling group chat: a photograph of the open drawer, no caption. Then Inés's response: "What is that?" Then Tomás's: "Don't touch anything." Followed by the first judicial document, described with the clinical precision of Nuria's trained eye — she is a paper conservator at the Archivo Histórico Nacional in Madrid. She notes the paper stock (laid paper, watermarked with the Francoist eagle), the typeface (a Hispano-Olivetti Lexikon 80), the ink (iron gall, showing early signs of corrosion around the signature). The grandfather's signature: steady, unhurried. The pen pressed firmly. The date: 14 March 1943. Below the signature, a name that means nothing to the siblings yet.

**Beat 2 — The Memoirs (The Grandmother's Three Attempts)**
The siblings find the three memoir beginnings. We read excerpts from each:

*1978 fragment* — Consuelo writes about the house in Teruel, about the light in the kitchen at four in the afternoon, about the way Esteban would come home and wash his hands before sitting down to eat. She writes around the thing without touching it. The prose is careful, decorative, full of weather and furniture. She stops mid-sentence: "The thing about the years between the war and the—"

*1995 fragment* — Typed, crisper, less evasive. Consuelo writes about a specific night in 1946 when she heard Esteban on the telephone, speaking in the formal register he used only for court, and she understood — not from the words, which she could not hear clearly, but from the register — that someone was going to die. Tucked between the pages are the spirit-duplicated attendance records in fading purple ink. Names of children. No explanation.

*2018 fragment* — One paragraph, dictated. Consuelo says: "Esteban signed them because they were put in front of him, and he did not sign them because he was cruel, and he did not sign them because he was a coward, and I cannot tell you why he signed them because in sixty years of marriage I never asked, and not asking was the thing that held every other thing in place."

**Beat 3 — The Argument (Positions Harden)**
Text messages escalate. Tomás wants to contact El País. He sends links to the 2022 Democratic Memory Law, to articles about the Asociación para la Recuperación de la Memoria Histórica. He types: "We have an obligation." Nuria responds: "To whom?" Inés reads but does not respond for eleven hours. Then she sends: "The ink on the March 1943 order is eating through the paper. In ten years the signature won't exist. The document is destroying itself."

Tomás drafts the email to the journalist. We read it. It is eloquent, righteous, historically informed. It is also performing something — the draft reveals a man constructing himself as the grandson-who-chose-justice, and the performance is visible in the prose. He saves it to drafts. He does not send it.

**Beat 4 — The School Records (The Thing That Changes Everything)**
Inés, unable to sleep (the text messages from this section are all time-stamped between 1:47 a.m. and 3:22 a.m.), goes back through the 1995 memoir fragment and examines the spirit-duplicated school records. She cross-references the names. Four names appear on both the classroom attendance lists and the execution orders. Children whose teacher was the wife of the man who signed their parents' death sentences. Consuelo taught the children of the people Esteban condemned. She kept the attendance records. She never wrote this in any of the three memoirs.

Inés photographs the overlapping names and sends them to the group chat at 2:58 a.m. No caption, no comment. Just the photographs. Tomás responds at 3:04 a.m.: "Jesus." Nuria responds at 7:15 a.m.: "I already knew. I noticed when I first catalogued the papers."

Then the gap in the text chain: fourteen hours of silence. The longest silence in the story.

**Beat 5 — The Letter and the Open End**
Nuria writes her letter to the paper conservation service. It is professional, meticulous, utterly controlled. She describes the collection: "Eleven judicial documents, 1941–1951, various paper stocks, iron gall ink throughout, moderate foxing, early-stage ink corrosion on three items." She requests a preservation assessment. She asks about deacidification treatment options. She asks about digitization. The letter reads as if she has decided to preserve the documents — but it is a conservation request, not a moral statement. She is preserving the paper. Whether she is preserving the truth or preserving the wound is left entirely to the reader.

The story ends with Inés's final text, sent to no one — typed and then deleted. We see it only because the narrative form includes deleted messages. It reads: "She taught their children. Every day. For years. And she never —" The message stops there. Deleted at 11:42 p.m. The story does not resolve whether the documents are published, burned, or archived. The third sibling is still reading.

## 4. Key Scenes

- **The drawer photograph**: The text message with no caption. The image of the open drawer. The moment before anyone knows what they are looking at, but the photograph already tells the reader (through Nuria's subsequent description) everything about the paper, the ink, the institutional weight of the documents. This scene establishes the epistolary form and introduces Nuria's clinical eye as the story's primary sensory instrument.

- **Consuelo's 1978 fragment stopping mid-sentence**: The handwritten memoir dissolving into an em-dash. The reader feels the flinch. What follows the dash — "the years between the war and the" — is the gap the entire story lives inside. This is where Marías's influence is most visible: the sentence that cannot reach its own ending.

- **The 2:58 a.m. photographs of the overlapping names**: Inés alone, cross-referencing lists. The discovery that Consuelo taught the orphans her husband created. No narration. No reaction shot. Just the photographs sent into the group chat, and the silence that follows. This is the crisis point — not because it changes the facts, but because it makes the grandmother's silence legible in a new way. She wasn't protecting herself. She was holding together something that would destroy everyone if it fell apart.

- **Tomás's unsent email**: The reader watches a man construct a moral argument in real time and then fail to send it. The email reveals more about Tomás — his need to be the righteous one, his half-awareness that righteousness is also a performance — than any direct characterization could. This scene operates like a Cusk conversation: the character reveals himself through what he says, and the reader sees what he cannot.

- **Nuria's conservation letter**: The climax is not a confrontation but a professional request. Nuria asks for deacidification treatment. She is asking, in the language of paper conservation, whether the documents can be saved. The question beneath the question — whether the family can be saved from what the documents contain — is never spoken.

## 5. The Emotional Trajectory

The reader begins in **curiosity** — the discovery of hidden documents, the genre pleasure of secrets revealed. This modulates into **dread** as the judicial documents are described with Sebald-like precision and the weight of what the grandfather did becomes concrete. The grandmother's memoir fragments shift the feeling to **grief mixed with frustrated sympathy** — the reader wants Consuelo to finish the sentence, to say the thing, and feels the same compulsion Inés feels to arrange the fragments into meaning.

The discovery of the overlapping names — children in Consuelo's classroom whose parents Esteban killed — produces something harder to name: a recognition that the grandmother's silence was not cowardice but a daily, impossible negotiation between knowledge and survival. The reader should feel the floor shift: not a revelation that changes what happened, but one that changes what silence meant.

By the end, the reader should feel **the specific helplessness of having understood something that cannot be acted on** — the same helplessness that drove Consuelo to start three memoirs and abandon each one. Not devastation. Not catharsis. The feeling of holding documents that are slowly destroying themselves, knowing that preserving them and destroying them are both moral acts, and neither one is sufficient.

## 6. Formula Integration

**Marías (AuthorA)** — Most visible in: Consuelo's 1978 memoir fragment. Her prose — handwritten, exploratory, circling — should read like a Marías sentence translated into an elderly Spanish woman's voice. Long subordinate clauses that delay arrival at meaning. The way she describes the light in the kitchen at four in the afternoon for a full paragraph before approaching the thing she cannot say. The sentence that stops mid-clause: "The thing about the years between the war and the—" This is Marías's rhythm: the approaching truth that the sentence cannot complete.

**Cusk (AuthorB)** — Most visible in: Tomás's unsent email to El País. It reads like a monologue from Outline — a man revealing himself through what he says while believing he is making an argument about something else entirely. Also in the text message exchanges, where the siblings' characters emerge through their word choices, response times, and what they choose not to say. Cusk's principle: people tell you who they are if you let them talk long enough.

**Austerlitz / Sebald (WorkX)** — Most visible in: Nuria's descriptions of the judicial documents. The physical precision — watermarks, typefaces, ink chemistry, paper corrosion — is pure Sebald: objects carrying the weight of history in their material composition. Also in the overall structure: fragments, documents, photographs, the accumulation method that builds meaning through adjacency rather than argument. The spirit-duplicated school records in fading purple ink — documents that are erasing themselves — belong to Sebald's world of evidence that is also disappearance.

**A Visit from the Goon Squad / Egan (WorkY)** — Most visible in: the time-layering. The same family, the same house, the same silence, refracted across 1943, 1978, 1995, 2018, and the present. Each era's documents carry a different relationship to the past. The 1943 execution order is bureaucracy; the 1978 memoir fragment is the first attempt to make it memory; the 2018 dictated paragraph is the final, stripped-down version of a truth that took sixty years to become speakable. Time has not healed anything — it has transformed the injury into something different at every stage. Also in the structural variety: texts, emails, handwritten fragments, typed pages, formal letters — each format carrying its own relationship to time and self-presentation, like Egan's PowerPoint chapter.

## 7. Title

**Unfinished Depositions**

The title works on three levels: (1) Consuelo's three unfinished memoir attempts — depositions she could not complete; (2) the legal sense of "deposition" — sworn testimony, witness statements — connecting to the grandfather's judicial documents; (3) the geological sense — deposition as sedimentation, layers accumulating over time, the way the documents are literally deposited in the drawer, each layer from a different era. The title does not start with "The." It is two words, both of Latin origin, both carrying legal and geological weight. It suggests something left incomplete — which is exactly where the story ends.

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Javier Marías — A Heart So White (opening)
**Source**: AuthorA
**Origin**: A Heart So White, Chapter 1, opening paragraph (Margaret Jull Costa translation)
**Why representative**: The single-sentence opening that delays and delays, accumulating detail through subordinate clauses, approaching a death through domestic specificity.

> "I did not want to know but I have since come to know that one of the girls, when she wasn't a girl anymore and hadn't long been back from her honeymoon, went into the bathroom, stood in front of the mirror, unbuttoned her blouse, took off her bra and aimed her own father's gun at her heart, her father at the time was in the dining room with other members of the family and three guests."

**Writer note**: This rhythm — reluctant knowledge arriving in a single unstopping sentence — should inform Consuelo's memoir fragments, especially the 1978 handwritten attempt. The narrator knows, does not want to know, has come to know anyway. Every subordinate clause is a small delay, a small evasion, a small breath before the sentence reaches the thing it cannot avoid.

### Sample 2: Rachel Cusk — Outline (opening chapter)
**Source**: AuthorB
**Origin**: Outline, Chapter 1 (published excerpt, Paris Review)
**Why representative**: The narrator as listening device. Other people reveal themselves through their own monologues. Minimal authorial commentary. Austere observation of detail (the glass cup, the nylon stockings).

> "Before the flight I was invited for lunch at a London club with a billionaire I'd been promised had liberal credentials."

And the method: the narrator's seatmate on a plane to Athens tells her about his family, his marriages, his children. The narrator records everything — what others tell her, the way an airplane meal looks, the orderly décor of an Athens apartment she's visiting. She transforms everything she observes into some more resonant version of itself. Eyebrows like "grasses in a rocky plain"; unresponsiveness "like an armchair."

**Writer note**: This method should shape the text messages and especially Tomás's unsent email. Characters reveal themselves by what they say. Inés, as the listening sibling (the one who reads and does not respond for eleven hours), is structurally the Cusk narrator — defined by what accumulates around her, by her outline rather than her fill.

### Sample 3: W.G. Sebald — Austerlitz (opening pages)
**Source**: WorkX
**Origin**: Austerlitz, p. 1 and p. 14 (Anthea Bell translation)
**Why representative**: The meditative, recursive sentence that moves through architectural and documentary detail toward historical weight. The narrator encountering another person whose knowledge becomes "a gradual approach to a kind of historical metaphysic."

> "In the second half of the 1960s I travelled repeatedly from England to Belgium, partly for study purposes, partly for other reasons which were never entirely clear to me, staying sometimes for just one or two days, sometimes for several weeks."

> "From the first I was astonished by the way Austerlitz put his ideas together as he talked, forming perfectly balanced sentences out of whatever occurred to him, so to speak, and the way in which, in his mind, the passing on of his knowledge seemed to become a gradual approach to a kind of historical metaphysic, bringing remembered events back to life."

**Writer note**: This should inform Nuria's descriptions of the documents. The clinical precision that becomes, through accumulation, a way of approaching trauma without naming it. Nuria describes paper the way Austerlitz describes railway stations — as structures that carry more than they were designed to hold. The raccoon in the Antwerp zoo, "washing the same piece of apple over and over again, as if it hoped that all this washing would help it escape the unreal world in which it had arrived" — this is the motion of the grandmother's three memoirs, washing the same story over and over, hoping it will come clean.

### Sample 4: Jennifer Egan — A Visit from the Goon Squad (structure and time)
**Source**: WorkY
**Origin**: Novel structure and critical descriptions
**Why representative**: The way each chapter shifts perspective, time period, and even format (including a chapter as PowerPoint slides), while the central subject — what time does to people — accretes through juxtaposition rather than linear narrative.

The novel's central metaphor: "Time's a goon" — time as the force that robs characters of their youth, innocence, and success, but also transforms memory itself. The same event, encountered in a different chapter from a different character's perspective in a different decade, means something entirely different. Egan's innovation is not just temporal jumping but formal variety — each chapter finds its own best container.

**Writer note**: Each document type in "Unfinished Depositions" should feel like an Egan chapter — formally distinct, self-contained, but accreting meaning when placed beside the others. The execution order and the text message occupy entirely different relationships to time, language, and self. The judicial document is frozen bureaucracy; the text message is liquid, instantaneous, already disappearing. The memoir fragments exist somewhere between — private attempts to convert experience into narrative, each one finding a different failure mode. The variety of formats IS the Egan influence.

## Specific Details for the Writer
- The apartment is on Calle de Atocha, near the Reina Sofía museum. Fifth floor, no elevator. The building has a portería with a glass-and-iron door.
- The drawer is in a secretary desk (a *secreter*) made of walnut, which has been in the family since Teruel.
- Consuelo's handwriting in the 1978 fragment uses the old-style Spanish cursive taught in the 1930s — looping, formal, with the characteristic reversed-direction capital letters.
- The execution orders are stamped with the seal of the Juzgado de Instrucción (Court of First Instance) of a town in Teruel province. The town should be small: Calamocha or Montalbán.
- The Hispano-Olivetti Lexikon 80 typewriter: standard issue in Spanish government offices from the late 1930s through the 1960s. The typeface has a distinctive squared-off lowercase 'a'.
- Consuelo's 2018 dictated paragraph is transcribed by her home aide and contains the aide's transcription errors — small misspellings, a missed word — which make it feel real.
- The spirit-duplicated school records (purple/mauve ink, fading) show children's names, attendance marks, and the year: 1944–1945. Some names are nearly illegible.
