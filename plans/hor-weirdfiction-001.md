# Story Plan: hor-weirdfiction-001

## Combination Spec

- **Genre**: Horror / Weird Fiction
- **AuthorA**: Robert Aickman — "Strange stories" that resist explanation; deeply unsettling ambiguity; the uncanny as art form
- **AuthorB**: Carmen Maria Machado — Surreal, queer, formally inventive horror; the body as site of terror and desire; fairy-tale structures
- **WorkX** (structure): *House of Leaves* — Nested narratives within narratives; documentary evidence that contradicts itself; the text as labyrinth
- **WorkY** (themes): *The Lottery* — Communal violence accepted as routine; the horror of what people agree not to question; ordinary settings made monstrous through social consensus
- **Target**: 4,800 words (±300) / 19 min read
- **Risk Card**: MULTIPLE VOICES — 2-3 distinct narrators whose accounts overlap, contradict, or illuminate each other. Each voice is formally distinguishable. The truth lives in the gaps between their versions.

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Robert Aickman (AuthorA)

**Source**: Various stories, compiled from critical excerpts
**Why representative**: Demonstrates Aickman's method — precise mundane detail that accumulates into wrongness, sentences that seem ordinary but carry an undertow of the inexplicable.

From "The Swords": "I have noticed several times that it is to beginners that strange things happen, and often, I think, to beginners only." / "There was nothing gleaming about them, and nothing decorative."

From "The Hospice": "It was somewhere at the back of beyond." / Maybury's experience of a place where the food is wrong — too much, keeps coming, the host keeps insisting — and you eat it because your host is watching. The wrongness is never explained but the stomach knows.

From "Into the Wood": "One had to lose the noise of the mechanism, not least the ever-deafening inner echoes of it..." / "Dreams … are misleading, because they make life seem real. When it loses this support of dreams, life dissolves."

From "The Unsettled Dust": "It is strange that people train themselves so carefully to go to waste so prematurely."

From "The Fetch": "It is amazing how full a life a man can lead without for one moment being alive at all..."

From "My Poor Friend": "'Teeth,' said Enright, dropping the toy on the floor. 'Just teeth.'" / "'You mean there's something unusual about the children?' 'They're not human at all. They ought not to live. But my wife naturally doesn't see it like that.'"

From "The Inner Room": "It was not merely an out-of-fashion shop, but a shop that at best sold too much of what no one wanted." / "But that perhaps was the worst of it: I was plainly not dreaming now."

**Key Aickman techniques for the writer**:
- Social obligation as horror mechanism — you participate because someone is watching
- Mundane specificity (trains, hotel rooms, meals) that suddenly tilts
- Stories that refuse to explain themselves; the ambiguity IS the substance, not the shell
- Characters who are deeply polite even as the world distorts around them
- Understated British register that makes the wrongness more uncanny by contrast

### Sample 2: Carmen Maria Machado (AuthorB)

**Source**: "The Husband Stitch" (*Her Body and Other Parties*)
**Why representative**: Shows Machado's formal inventiveness, fairy-tale structures embedded in realistic fiction, the body as document, and the blurring of story and instruction.

Opening voice instructions: "If you read this story out loud, please use the following voices: Me: as a child, high-pitched, forgettable; as a woman, the same. The boy who will grow into a man, and be my spouse: robust with his own good fortune."

"In the beginning, I know I want him before he does."

"'Oh, this?' I touch the ribbon at the back of my neck. 'It's just my ribbon.'… 'You shouldn't touch it,' I say. 'You can't touch it.'"

"I once heard a story about a girl who requested something so vile from her paramour that he told her family and they had her hauled off to a sanatorium."

"'Anything could move out there in the darkness,' I think. A hook-handed man. A ghostly hitchhiker forever repeating the same journey. An old woman summoned from the repose of her mirror by the chants of children."

"Brides never fare well in stories. Stories can sense happiness and snuff it out like a candle."

"When you think about it, stories have this way of running together like raindrops in a pond. Each is borne from the clouds separate, but once they have come together, there is no way to tell them apart."

"I have heard all of the stories about girls like me, and I am unafraid to make more of them."

"They certainly bloodied her breasts, but she did not mind, because they were hers and only hers."

"I have always been a teller of stories."

**Key Machado techniques for the writer**:
- The body as narrative — flesh that tells a different story than words
- Fairy-tale interpolations woven into the realistic frame: "I once heard a story about..."
- Direct address and reading instructions that break the fourth wall while deepening intimacy
- Desire as both vulnerability and power; queerness as a structural principle, not a label
- The specific, visceral physical detail: knuckle-deep, bloodied breasts, the ribbon
- Stories that name themselves as stories without losing their power

### Sample 3: *House of Leaves* by Mark Z. Danielewski (WorkX — Structure)

**Source**: Opening pages and key passages
**Why representative**: Demonstrates the nested narrative architecture, the documentary-academic voice, and the way contradicting accounts create an impossible space.

Johnny Truant's introduction: "I still get nightmares. In fact, I get them so often I should be used to them by now. I'm not. No one ever really gets used to nightmares."

Truant's warning to the reader: "With a little luck, you'll dismiss this labor, react as Zampano had hoped, call it needlessly complicated, pointlessly obtuse, prolix — your word —, ridiculously conceived, and you'll believe all you've said, and then you'll put it aside — though even here, just that one word, 'aside,' makes me shudder, for what is ever really just put aside? — and you'll carry on, eat, drink, be merry and most of all you'll sleep well. Then again there's a good chance you won't. This much I'm certain of: it doesn't happen immediately..."

Zampanò's fragment: "Little solace comes to those who grieve when thoughts keep drifting as walls keep shifting and this great blue world of ours seems a house of leaves moments before the wind."

**Key structural principles for the writer**:
- Nested frames: Zampanò narrates the Navidson Record, Truant narrates Zampanò, editors annotate Truant. Each layer contradicts the one below it.
- The impossible architecture: the hallway is longer than the house that contains it. Measurements change depending on who measures.
- The central object (the house) remains consistent even as every description of it contradicts. The narrators distort, but the thing at the center is real.
- Documentary apparatus (footnotes, cross-references, academic citations) that looks authoritative but undermines itself.
- The reading experience itself becomes spatially disorienting — the text IS the labyrinth.

### Sample 4: "The Lottery" by Shirley Jackson (WorkY — Themes)

**Source**: Full story (1948, *The New Yorker*)
**Why representative**: The foundational text for communal violence as routine — the horror of what people agree not to question.

Key elements and passages:
- Opens with weather: "The morning of June 27th was clear and sunny, with the fresh warmth of a full-summer day; the flowers were blossoming profusely and the grass was richly green." The ordinariness is the point.
- The children gathering stones: "Bobby Martin had already stuffed his pockets full of stones, and the other boys soon followed his example, selecting the smoothest and roundest stones."
- The reportorial flatness of the narration — county fair tone applied to ritual murder.
- Old Man Warner as the voice of tradition: "Lottery in June, corn be heavy soon." Ritual justified by proverb.
- Tessie Hutchinson's protest is not ethical but procedural: "It isn't fair, it isn't right" — she objects to the process, not the principle. She would have thrown stones at someone else.
- The child is given pebbles to throw at his mother. Little Davy Hutchinson. The community reproduces itself.

**Key Jackson techniques for the writer**:
- The flat, reportorial voice that describes horror as if it were routine — because for the community, it IS routine
- Social dynamics rendered precisely: who arrives first, who stands where, who speaks up
- The horror is not hidden — it's conducted in sunshine. No secrets, only consensus.
- The victim who does not run. She could. Nothing physical prevents it. She stays because the communal reality is more real than survival.
- The mechanism of ordinary hands: picking up stones, drawing slips, the physical apparatus of communal violence

### 2 Selected Concepts

**1. Aphanothece** (invisible box/sheath cyanobacteria)
**Integration**: The community in the story is located near thermal springs or a mineral pool where colonies of cyanobacteria grow — blue-green mats that form in warm, alkaline water. The narrator who documents the community uses the name *Aphanothece* when describing the growth in the communal bathing pool or cistern where the ritual occurs. The word itself — from Greek for "invisible sheath" — becomes a recurring motif: the thing that encloses the community, visible only under magnification, named only by someone who has studied it closely. The bacteria's ability to form colonies from individual cells mirrors the story's concern with how individual bodies become a collective organism. The thermal spring setting also provides the physical site for the ritual — warm water, mineral smell, the blue-green color of the growth giving the pool an uncanny appearance.

**2. Eduardo e Cristina (Rossini's pastiche opera, 1819)**
**Integration**: One of the narrators — the academic or documentary voice — references this opera in a footnote or aside when discussing the community's annual festival. The key detail: Rossini composed the opera in extreme haste by cannibalizing 19 of 26 musical numbers from his own previous works, creating something "new" from recycled pieces. The opera was a huge success despite — or because of — being assembled from pre-existing parts. This becomes a metaphor for the community's ritual itself, which is described as having been assembled over generations from older practices, none of which individually seem violent, but which together produce something monstrous. The narrator notes that the community's festival, like Rossini's opera, is a pastiche that nobody recognizes as borrowed — the parts have been so thoroughly recombined that they appear original. The detail also serves as structural commentary on the story itself, which is built from four literary sources.

## 1. Premise

In a small community on the English coast near a cluster of thermal springs, residents participate in an annual ritual called the Attending — a communal ceremony centered on a warm, mineral-rich pool where blue-green colonies of *Aphanothece* bloom in the alkaline water. Three separate documents attempt to describe what happens during the Attending: a personal account by a woman who participated and then left the community, a quasi-academic investigation by an outsider who visited briefly and took photographs that don't match any of the other accounts, and a collective statement from the community itself, written in the first-person plural. Each document describes the same evening. None of them agree on what happened to the body in the pool.

The engine: What does a community do to the bodies of its members, and what happens when three people try to describe it and discover they have witnessed three different events — all of which left marks?

---

## 2. Protagonist

**Nessa Firth**, 41, former resident of the coastal community of Theal. Moved to Theal with her partner, Ros, seven years ago; left eighteen months ago. Now lives alone in a rented flat in Norwich, working as a medical transcriptionist — a job that consists entirely of converting doctors' spoken words into written records. She transcribes bodies all day: their failures, their anomalies, their surgeries. She is good at rendering the physical into language.

- **What she WANTS**: To write a definitive account of what happened during the last Attending she witnessed — the one that made her leave. She wants to fix it in language, to make it legible, so she can stop dreaming about the pool.
- **What she NEEDS**: To accept that the experience cannot be made legible — that the community's power lies precisely in its resistance to being described, and that her desire to document it is itself a form of the community's hold on her.
- **Her flaw/blind spot**: She believes that accurate language can neutralize experience. She is a transcriptionist — she converts the spoken to the written, the physical to the textual, and she trusts this conversion. She cannot accept that some experiences distort whatever language tries to contain them. Her account will be the most precise, the most physically detailed, and for that reason the most unreliable.

---

## 3. Structure

The story alternates between THREE narrative voices, each formally distinct:

**Voice 1 — NESSA** (first person, past tense, precise and physical): A personal account written in the months after leaving Theal. Intimate, body-centered, full of specific sensory detail in the Machado mode. She describes the Attending as an act performed on a body — Ros's body — in the pool. Her hands were involved. She describes what her hands did. She describes what the warm water felt like. She describes the blue-green colonies floating at the surface. Her account reads as a medical/erotic testimony: tenderness that looks like surgery, or surgery that looks like tenderness. She cannot tell.

**Voice 2 — DR. LEAH SABLE** (third person, academic register, present tense): An outsider, a folklorist from the University of East Anglia, who visited Theal for three days during the Attending season but was not permitted to witness the ritual itself. Her account is assembled from interviews, photographs, and architectural measurements of the community's gathering space — the pool chamber. Her measurements don't match. The chamber, when she measures it from inside, is larger than the building that contains it. She documents this discrepancy with scholarly precision but cannot explain it. Her photographs show the pool empty, the water clear, the room small. But her tape measure says the room is fifty-three feet across, and the building is forty-one feet wide. She includes footnotes. Some of the footnotes contradict the text. She references Rossini's *Eduardo e Cristina* — a pastiche assembled from borrowed parts — when analyzing the community's festival calendar.

**Voice 3 — THE COMMUNITY** (first person plural, present tense, flat and warm): A collective statement, authored by no one and everyone. "We attend. We have always attended." This voice describes the Attending as beautiful, voluntary, nourishing. The tone is Jackson's lottery-voice: county fair warmth applied to something the reader gradually recognizes as monstrous. This voice never names what happens in the pool. It describes preparation, gratitude, the feeling of warmth, the honor of being chosen. It is the most loving voice in the story, and for that reason the most frightening.

### Beat Structure:

**Opening**: The community voice. A warm, declarative paragraph about Theal, the springs, the tradition. "We attend. The springs have been here longer than we have. The water is good." Establishes the tone of normalcy, beauty, and belonging. Ends with: "There is nothing to explain."

**Inciting disruption**: Cut to Nessa's voice. She is writing from Norwich. She is trying to describe what happened to Ros in the pool. She provides physical detail — the temperature of the water, the texture of the algae, the position of Ros's body. She describes something that reads as both surgery and an act of intimacy. She describes her own hands. Her account is precise but contradicts itself: at one point she says Ros was willing, at another she says Ros was held. She does not seem to notice the contradiction.

**Escalating middle**: Dr. Sable's account interleaves. She arrives in Theal and finds a community that is polite, welcoming, and eager to discuss the Attending in general terms while refusing every specific question. She measures the pool chamber. She photographs it. She catalogs the cyanobacteria (*Aphanothece*) in the water and notes that the colonies are unusually large — "several centimeters in diameter, visible to the naked eye, which is atypical for the genus." She interviews residents who all describe the Attending in identical language, as if reciting. Her footnotes begin to argue with her own text: one footnote observes that "the interview transcripts, when cross-referenced, contain no variation in phrasing whatsoever across seven separate subjects — a statistical impossibility in natural speech." The room keeps measuring wrong. She re-measures. It keeps being bigger than the building.

Meanwhile, Nessa's account deepens. She describes how she came to Theal, how she and Ros were welcomed, how the community enfolded them. She describes the first Attending she witnessed — someone else's body in the pool, the community gathered around, hands reaching into the warm water. She describes it as the most beautiful thing she'd ever seen. She describes the second Attending. The third. Each one she describes differently — not more violent, but more ambiguous. The hands in the water are doing something, and she cannot decide what.

The community voice returns between sections, shorter each time, more insistent. "We attend. It is an honor. There is nothing to explain." Then: "She misremembers." Then: "That is not what happened." The community voice begins to respond to Nessa's account directly, though they should not have access to it.

**Crisis point**: Nessa's account of the final Attending — the one where Ros was in the pool. Nessa describes her own hands on Ros's body. She describes the water turning the blue-green of the *Aphanothece* colonies, except the color is wrong — too vivid, almost fluorescent. She describes what her hands were doing. The reader cannot tell if this is violence or tenderness or something the language has no word for. Ros's body under the water has changed — has too many of something, or not enough. Nessa tries to describe this precisely and the sentences begin to break: fragments, repetitions, words that don't quite mean what she intends. She describes trying to pull Ros from the pool and realizing she does not want to. She describes her hands not opening.

Dr. Sable's account, interleaved, reaches its own crisis: she enters the pool chamber at night, after the Attending, and the room is different. Her torch illuminates walls that should not be there. Corridors. The pool itself is larger than it was during the day. The water is warm. She notes in a footnote that her tape measure now reads sixty-eight feet and the building exterior has not changed. She does not panic. She takes photographs. The photographs, when developed, will show an empty room.

**Ending**: The community voice, one last time. Warm, final, absolute. It describes the morning after the Attending. The springs are clear. The sun is warm. Everyone is present. The flowers are blooming. A new resident has arrived — a woman, alone, who has been corresponding with the community for months. She is welcomed. She is given a tour of the springs. She is told there is nothing to explain.

Nessa's account ends mid-sentence. She has been trying to describe the shape of what was in the pool, or what the pool had become, or what her hands —

The sentence does not finish.

Dr. Sable's final footnote, alone at the bottom of the page: "The preceding account was recovered from the subject's laptop after her return to Theal. She had deleted the file, but the recovery software reconstructed it in full. The document was last modified at 3:47 AM on the morning she left Norwich. Her flat was found empty. Her lease had been paid through the end of the quarter."

## 4. Key Scenes

1. **The pool chamber measurement scene** (Dr. Sable): She measures the interior at fifty-three feet. Measures the exterior at forty-one. Re-measures both. The numbers hold. She writes this down with scholarly composure while the reader's skin crawls. The Danielewski architecture made miniature and precise.

2. **Nessa's first Attending** (Nessa): Her description of watching another person in the pool — the community gathered around, hands reaching into warm water. She describes it as beautiful and cannot say why. She describes the sound of the water. She describes the person in the pool smiling. She describes the hands. This scene establishes the Attending as something that looks like care and reads like violation, or looks like violation and reads like care.

3. **The identical interviews** (Dr. Sable): Seven residents, interviewed separately, use the exact same phrasing. Dr. Sable's footnote notes the statistical impossibility. The community's language has replaced individual speech. Jackson's consensus horror rendered as data.

4. **Nessa's hands on Ros** (Nessa): The climactic scene. Her hands in the warm water, on Ros's body. The prose becomes Machado's body-as-document — every sensation physicalized, rendered with erotic and surgical precision. The reader cannot determine what is happening. Nessa cannot determine what is happening. The blue-green of the *Aphanothece* is wrong, too bright. Ros's body has changed. Nessa's hands will not open.

5. **The new arrival** (Community voice): The final scene. A woman arrives alone. She is welcomed. The community describes her arrival with the same warm, flat, Jackson-lottery voice they've used throughout. The cycle continues. There is nothing to explain.

---

## 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning**: The reader feels welcome. The community voice is warm, inviting, a little old-fashioned — like arriving in a village that still does things the proper way. Mild curiosity.

**Early middle**: Unease enters. Nessa's account is too specific about bodies — the intimacy of her descriptions feels transgressive, like reading someone's medical records or their private letters. The reader is uncertain whether to be moved or disturbed.

**Mid-story**: The academic account provides false reassurance — a rational frame — then systematically undermines it. The measurements don't match. The interviews are identical. The footnotes contradict the text. The reader's rational ground shifts.

**Late middle**: The three voices begin to bleed into each other. The community responds to Nessa's account. The academic footnotes reference events only Nessa has described. The reader realizes the documents are not separate — they are somehow aware of each other, despite being written at different times, in different places, by people who have never met. The labyrinth of the text becomes apparent.

**Ending**: The reader feels implicated. The new arrival at the end is not named. She could be anyone. The community's welcome is identical to Nessa's initial welcome. The cycle will repeat. The reader, having read all three accounts, knows something no single narrator knows — and it doesn't help. Knowing is not protection. The reader finishes the story and feels the warmth of the pool on their hands and wants to wash them and is not entirely sure why.

## 6. Formula Integration

**Robert Aickman (AuthorA)** — Most visible in: **Dr. Sable's sections and the overall tonal architecture.** The Aickman influence shapes the story's refusal to explain. Dr. Sable's scholarly composure in the face of impossible measurements is pure Aickman — the polite, reasonable person confronting the inexplicable and responding with courtesy rather than panic. The pool chamber that is larger than the building. The community that is hospitable and Wrong. The story's ending, which does not resolve, does not explain, leaves the reader holding two true and contradictory things. The specific influence of "The Hospice" — the host who keeps insisting, the guest who keeps eating — maps onto the community that keeps welcoming and the newcomer who keeps arriving.

**Carmen Maria Machado (AuthorB)** — Most visible in: **Nessa's sections.** Nessa's voice carries the Machado DNA — the body as document, the physical specificity, the blurring of surgery and tenderness, the erotic charge of the uncanny. Her descriptions of hands on Ros's body in the pool are Machado's "The Husband Stitch" rendered as communal horror: the body that is touched by everyone, the desire that cannot be spoken, the ribbon that must not be untied. The fairy-tale interpolation technique appears in Nessa's account: she interrupts her testimony to reference other stories she's heard about Theal, stories that contradict hers, stories that might be older versions of her own experience. The queerness of Nessa and Ros's relationship is structurally central — the community's ritual involves desire, and the regulation of desire, and the inability to name what kind of desire the ritual permits.

**House of Leaves (WorkX)** — Most visible in: **The overall narrative architecture and Dr. Sable's academic apparatus.** The triple-nested structure — Nessa's personal account, Dr. Sable's documentary investigation, the community's collective statement — mirrors Danielewski's Truant/Zampanò/Navidson layering. The pool chamber that measures larger inside than out is the Five-and-a-Half-Minute Hallway compressed into a single room. The footnotes that contradict the text. The photographs that show a different room than the one measured. The document recovered from a deleted laptop file. The entire story as a space the reader moves through, discovering that the corridors keep extending.

**The Lottery (WorkY)** — Most visible in: **The community voice and the thematic substrate.** The first-person-plural narrator IS Jackson's narrative voice — flat, warm, reportorial, describing the monstrous as routine. "We attend. We have always attended." The structure of the Attending — communal, seasonal, involving a selected body, surrounded by willing hands — is the structure of the lottery. The identical interviews (seven residents using exactly the same words) are Jackson's "Lottery in June, corn be heavy soon" — ritual language that has replaced thought. The new arrival at the end, who is welcomed, is the new family that moves to Jackson's village: they will be given stones, and they will throw them, because the alternative is being outside the community.

## 7. Title

**Attending at Theal**

- Does not start with "The"
- Gerund + place name: distinctive structure, not duplicated among existing titles
- "Attending" carries double meaning: the ritual's name (the Attending) and the act of being present, of paying attention — which is what the story demands of its reader
- "Theal" sounds like an old English coastal village (plausible, specific, slightly off), and nearly homophonous with "the ill" or "to heal" — sickness and care occupying the same phoneme, just as the story's ritual occupies the space between surgery and tenderness
