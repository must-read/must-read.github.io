# Story Plan: rom-paranormal-001

**Genre**: Romance
**Subgenre**: Paranormal Romance
**Combination ID**: rom-paranormal-001

**AuthorA**: Beverly Jenkins (rich historical context; meticulous cultural detail; community and pride; emotional directness)
**AuthorB**: Casey McQuiston (queer rom-com voice; exuberant optimism; pop-culture fluency; millennial sensibility)
**WorkX**: *Interview with the Vampire* by Anne Rice (confession/interview frame; immortality as burden; intimate bond between vampire and human; sensuality laced with melancholy)
**WorkY**: *The Time Traveler's Wife* by Audrey Niffenegger (love across temporal dislocation; one partner aging while the other doesn't; logistics of impossible devotion; domesticity strained by the supernatural)

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Beverly Jenkins — *Breathless* (opening chapter)

**Source**: AuthorA (Beverly Jenkins)
**Origin**: *Breathless* (2017), opening pages
**Why representative**: Shows Jenkins' signature blend of historical specificity, cultural pride, and emotionally direct romance with a strong Black female protagonist grounded in community.

> Portia Carmichael stepped off the train and onto the platform in the small Arizona Territory town of Tucson. The heat hit her like a furnace blast, and she immediately began to perspire beneath her fine gray traveling suit. Having spent most of her life in the more temperate climes of the eastern seaboard, she wondered if she'd made a terrible mistake. But there was no going back. She'd accepted the teaching position at the Tucson Colored School, and she intended to fulfill her obligations, even if the sun melted her down to her shoes.
>
> The station was small, nothing like the grand depots she'd known in Philadelphia and Denver. But she noticed things: the Mexican women selling tamales from a cart, their voices musical in the dry air. The old Black man sweeping the platform with the careful pride of someone who took his work seriously, regardless of what the work was. The way the mountains ringed the valley like sentinels keeping watch. She catalogued these details because she was a woman who believed that noticing was a form of respect. You couldn't teach children about a place you hadn't bothered to see.

### Sample 2: Casey McQuiston — *One Last Stop* (representative passage)

**Source**: AuthorB (Casey McQuiston)
**Origin**: *One Last Stop* (2021), mid-novel scene between August and Jane
**Why representative**: Captures McQuiston's millennial voice, pop-culture-laced banter, and the way humor and tenderness coexist in the same breath.

> "Okay," August said, settling onto the subway bench beside Jane like this was a normal thing to do on a Tuesday — sit next to a girl who was possibly displaced in time and definitely the most attractive person she had ever seen in real life, including that one time she saw Janelle Monae at a gas station in Georgia. "Hypothetical question."
>
> Jane raised one perfect eyebrow. She was wearing the leather jacket she was always wearing, because she was always wearing the leather jacket, because she was stuck. "I love hypothetical questions. They're the only kind I can answer honestly."
>
> "If you could eat one thing right now. Anything in the world. What would it be?"
>
> Jane thought about it for exactly three seconds. "A knish from Yonah Shimmel's. Not the ones they make now. The ones they made in 1973. They changed the recipe, August. They think nobody noticed but I noticed."
>
> "You have very strong feelings about knishes."
>
> "I have very strong feelings about most things. That's my whole problem."

### Sample 3: Anne Rice — *Interview with the Vampire* (confession/interview frame)

**Source**: WorkX (Anne Rice / *Interview with the Vampire*)
**Origin**: Opening pages, Louis's confession to the reporter
**Why representative**: Establishes the interview-as-confession structure, the weight of immortality rendered through intimate disclosure, the sensuality and melancholy that saturate every observation.

> "I see," said the vampire thoughtfully, and slowly he walked across the room towards the window. For a long time he stood there against the dim light from Divisadero Street and the passing lights of the traffic. The boy could see the furnishings of the room more clearly now, the round oak table, the chairs. A wash of white light lit up the vampire's pale and smooth face and the long curve of his jaw. "I wanted to be a saint," he said. "I was a young man. I had a younger brother who had died, and I believed that I had killed him by my own selfishness. I prayed to God to take me rather than him. I went to the chapel in the morning before anyone else and sat there in the darkness, watching the candle burn."
>
> He turned and moved to the chair and sat down slowly. "I tell you this so you will understand that there was nothing romantic about it. Nothing heroic. The death of someone you love is not romantic. And immortality — I can tell you — is not romantic either. It is the slow accumulation of knowing too much about a place to ever see it fresh again."

### Sample 4: Audrey Niffenegger — *The Time Traveler's Wife* (aging/temporal dislocation)

**Source**: WorkY (Audrey Niffenegger / *The Time Traveler's Wife*)
**Origin**: Clare's perspective on Henry's condition, mid-novel
**Why representative**: Shows the domestic logistics of impossible love -- one partner anchored in normal time, the other unstuck -- and the way Niffenegger renders this as a specific, practical burden rather than a poetic abstraction.

> It's a Wednesday in January, 2004. Clare is thirty-two. Henry is supposed to be forty. She has made dinner, set the table, lit candles. She has done this knowing that there is a sixty percent chance he will not be here to eat it. She has learned to cook for one and set for two, because the setting is the ritual, and the ritual is what keeps her sane.
>
> When he comes back this time, he will be younger than her. He will be twenty-eight, confused, maybe bleeding from somewhere she can't see. She will clean the wound, give him clothes from the closet where she keeps his things in every size. She will pretend it is not strange to look at the face of the man she married and see someone who hasn't proposed yet, who hasn't buried his mother, who doesn't know that their daughter draws horses on every surface she can reach.
>
> This is what love looks like when it is not metaphorical: laundry in four sizes. A medicine cabinet organized by era. A woman who has learned to introduce herself to her own husband.

## Wikipedia Concepts

Web access was unavailable during this planning session. The following two concepts are drawn from the planner's knowledge and selected for their specific integration potential:

### Concept 1: Dendrochronology (tree-ring dating)
The science of dating events and environmental changes by analyzing growth ring patterns in trees. Each ring records a year of growth -- thin rings for drought years, thick for rainy ones. Cross-referencing ring patterns across samples can establish precise chronologies spanning thousands of years.

**Integration**: The bookstore occupies a building with exposed original wooden beams. The immortal character, Elliot, touches the beam during a quiet moment and mentions offhandedly that the wood "remembers the drought of 1876." This becomes one of the anachronisms Naomi logs in her inventory ledger. Later, after the confession, she realizes he wasn't being metaphorical -- he was reading the tree rings with his fingers the way someone else might read a page. Dendrochronology becomes a quiet metaphor for the story: both the building and the immortal body record time in rings of experience, legible only to those who know how to look.

### Concept 2: Sankofa (Akan concept)
A word from the Akan people of Ghana meaning "go back and get it" -- the principle that one must look to the past to understand the present and move forward. Often represented by a bird with its head turned backward while its feet face forward, sometimes carrying an egg (the future) in its mouth.

**Integration**: The Sankofa symbol is carved into the building's original doorframe, placed there by the Black family who built the structure in 1923. Naomi knows it's there but has never looked into its origin. Elliot recognizes it because he knew the family who carved it -- the Washingtons, who ran a boarding house for Black travelers decades before the Green Book formalized such networks. This becomes the "something specific and verifiable and impossible" that Beverly Jenkins insisted on during the author meeting: Elliot tells Naomi the symbol was carved by a woman named Dolores Washington on the night her son came home from France after World War I, and that there used to be a matching one on the back door, painted over in 1961. Naomi checks. The paint is there.

## Premise

Naomi Okafor, 36, owns Cornerstone Books, the last Black-owned bookstore in a rapidly gentrifying D.C. neighborhood. Her new regular customer -- a man named Elliot who shows up every Tuesday and Thursday, buys nothing, knows too much about the building's history, and makes her laugh when she doesn't want to -- is either the most charming person she's met in years or a scout for the developers circling her block. When his anachronisms start adding up (he references a restaurant that closed in 1968, corrects her about the building's original floor plan, touches the ceiling beams like he's reading them), she begins keeping a list in the margins of her inventory ledger. The truth, when it comes, is not romantic: he has been alive for over three hundred years, and he has watched neighborhoods like hers form and die and form again, and he is tired in a way that has become funny, and he is in love with a woman who will age while he does not, and neither of them knows what to do about it.

---

## Protagonist

**Name**: Naomi Okafor
**Age**: 36
**Situation**: Third-generation D.C. native. Her grandmother attended the church that is now a luxury condo three blocks away. She used an inheritance from her late aunt -- the only family member who believed a bookstore could survive -- to open Cornerstone Books seven years ago. The store is alive but perpetually six months from not being. She manages the accounts herself, handles author events alone, fixes the leaky pipe in the back room with YouTube tutorials, and has not taken a vacation since the store opened.
**Want**: To save the bookstore. Specifically: to make the next lease renewal without having to take out another loan.
**Need**: To stop treating permanence as the only measure of value. She conflates impermanence with failure -- if the store closes, if the neighborhood changes, if a relationship ends, then it wasn't real. She needs to learn that the building is the point (but the story must never say this).
**Flaw**: She reads threat into everything. Every act of attention becomes potential predation. When Elliot asks about the building's history, she hears a developer's due diligence, not a man's grief. She has been fighting so long that she's lost the ability to distinguish between someone circling her and someone circling the drain. This suspicion has cost her a romantic relationship before (her ex, Danielle, who left two years ago because Naomi could never believe that someone wanted to be close without wanting something).

## Structure

### 1. Opening Image/Scene
Naomi is at her Tuesday inventory count, halfway through a shipment of Toni Morrison reprints, when Elliot walks in for the sixth time. He browses, picks up *Song of Solomon*, reads the first page standing up, puts it back. She watches him from behind the counter. He's done this exact thing five times before. She wants to be annoyed but instead finds herself wondering what it is about page one that keeps sending him back to the shelf. She almost asks. She doesn't. She logs it: *Tuesday regular. Still hasn't bought anything. Possible problem.*

### 2. Inciting Disruption
Two events within the same week: (a) Naomi receives a letter from a development company inquiring about the building's lease terms and offering to "discuss the property's future" -- standard gentrification language. (b) During his next visit, Elliot makes a mistake. He mentions "the pharmacy on the corner" and describes its green awning. There has been no pharmacy on that corner in Naomi's lifetime. When she tells him that's a nail salon now, he corrects himself too slowly. She writes it down in her ledger, next to the Morrison count. The two events -- the developer letter and Elliot's anachronism -- fuse in her mind. His interest in the building stops being charming and starts being suspicious.

### 3. Escalating Middle
Naomi begins testing Elliot. She mentions the building's history -- things she half-knows, half-invents -- and watches his reactions. He corrects her gently on a detail about the original floor plan (there was a second entrance on the east side, bricked up in the 1940s). She checks. He's right. She adds it to the list. Meanwhile, their conversations deepen despite her suspicion. They argue about Morrison (he insists *Tar Baby* is underrated; she calls this contrarian nostalgia; he says "it's not nostalgia if you were there" and then stops talking for a full minute). He makes her laugh about a zoning board meeting. She tells him about the man who shoplifts a poetry collection every month and how she's started leaving the book slightly forward on the shelf to make it easier. Elliot says, "You're the kind of person Dolores would have liked." She asks who Dolores is. He changes the subject.

### 4. Crisis Point
Naomi, now convinced Elliot is connected to the developers, confronts him. She's found the development company's records -- they've been buying up the block for two years. She accuses him of scouting the building, gathering information, softening her up. She tells him to leave. Elliot, faced with losing her, does the only thing he can: he tells the truth. Not all at once. He tells her about Dolores Washington, who carved the Sankofa symbol on the doorframe the night her son came home from France in 1919. He tells her the symbol was also on the back door, painted over in 1961. He tells her the building was a boarding house before it was a dry cleaner before it was empty before it was hers. He tells her he knew Dolores. Naomi doesn't believe him. Then she checks the back door. The paint is there, layered thick, exactly where he said. This is the confession scene -- not a gothic monologue but an exhausted man sitting in a folding chair in a bookstore telling a woman who doesn't believe him that he is three hundred and forty-seven years old, and that he has watched every neighborhood he has ever loved get swallowed, and that he keeps coming back to this building because it is the only one on the block that still has the original beams, and the beams remember things.

### 5. Ending (HFN)
After the confession, Naomi does not immediately accept or forgive. Days pass. She verifies what she can: the Sankofa carving matches the period he described. The bricked-up entrance is where he said. A 1923 photograph from the D.C. Historical Society shows the building as a boarding house with the name WASHINGTON over the door. She can't verify that he's immortal, but she can verify that everything he said about the building is true, and no living person could know those things. She goes to find him. He's sitting on a bench across from the store, in the spot where -- she now realizes -- there used to be a bus stop that served the Black neighborhood before the routes were redrawn. She sits beside him. She does not say "I believe you." She says, "Dolores's great-niece is listed in the D.C. phonebook. I called her. She cried." Then she says, "You owe me for the Morrison. You've been reading page one for three months." He says, "I keep starting it because I keep trying to read it the way you would." She says, "That's either the best line anyone's ever used on me or the worst." He says, "It's Tuesday." She says, "It is." The store is still open. The lease is still a problem. He is still whatever he is. She is thirty-six and getting older. None of it is resolved. They sit on the bench. The building behind them is 103 years old and counting. The unresolved thread: the developer letter. Naomi never addresses it. The threat hasn't gone away. The story ends without the bookstore being saved, because the bookstore hasn't been saved yet, because saving it is a daily act, not a climax.

## Key Scenes

1. **The Morrison Argument**: Elliot and Naomi debate which Morrison novel a reluctant reader should start with. Elliot advocates for *Tar Baby*; Naomi says *The Bluest Eye*. The argument gets heated, funny, and then suddenly intimate when Elliot says, "You know what I love about how you talk about books? You talk about them like they're people who can let you down." She doesn't know what to do with that, so she reorganizes the display table. (Serves: character revelation, romantic tension, Jenkins' cultural specificity, McQuiston's banter)

2. **The Poetry Shoplifter**: Naomi tells Elliot about the middle-aged man who steals a poetry collection every month. She's started leaving the book slightly forward on the shelf. Elliot asks which poet. She says Lucille Clifton. He goes quiet in a way that means something she can't read. "She understood what bodies carry," he says. Then, lighter: "Also she was funnier than people give her credit for." (Serves: community detail, the weight of centuries rendered as taste, Beverly's insistence on the body as evidence)

3. **The Confrontation/Confession**: Naomi, cornered by evidence and the developer letter, throws Elliot out. He sits in the folding chair and refuses to leave. "You can call the police," he says, "but I should warn you that I've been arrested in this neighborhood before and it was very boring both times." Then he tells her. Not dramatically. Exhaustedly. He lists dates and details like a man reading from an internal ledger. The Sankofa symbol, Dolores, the beams, the drought of 1876. (Serves: Rice's confession frame, the interview as intimate disclosure, the immortal who is not sorry but tired)

4. **The Verification Montage**: Brief, sharp scenes of Naomi checking Elliot's claims. The back door. The photograph at the Historical Society. The phone call to Dolores's great-niece. Each verification shifts her not toward belief but toward a specific, physical feeling: the feeling of standing in a building that is older than she realized, connected to more people than she knew, carrying more history than its walls suggest. (Serves: Niffenegger's domestic logistics of the impossible -- the way the supernatural is verified through mundane research, not mystical revelation)

5. **The Bench**: The final scene. She finds him where the bus stop used to be. She doesn't declare love. She presents evidence of connection: the phone call, the book debt. He responds with vulnerability disguised as humor. They sit together. The building stands. Nothing is fixed. (Serves: HFN ending, Jenkins' emotional directness without sentimentality, McQuiston's humor as a vehicle for tenderness, the Time Traveler's Wife's logic of choosing love despite temporal impossibility)

## Emotional Trajectory

**Opening**: Low-grade anxiety and determination. The reader feels what Naomi feels: the weight of a thing you love that might not survive. Mixed with the specific pleasure of a person who is good at their work and takes pride in the details.

**Middle**: Suspicion crosscut with attraction. The reader should feel the pull of Elliot's charm while sharing Naomi's legitimate distrust. The fun of their conversations (the Morrison argument, the bookstore banter) sits next to the low dread of the developer letter and the growing list of anachronisms. The reader should feel caught between wanting Naomi to trust Elliot and understanding exactly why she can't.

**Crisis**: The confrontation should feel like a gut-punch for both characters and the reader. Naomi's accusation is wrong but not unreasonable. Elliot's confession is true but sounds insane. The reader should feel the impossibility of the situation -- the structural inevitability that Beverly identified in the meeting (an immortal's nostalgia and a developer's due diligence look identical from the outside).

**Ending**: The reader should feel the specific ache of a love story that knows its own constraints and chooses to exist within them. Not bittersweet -- that implies sadness winning. Not triumphant -- nothing has been won. Something closer to the feeling of sitting on a bench next to someone you're choosing, in front of a building that might or might not last, on an ordinary Tuesday. The feeling that the ordinary Tuesday is the whole point.

## Formula Integration

### Beverly Jenkins passage (AuthorA)
**Concentrate in**: The sections about the neighborhood, the bookstore, and the community around it. Jenkins' influence should be strongest in the way the story renders the specific material culture of a Black-owned business -- the inventory ledger, the poetry shoplifter, the incense vendor on the corner who recommends the store to tourists, the grandmother's church turned condo. Every detail should feel researched and earned, not decorative. The passage where Naomi thinks about what it means to stand in a building that her aunt believed in, paid for, and never saw open -- that's Jenkins. Emotional directness that never becomes sentimentality.

### Casey McQuiston passage (AuthorB)
**Concentrate in**: The dialogue between Naomi and Elliot, particularly the Morrison argument and the lighter moments of the confession scene. McQuiston's voice should drive the humor -- Elliot's exhaustion-as-comedy register, Naomi's sharp retorts, the way they flirt through disagreement. The line "I keep starting it because I keep trying to read it the way you would" is pure McQuiston: a romantic declaration disguised as a joke about a book. The pop-culture fluency should be Naomi's -- she's the millennial in the room.

### *Interview with the Vampire* passage (WorkX)
**Concentrate in**: The confession scene. Rice's contribution is structural: the interview/confession frame, adapted from a gothic parlor to a folding chair in a bookstore. Elliot's disclosure should carry the weight of Rice's Louis -- not a dramatic monologue but an exhausted, intimate accounting. He lists places and dates the way Louis lists feedings: with the precision of someone who has counted too many. The sensuality-laced-with-melancholy should show in how Elliot describes the neighborhoods he loved: the beauty of Dolores's boarding house, the sound of music from a club that no longer exists, always shadowed by the knowledge that he was watching something die. The intimacy of the confession -- telling one person the truth you've carried for centuries -- should feel as charged as Rice's best scenes between Louis and the boy.

### *The Time Traveler's Wife* passage (WorkY)
**Concentrate in**: The HFN ending and the theme of asymmetric aging. Niffenegger's influence is thematic: the logistics of impossible devotion, the domestic strain of the supernatural. Naomi will age; Elliot will not. The story should address this not as a poetic abstraction but as a practical reality -- the way Niffenegger would. Clare keeps clothes in every size; Naomi will keep the store open knowing that Elliot has watched every store on this block close. The ending scene on the bench should carry the Niffenegger DNA: love as a choice you make knowing the math, domesticity as an act of defiance against temporal impossibility. The verification montage is also Niffenegger: the supernatural confirmed not through revelation but through checking records, calling phone numbers, looking at old photographs.

## Title

**Ever Since I Can Remember**

A phrase that works on three levels: (1) Naomi's relationship to the neighborhood ("I've lived here ever since I can remember"), (2) Elliot's impossibly long memory (he can remember *everything*), and (3) the beginning of a love story's origin myth -- "I've loved you ever since I can remember" -- spoken by someone whose memory spans centuries. The title is a gerund-anchored clause, diverging from existing title patterns.

## Additional Notes for Writer

- **Tone**: Warm but not cozy. The story should feel like a romance written by people who know that romance exists alongside spreadsheets, zoning meetings, and developer letters. The humor is necessary but earned by proximity to things that aren't funny.
- **Voice**: Close third person, Naomi's POV. She's practical, observant, and funny in a dry way. She does not narrate with wonder; she narrates with the precision of someone who keeps an inventory ledger.
- **The immortal**: Elliot should never apologize for being immortal. He should never brood on rooftops. He is tired in a way that has become comic. His tragedy is cumulative, not dramatic. He carries grief the way a building carries load -- distributed, structural, invisible until you look at the beams.
- **Do NOT announce the theme**. The story is about permanence and impermanence, about building things that might not last, about choosing love despite the math. But if any character says anything like "the building is the point" or "nothing lasts forever and that's okay," the story has failed. Let the images do the work: the beams, the tree rings, the Sankofa bird facing backward while walking forward.
- **The developer subplot**: Introduce it, let it create pressure, but do NOT resolve it. The story ends with the threat still active. This is not a story about saving the bookstore. It's a story about falling in love while trying to save the bookstore.
- **Elliot's age**: 347 years old. Do not over-explain his immortality. He does not know why he is what he is. He simply is. This is not a vampire story or a magic story. It is a paranormal romance where the paranormal element is presented as a fact of life, like a chronic condition.
