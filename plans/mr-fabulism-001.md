# Story Plan: mr-fabulism-001

## Assignment

- **Genre**: magical-realism
- **Subgenre**: fabulism
- **AuthorA**: Karen Russell — American fabulism; swamp Gothic; inventive premises; playful, sensory language
- **AuthorB**: Laura Esquivel — Sensory, food-centered magical realism; "Like Water for Chocolate" tradition; emotion as physical force
- **WorkX**: The House of the Spirits by Isabel Allende — structure: Multi-generational saga compressed into key episodes; each generation inherits and transforms the previous one's gift; the house itself as accumulating memory
- **WorkY**: Piranesi by Susanna Clarke — themes: A world that is self-contained and internally logical; beauty and wonder in confinement; the question of whether escape from a strange paradise is actually desirable
- **Risk card**: none
- **Target word count**: 4,200 words (acceptable range: 3,900-4,500)
- **Reading time**: 17 minutes

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Karen Russell — *Swamplandia!* (opening)

**Source**: AuthorA (Karen Russell)
**Origin**: *Swamplandia!*, Chapter 1, opening pages
**Why representative**: Demonstrates Russell's signature move — the fantastical premise delivered matter-of-factly through a child narrator, with inventive sensory language and playful metaphor.

> Our mother performed in starlight. Whose innovation this was I never discovered. Probably it was Chief Bigtree's idea, and it was a good one — to blank the follow spot and let a sharp moon cut across the sky, unchaperoned; to kill the microphone; to leave the stage lights tin eyelids scrolled and give the tourists in the stands a chance to enjoy the darkness of our island; to encourage the whole stadium to gulp air along with Swamplandia!'s star performer, the world-famous alligator wrestler Hilola Bigtree.
>
> Four times a week, our mother climbed the ladder above the Gator Pit in a green two-piece bathing suit and stood on the edge of the diving board, breathing. If it was windy, her long hair flew around her face, but the rest of her stayed motionless.
>
> Trumpets tooted from our big, old-fashioned speakers, and the huge unseeing eye of the follow spot twisted through the palm fronds until it found Hilola. Just like that she ceased to be our mother. Fame settled on her like a film.
>
> The lake was planked with great gray and black bodies. Hilola Bigtree had to hit the water with perfect precision, making incremental adjustments midair to avoid the gators. The Chief's follow spot cast a light like a rime of ice onto the murk, and Mom swam inside this circle across the entire length of the lake. People screamed and pointed whenever an alligator swam into the spotlight with her, a plump and switching tail cutting suddenly into its margarine wavelengths, the spade of a monster's face jawing up at her side.

**Additional Russell quotes for voice reference:**

- "My older sister has entire kingdoms inside of her, and some of them are only accessible at certain seasons..."
- "Any place can become a cemetery. All it takes is your body."
- "There are certain prehistoric things that swim beyond extinction."
- "Wear your skeleton on the inside out, and keep your insect heart secret."

**From "Vampires in the Lemon Grove":**
> In October, the men and women of Sorrento harvest the primofiore, or 'first flowering fruit,' the most succulent lemons; in March, the yellow bianchetti ripen, followed in June by the green verdelli. In every season you can find me sitting at my bench, watching them fall.

> I took a gulp, and a whole small lemon lodged in my mouth... It was bracingly sour, with a delicate hint of ocean salt. After an initial prickling — a sort of chemical effervescence along my gums — a soothing blankness traveled from the tip of each fang to my fevered brain.

**Key voice elements to capture**: Wild premises delivered deadpan; inventive metaphor (spotlight as "rime of ice," tail cutting through "margarine wavelengths"); child or naif narrator who doesn't register strangeness as strange; playful sensory specificity; sentences that accelerate through accumulating clauses.

### Sample 2: Laura Esquivel — *Like Water for Chocolate* (Chapter 3, March — Quail in Rose Petal Sauce)

**Source**: AuthorB (Laura Esquivel)
**Origin**: *Like Water for Chocolate*, Chapter 3
**Why representative**: The central scene of Esquivel's method — food as literal emotional transmission, the chemistry of cooking as magical event, the body as the site where meaning lands.

> It was as if a strange alchemical process had dissolved her entire being in the rose petal sauce, in the tender flesh of the quails, in the wine, in every one of the meal's aromas. That was the way she entered Pedro's body, hot, voluptuous, perfumed, totally sensuous.

> Tita was literally 'like water for chocolate' — she was on the verge of boiling over.

> Not that night, nor many others, for as long as she lived, could she free herself from that cold.

> She stopped grinding, straightened up, and proudly lifted her chest so Pedro could see it better.

> Instead of eating, she would stare at her hands for hours on end.

> He left because I had exhausted his strength, though he hadn't managed to quench the fire inside me.

**Key voice elements to capture**: Food as literal emotional conductor, not metaphor; the body as first reader of feeling (cold, heat, hunger); recipes as structural scaffolding; declarative sentences with the weight of proverbs; the domestic space as theater of power; emotion described in terms of physical temperature, chemistry, and digestion.

### Sample 3: Isabel Allende — *The House of the Spirits* (opening and structural pattern)

**Source**: WorkX (Isabel Allende)
**Origin**: *The House of the Spirits*, opening line and structural approach
**Why representative**: Demonstrates the multi-generational compression technique — beginning with one generation's voice framing another's, using a journal/notebook device to allow time-jumps, treating magical abilities matter-of-factly.

> Barrabas came to us by sea, the child Clara wrote in her delicate calligraphy. She was already in the habit of writing down important matters, and afterward, when she was mute, she also recorded trivialities, never suspecting that fifty years later I would use her notebooks to reclaim the past and overcome terrors of my own.

**Structural pattern to emulate**: Each generation inherits a gift from the previous one but transforms it. Clara has telekinesis; her daughter Blanca has artistic hands that shape clay; her granddaughter Alba has the gift of survival and testimony. The house on the corner is both a physical place and a repository of accumulated memory — walls marked, rooms added, spirits gathering. Time is compressed through episodic narration: key scenes from decades, not continuous chronology. The narrating voice looks backward from a position of loss, reclaiming what the house held.

**Key structural elements to capture**: Multi-generational compression through key episodes; each generation's gift as variation on a theme; the house as accumulating memory; a notebook or record that bridges generations; matter-of-fact treatment of the magical; political and domestic histories intertwined.

### Sample 4: Susanna Clarke — *Piranesi* (voice and thematic pattern)

**Source**: WorkY (Susanna Clarke)
**Origin**: *Piranesi*, opening pages and key refrains
**Why representative**: Demonstrates Clarke's method — a self-contained world presented with total sincerity by a narrator who finds it beautiful and logical, the question of whether leaving paradise constitutes rescue or loss.

> When the Moon rose in the Third Northern Hall I went to the Ninth Vestibule to witness the joining of three Tides.

> The Beauty of the House is immeasurable; its Kindness infinite.

> The House is valuable because it is the House. It is enough in and of Itself. It is not the means to an end.

> The World feels complete and whole, and I, its Child, fit into it seamlessly.

> It does not matter that you do not understand the reason. You are the Beloved Child of the House. Be comforted.

> May your Paths be safe, your Floors unbroken and may the House fill your eyes with Beauty.

> Perhaps even people you like and admire immensely can make you see the World in ways you would rather not.

> My last thought before I fell asleep was: He is dead. My only friend. My only enemy.

**Key thematic elements to capture**: A self-contained world with its own internal logic; a narrator who genuinely loves their confinement; the house as a character — benevolent, immense, alive; wonder and cataloguing as forms of devotion; the tension between beauty-in-confinement and the possibility of freedom; the question of whether the person who leaves is the same person who stayed; capitalized nouns for things the narrator reveres.

## Wikipedia Concepts

### Articles Surveyed (20)

1. **Caprification / fig wasp** — Female fig wasps enter through the ostiole (tiny pore), pollinate, lay eggs, and die inside. The fig absorbs the wasp's body. Symbiotic relationship spanning 80 million years.
2. **Strangler fig** — Seeds germinate in the canopy, send roots earthward, slowly envelop and kill the host tree, leaving a hollow core. A tree shaped like absence.
3. **Syconium** — The fig is not a fruit but an inverted flower (inflorescence), containing up to 7,000 tiny flowers inside a fleshy receptacle. You eat the flowers, not the fruit.
4. **Geosmin / petrichor** — The smell of rain on earth is caused by geosmin, a compound produced by soil bacteria (Streptomyces). Humans can detect it at 5 parts per trillion — an evolutionary sensitivity possibly linked to finding water.
5. **Living root bridges of Meghalaya** — The Khasi people of India guide rubber fig tree roots across rivers, growing bridges over 20-25 years. In Khasi mythology, ancestors descended via a living-root ladder connecting heaven and earth.
6. **Maillard reaction** — The chemical reaction between amino acids and sugars that creates browning and flavor in cooked food. "By far the most widely practiced chemical reaction in the world."
7. **Sourdough starter / mother dough** — A living culture of wild yeast and bacteria, maintained across generations. Some starters are over 100 years old. The culture self-regulates, preventing colonization by hostile organisms.
8. **Tsukumogami** — Japanese belief that household objects acquire a spirit (kami) after 100 years. People would discard objects before the centennial to prevent them from becoming sentient.
9. **Wardian case / sealed terrarium** — David Latimer's sealed bottle garden has been self-sustaining since 1960, watered only once in 65 years. A perfect closed ecosystem of light, moisture, and decay.
10. **Foxfire** — Bioluminescent fungi that make decaying wood glow blue-green at night. Known since Aristotle (382 BC). The light attracts insects that spread spores — the glow is regulated by circadian rhythms.
11. **Pando** — A quaking aspen grove in Utah: 47,000 stems that appear to be individual trees but are a single organism connected by roots, spanning 106 acres. Estimated 9,000-16,000 years old.
12. **Gastric brooding frog** — Now extinct; the female swallowed fertilized eggs and incubated them in her stomach. The tadpoles secreted a substance (prostaglandin E2) that turned off her stomach acid.
13. **Emotional contagion** — The unconscious acquisition of others' emotional states through mimicry. Distinct from empathy: no self-other distinction. Feelings spread like infection.
14. **Phantom limb** — After amputation, the brain retains proprioceptive memory of the limb. The body remembers a shape it no longer has.
15. **Antikythera mechanism** — A 2,000-year-old Greek analog computer found in a shipwreck. Predicted planetary positions and eclipses. The size of a shoebox.
16. **Aeolian harp** — An instrument played by wind alone. Produces only harmonic frequencies — never the fundamental. Named for Aeolus, god of wind.
17. **Wunderkammer** — 16th-century European rooms devoted to collecting singular objects: naturalia, artificialia, mirabilia. Evolved from medieval reliquaries.
18. **Maria Sabina** — Mazatec wise woman in Oaxaca who led psilocybin ceremonies (veladas). After Western exposure, her community turned against her; her house was burned.
19. **Isfahan** — "Isfahan is half the world" (Persian proverb). A city so beautiful the proverb claims seeing it is equivalent to seeing half of existence.
20. **Potosi** — Bolivian silver mountain that produced 80% of the world's silver. "The mountain that eats men." Gave rise to the Spanish expression "valer un Potosi" — to be worth a fortune.

### Selected Concepts (2)

**1. Caprification / fig wasp symbiosis**

The female wasp enters the fig through the ostiole, a passage so tight it tears off her wings and antennae. She pollinates the internal flowers, lays eggs, and dies. The fig absorbs her body entirely. If she enters a female fig (the edible kind) instead of a caprifig, she cannot lay eggs and dies for nothing — but the fig still absorbs her, and the pollination still takes. The relationship is 80 million years old.

**Integration**: The grandmother's fig tree in the courtyard operates by this ancient logic. When the story's narrator learns how figs are actually pollinated — that every fig she has ever eaten contained the dissolved remains of a wasp — it becomes the moment of recognition: the house works the same way. The family enters the house through the narrow passage of birth, the house tears off something (their autonomy, their separateness), and they pollinate its rooms with their living, and when they die, the house absorbs them entirely. The fig tree is not a metaphor for the house. The fig tree is how the house feeds. This detail should feel inevitable, botanical, and terrifying in equal measure.

**2. Aeolian harp**

An instrument played only by wind, producing harmonic overtones but never the fundamental note. The string vibrates in fractions — halves, thirds, quarters — creating chords no human hand could play.

**Integration**: The house sings. (The meeting established this: "a house that sings recipes in the voices of its dead.") The mechanism is aeolian: wind moves through the house's corridors and the walls, now threaded with fig roots, vibrate in harmonics. The voices of the dead are never heard as fundamental tones — the grandmother's voice is always an overtone of the great-grandmother's, and the mother's an overtone of the grandmother's. The narrator recognizes individual ancestors the way one hears individual notes in a chord. The house's song is never a melody — it is a recipe, instructions for a dish no one alive remembers how to begin. This gives the house's singing a specific, non-magical mechanism (wind through root-threaded walls) that simultaneously makes it more magical, not less.

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

A young woman named Paloma inherits a house that has been slowly eaten — and held upright — by a fig tree her grandmother planted sixty years ago. The house digests the memories of everyone who has lived in it, and the fig tree produces fruit that tastes of the dead. Paloma has never eaten anything that didn't come from the house's kitchen, and the question the story asks is not whether she should leave, but what happens to a person who has been nourished entirely on her own family's processed grief and joy — and who one day tastes something that tastes of no one.

### 2. Protagonist

- **Name**: Paloma Resendiz, 26 years old, the third generation in the house. Lives alone now — her mother died two years ago, and her grandmother died before Paloma was born. Works as a bookkeeper for a mezcal distributor in the nearby town of San Andres, a job she does remotely from the house's library, which hums when she opens spreadsheets.
- **What she WANTS**: To maintain the house. To keep it fed, keep it warm, keep the kitchen producing. She understands the house as her responsibility, her inheritance, her purpose.
- **What she NEEDS**: To discover that devotion to something beautiful can be a form of self-erasure — that she has been slowly dissolving into the house the way the fig wasp dissolves into the fig.
- **Her flaw**: She mistakes being needed for being loved. The house needs her. She interprets this as the house loving her. She cannot distinguish between the two because she has never experienced love that did not also consume.

### 3. Structure

**Opening image/scene**: Paloma in the kitchen at dawn, grinding dried chilies for a mole her grandmother never finished teaching her mother. The house is singing — wind through fig-root walls producing harmonics in the voices of the dead. Paloma hears her grandmother's voice as a low rasp under her mother's clearer soprano. She adjusts the chocolate ratio based on what the walls tell her. This is ordinary. This is Tuesday.

**The inciting disruption**: A fig wasp lands on Paloma's wrist while she is harvesting figs from the courtyard tree. She watches it crawl toward the ostiole of an unripe fig. She follows it with her eyes and, for the first time, understands the mechanism: the wasp enters, the wasp dies, the fig absorbs. That evening, eating a ripe fig, she tastes something new in it — not a person, not a memory, but the wasp itself: a thin note of chitin and wing. The house has been absorbing more than family. The house absorbs everything that enters it.

**The escalating middle**: Paloma begins noticing things she has never noticed. The front door's threshold is narrower than it was last year — the fig roots are slowly closing the ostiole. Her mother's bedroom has developed a sweet, composting smell, like overripe fruit. The library's hum has shifted pitch; it no longer simply processes her spreadsheets but seems to be digesting them, incorporating numerical patterns into its song. She finds a root growing through her mother's pillow. She finds that her own bedroom walls, when she presses her ear to them, pulse with a rhythm that matches her heartbeat — or her heartbeat has begun matching the walls. She cannot tell which came first.

She calls a man about removing the fig tree — an arborist named Diego from San Andres who specializes in strangler figs. He arrives, walks the property, and tells her what she doesn't want to hear: the fig IS the house now. The original adobe walls have been replaced, cell by cell, root by root. If you remove the fig, the house collapses. It's not a tree growing on a house. It's a house-shaped tree.

**The crisis point**: Paloma goes to San Andres for a meeting with the mezcal distributor — her first time leaving the house in weeks. She sits at a taco cart and eats a taco al pastor. The pork is ordinary. The salsa verde is sharp and anonymous. There is no ghost in it. No grandmother. No memory. It is the emptiest, most liberated thing she has ever tasted. She sits on the curb with salsa on her chin and feels something she has never felt: the absence of the house's hum in her bones. Silence. She is, for the first time, just a body eating food that was made by a stranger for a stranger.

She goes back. Of course she goes back.

**The ending**: Paloma returns to find the house has reacted to her absence. The kitchen is cold for the first time — not broken, but sulking, the way a living thing withdraws warmth when it feels abandoned. The fig tree has dropped every ripe fig in the courtyard, a carpet of burst fruit, and the air smells of every person who ever lived here, all at once, a perfume so dense it's almost visible. She walks through the fruit. She enters the kitchen. She begins to cook — not a recipe from the walls, but something new: a dish that uses the house's figs and the memory of the stranger's salsa verde. Something that is both the house and not the house. The walls begin to hum a note they have never hummed before. Whether this is the house learning something new or the house beginning to digest this last act of independence — Paloma doesn't know. The story doesn't say. She grinds the chilies. The house sings.

### 4. Key Scenes

- **The mole at dawn**: Paloma cooking by instruction from the walls' harmonics, adjusting spice ratios by listening to the dead. Establishes the house's internal logic as a self-contained Piranesi-like world. Reveals the sensory richness that makes the trap desirable. (Esquivel's food-as-emotion; Clarke's wonder-in-confinement)

- **The fig wasp on the wrist**: Paloma watches the wasp enter the ostiole and recognizes the mechanism of her own life. A scene of quiet horror disguised as botanical observation. The taste of chitin in the fig that evening. (Russell's inventive premise delivered deadpan; the Wikipedia concept made structural)

- **Diego the arborist's diagnosis**: "The fig IS the house." He traces a root from the courtyard through the kitchen wall, through the library floor, up through her mother's bedroom, into the roof. He shows her where adobe ends and root begins, and she cannot find the seam. He is clinical, professional, slightly awed. He has never seen a strangler fig this thorough. He uses the word "inosculation" — the process by which two plants grow together until they fuse. (Allende's structural compression — the generations' work revealed in one walk-through)

- **The taco on the curb**: The revelation of anonymity. Food without memory. Freedom as emptiness; emptiness as freedom. The silence in her bones where the hum used to be. (Esquivel's hunger vs. Russell's wonder — the meeting's unresolved argument enacted in a single bite)

- **The return and the new dish**: Paloma cooking something unprecedented — figs from the house combined with a technique from outside. The house's reaction: not rejection, not acceptance, but a new note. An overtone never heard before. Whether this is evolution or consumption remains open. (Allende's generational transformation — the third generation changes the gift; Clarke's question: is this escape or deeper confinement?)

### 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning**: The reader feels warmth, safety, and fascination. The house is a marvel. The kitchen smells incredible. The singing walls are strange and beautiful. The reader wants to live here.

**Middle**: Growing unease that never fully displaces the warmth. The reader begins to recognize the house's beauty as a mechanism — the way a pitcher plant's interior is slippery and fragrant. The narrowing threshold, the root through the pillow, the heartbeat matching the walls. Horror and desire coexist.

**End**: The reader feels the ache of an unresolvable dilemma — the same ache as choosing between freedom and belonging, between anonymous food and food that knows your name. The final scene should produce not resolution but appetite. The reader should want to cook something. The reader should want to go home. The reader should be unsure whether those two impulses are the same.

### 6. Formula Integration

**AuthorA — Karen Russell**: Most visible in the **fig wasp scene** and throughout the narration's voice. The deadpan delivery of impossible facts (the house sings, the walls have a heartbeat, the figs taste of ancestors) without ever marking them as strange. The specific, playful sensory inventions — "margarine wavelengths" becomes "the air smells like a bruised church" or similar Russell-esque synesthetic metaphors. The insect-scale attention to the wasp, the biological specificity of the ostiole, the willingness to make the premise simultaneously whimsical and disturbing.

**AuthorB — Laura Esquivel**: Most visible in the **mole at dawn** and **taco on the curb** scenes. Every food scene must land in the body — the reader should taste the ancho chile, feel the chocolate dissolve, experience the salsa verde as a physical event. Esquivel's method is not metaphor but literalism: the food does not represent emotion, the food IS the emotion. The mole scene should read like a recipe for grief. The taco scene should make the reader's mouth water with the taste of freedom.

**WorkX — The House of the Spirits (Allende)**: Most visible in the **overall structure** — three generations compressed into key episodes, each inheriting and transforming the previous generation's relationship with the house. The grandmother planted the fig and built the kitchen. The mother expanded the kitchen's power, made the house more efficient, more digestive. The granddaughter inherits a machine she didn't build. The walk-through with Diego functions as the structural reveal — decades of accumulation exposed in a single scene. The notebook/record device is replaced by the walls themselves: the house IS the record.

**WorkY — Piranesi (Clarke)**: Most visible in the **opening pages and the emotional question**. The house must be presented as Piranesi's House is presented — a self-contained world with its own logic, genuinely beautiful, genuinely kind (in its way), and genuinely total. Paloma's love for the house must read as Piranesi's love for the House: sincere, devotional, and unsettling only to the reader, never to the narrator (until the fig wasp moment cracks the seal). The question of whether leaving constitutes rescue or loss must remain genuinely open at the end.

### 7. Title

**Paloma and the Singing Fig**

Structure: Name + conjunction + noun phrase. Does not start with "The." Evokes fabulism's fairy-tale register without being precious. "Singing fig" captures both the aeolian harp mechanism and the Esquivel insistence on food that does more than nourish. The name Paloma (dove) carries its own resonance — a bird in a beautiful cage — without the story ever needing to say so.

**Alternate titles considered:**
- *Every Fig Contains a Wasp* (too didactic, too much like an essay title)
- *Ostiole* (too obscure, though beautiful)
- *Recipes for the Dead* (too Gothic, undersells the warmth)
- *What the Kitchen Digests* (pattern too close to existing title "What the Creek Said")
