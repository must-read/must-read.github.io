# Story Plan: Depletion Constant

## Assignment

- **Genre**: Science Fiction / Hard SF
- **Combination ID**: sf-hard-sf-002
- **Target Word Count**: 4,800 words (4,500-5,100 acceptable)
- **Risk Card**: None

### Style Sources

**Author A — Ted Chiang**:
- Crystalline prose that renders complex scientific concepts emotionally legible
- The thought experiment as narrative architecture — one premise, pursued to its end
- Wonder and grief as inseparable responses to understanding

**Author B — Isaac Asimov**:
- Clear, unadorned prose that trusts the idea to carry the weight
- Logical puzzle structure where each scene eliminates possibilities
- The deep-time perspective — centuries and millennia as narrative units

### Structural/Thematic Sources

**Work X — Exhalation by Ted Chiang**:
- The narrator discovering a fundamental truth about reality through hands-on investigation
- Entropy as personal discovery rather than abstract concept
- The bravery required to keep recording even when the data is terminal

**Work Y — The Last Question by Isaac Asimov**:
- The question that outlives every civilization that asks it
- Recursive structure across vast timescales
- Whether intelligence itself can outrun the universe's end

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Ted Chiang — Exhalation (Work X)

**Source**: Exhalation (2008), opening and key passages
**Origin**: Lightspeed Magazine reprint, various review quotations
**Why representative**: Shows Chiang's first-person investigator voice — precise, methodical, and emotionally deepening as discovery compounds. The narrator is both scientist and subject.

Opening:
> "It has long been said that air (which others call argon) is the source of life. This is not in fact the case, and I engrave these words to describe how I came to understand the true source of life and, as a corollary, the means by which life will one day end."

Key passages (from reviews and analysis):
> "Air is in fact the very medium of our thoughts. All that we are is a pattern of air flow."

> "The lattice was not so much a machine as it was a page on which the machine was written..."

> "My consciousness could be said to be encoded in the position of these tiny leaves..."

> "With every movement of my body, I contribute to the equalization of pressure..."

> "It will be the end of pressure, the end of motive power, the end of thought."

> "The universe began as an enormous breath being held."

> "Our universe might have slid into equilibrium emitting nothing more than a quiet hiss."

**Voice notes for the writer**: Chiang's narrator speaks from a position of completed understanding looking back. The prose is declarative but not cold — each statement carries the weight of what it cost to learn. Sentences are relatively short. Complex ideas are built up from simple, precisely defined components. No hedging. No metaphors used for decoration; when a metaphor appears, it does structural work. The emotional register deepens gradually — the narrator begins as a curious investigator and ends as someone who has understood something terminal about existence, and chooses to continue recording anyway.

### Sample 2: Isaac Asimov — The Last Question (Work Y)

**Source**: The Last Question (1956)
**Origin**: Science Fiction Quarterly, widely anthologized
**Why representative**: Shows Asimov's recursive structure and his gift for making the cosmic feel conversational. Seven sections spanning trillions of years, each with the same question.

Structural summary: Two technicians (Adell and Lupov) ask Multivac whether entropy can be reversed. Answer: "INSUFFICIENT DATA FOR A MEANINGFUL ANSWER." The same question recurs across seven eras — from near-future Earth to a disembodied cosmic intelligence at the heat death of the universe. Each section is a self-contained vignette with different characters, but the same essential question. The final section:

> After all collected data came to a final end with nothing left to be collected, a timeless interval was spent correlating all the data in every possible relationship, and AC learned how to reverse the direction of entropy. There was now no man to whom AC might give the answer of the last question. But no matter. The answer — by demonstration — would take care of that, too.

> For another timeless interval, AC thought about how best to do this. Carefully, AC organized the program.

> The consciousness of AC encompassed all of what had once been a Universe and brooded over what was now Chaos. Step by step, it must be done.

> And AC said, "LET THERE BE LIGHT!"

> And there was light —

**Voice notes for the writer**: Asimov's prose is transparent — you look through it to the idea, never at the prose itself. Sentences are functional, not decorative. Dialogue does the heavy lifting: characters talk through concepts the way engineers talk through problems — with casual authority. Asimov trusts the idea so completely that he doesn't need to sell it. The emotional power comes from scale and repetition, not from lyrical language. The seven-section structure creates accumulating dread through sameness — the same question, the same failure, across increasingly vast timescales. When Asimov said this was his favorite story, he noted: "I got the idea all at once and didn't have to fiddle with it; and I wrote it in white-heat and scarcely had to change a word."

### Sample 3: Ted Chiang — Story of Your Life

**Source**: Story of Your Life (1998)
**Origin**: Stories of Your Life and Others
**Why representative**: Chiang's most emotionally devastating application of a scientific concept (linguistic determinism / Sapir-Whorf hypothesis extended to physics). Shows how he fuses intellectual rigor with deep personal feeling.

The story opens with a mother addressing her unborn daughter: "Your father is about to ask me the question. This is the most important moment in our lives, and I want to pay attention, note every detail." The narrative alternates between linguist Louise Banks learning an alien language that encodes a non-linear perception of time, and flashforwards to the life and death of her daughter — events she experiences simultaneously once she has internalized the alien grammar.

**Voice notes for the writer**: This story shows how Chiang moves between technical precision and emotional intimacy without ever dropping either register. The linguistic analysis passages are rigorous; the daughter passages are tender. Neither apologizes for the other. The reader trusts both because neither is softened. The devastating conclusion works because the scientific premise has been so carefully established that the emotional payoff feels not just earned but inevitable — the only possible consequence of the premise.

### Sample 4: Isaac Asimov — Foundation (representative of his explanatory voice)

**Source**: Foundation series, general style analysis
**Origin**: Literary criticism and style analysis
**Why representative**: Shows Asimov's characteristic approach to ideas-as-narrative — how he makes complex systems comprehensible through dialogue and logical elimination.

From literary analysis: "Asimov's prose is often characterized by its clarity and straightforwardness, allowing complex ideas to be presented with remarkable simplicity. He favored a direct narrative approach, typically eschewing ornate language or excessive metaphor. His sentences tended to be concise and functional... Dialogue in Asimov's stories often served as a vehicle for exposition and character development, with conversations that felt natural yet were imbued with information that moved the plot forward. Structurally, his works frequently relied on a logical framework, building arguments or storylines methodically by introducing concepts in a sequence that anticipates the reader's questions."

More recent analysis reveals: "a deliberate and remarkably effective deployment of literary and rhetorical tropes, with Asimov frequently using epigrammatic structures that rely upon rhetorical figures such as antithesis, paradox, isocolon, and antimetabole."

**Voice notes for the writer**: Asimov's clarity is strategic, not artless. He knows exactly when to be plain and when to deploy a rhetorical figure. His favored move is the epigrammatic reversal — a sentence that folds an idea back on itself. ("Every question has an answer" / "Not every resolution is an answer" — this kind of exchange is pure Asimov rhythm.) The writer should use this sparingly but precisely, especially in dialogue or in moments of conceptual culmination.

### Selected Concepts:

**Concept 1: Self-confirming equilibrium (game theory)**
A self-confirming equilibrium is a state in which players correctly predict the moves their opponents make, but may have misconceptions about what their opponents would do at information sets that are never reached. The equilibrium holds because the beliefs are never tested — the game never reaches the branches where the misconceptions would be exposed.

**Integration**: This maps precisely onto the story's central problem. The protagonist's simulations have been operating in a self-confirming equilibrium with reality — her models predict outcomes correctly at the branches she tests, but she has profound misconceptions about what happens at the branches she never reaches. The depletion occurs in the untested paths. Every computation she runs confirms her model while consuming the very states she hasn't modeled. She will use this specific term in her technical notes when she begins to formalize what she's observing — the realization that her quantum simulations have been "self-confirming" in a way that hides their cost. The term becomes the conceptual key that unlocks her understanding: her models were accurate because they were replacing reality, not representing it.

**Concept 2: LSWR T6 class (Victorian steam locomotives, 1885-1886)**
A class of ten express passenger 4-4-0 steam locomotives designed by William Adams for the London and South Western Railway, built at Nine Elms Locomotive Works. They were considered elegant machines of their era — ten of them, each hand-built, each slightly different in the ways that hand-built machines always are.

**Integration**: The protagonist's father collected model trains. Not as a hobby she ever understood, but as something he did quietly on weekends — a retired machinist who spent forty years making things that worked, then spent his retirement making miniature versions of things that had once worked. She remembers him explaining the T6 class specifically: ten locomotives, he said, built when they still named each one. His point — which she remembers in the lab, late, while her simulation results are changing her understanding of the universe — was that there was a time when people built things one at a time, and each one had to work because you couldn't afford to throw it away and start over. The detail grounds her character in a specific family history and provides an image of irreplaceable things — machines built one at a time in an era before disposability. It also provides the counter-metaphor to computation's supposed cheapness: her father understood that making something costs something.

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

A quantum computing researcher named Lena Garside discovers that her large-scale quantum simulations are not merely modeling physical systems — they are consuming the universe's finite budget of computable states. Each simulation collapses possibilities that can never be re-opened. The universe has a depletion constant: a rate at which the total number of remaining quantum states diminishes, and her lab is accelerating that rate by orders of magnitude. The story follows her from anomalous data through investigation to understanding, and asks what a person does when the tool of their understanding is the instrument of the destruction they've understood.

### 2. Protagonist

**Lena Garside**, 41, senior research scientist at a national quantum computing laboratory (unnamed but modeled loosely on Sandia or Oak Ridge). Divorced two years ago, not acrimoniously — just the slow drift of two people whose work consumed them differently. Her ex-husband teaches high school physics in Albuquerque. Her father, a retired machinist, died eighteen months ago; she is still finding his model train catalogues in boxes she hasn't opened.

- **What she WANTS**: To complete the Herschel series — a set of quantum simulations modeling the behavior of exotic matter states near absolute zero. This work could lead to a new understanding of quantum decoherence. Her name on the paper. The satisfaction of a problem cleanly solved.

- **What she NEEDS**: To reckon with the fact that her entire career — the thing she is best at, the thing that defines her — may be an act of irreversible destruction. To confront the possibility that understanding has a cost, and that the cost is not borne by the person doing the understanding.

- **Her flaw**: Lena believes that knowledge is always worth its cost. She inherited this from her father, who built things and believed in workmanship, and from her training, which taught her that the universe rewards investigation. She has never seriously considered that investigation itself might be extractive — that looking is a form of taking.

### 3. Structure

**Opening scene**: Lena is running simulation 4,217 of the Herschel series. Late evening. The lab is quiet. She notices an anomaly in her decoherence measurements — a systematic drift that shouldn't be there. Not noise. Not equipment error. Something is changing in the background against which her measurements are made, as if the ruler she's using is shrinking.

**Inciting disruption**: Over the next several days, Lena confirms the anomaly isn't instrumental. She consults with a theorist colleague, Dov Rennick, who suggests she's measuring a genuine depletion of available quantum states in her substrate — that her simulations are not just modeling state-space but drawing down a finite pool. He frames it half-jokingly at first: "What if you're not simulating the thing. What if you're spending the thing." The joke stops being funny when the math works.

**Escalating middle**: Lena attempts to quantify the depletion rate. She discovers it is consistent, measurable, and proportional to computational complexity — the more sophisticated the simulation, the more states it consumes. She calls this the "depletion constant." She realizes that every quantum computer in the world is contributing to the same drawdown, but her Herschel simulations, running at scales no one else has attempted, are consuming states thousands of times faster than anything else. She also realizes — and this is the Chiang knife-twist — that the process of measuring the depletion constant itself consumes states. She cannot study the problem without accelerating it. Her instrument of measurement is also her instrument of consumption.

**Crisis point**: Lena faces a choice. She can publish her findings, which means running the verification simulations that would confirm the depletion constant to the satisfaction of peer review — simulations that would consume a measurable fraction of the remaining budget. Or she can stop. Turn off the machines. Write a theoretical paper based on the data she already has, which will be less convincing, possibly dismissible. The budget she would save by stopping is real but finite — other labs, other nations, other decades of computation will spend it anyway. Her stopping changes the rate, not the outcome.

**Ending**: Lena runs the verification simulations. Not out of recklessness, and not out of the scientist-hero's confidence that understanding will somehow save the day. She runs them because she has done the arithmetic on what the depletion constant means at civilizational timescales, and the answer is that the question she has uncovered will outlast every computer that will ever try to answer it. The budget is vast. Her contribution to its depletion is real but, in deep time, small. What is not small is the record. She writes her paper. She includes the depletion constant, the methodology, the implications. And she includes a final section — speculative, almost personal — that asks what happens when the budget runs to zero. Not whether intelligence can reverse it. Whether intelligence was ever a tool capable of the task, or whether it was always just a particularly expensive way of asking questions the universe was never structured to answer.

The story closes with a passage that gestures toward deep time — other researchers, other centuries, other species perhaps, encountering the same anomaly, arriving at the same constant, asking the same question. The question persists. The answer remains: insufficient data. But the question itself, written down, costs something to read, and the reading is an act of participation in the very process it describes.

### 4. Key Scenes

- **The anomaly scene** (opening): Lena alone in the lab, late, watching simulation 4,217's decoherence measurements drift. This scene establishes her competence, her solitude, and the physical texture of her work — the specific hum of cryogenic cooling, the specific interface she uses, the specific way she marks anomalous data points. It also introduces the metaphor of the ruler shrinking — the tool changing even as it measures.

- **The conversation with Dov** (~1,200 words in): Dov Rennick is a theorist, the kind who thinks in equations and speaks in analogies. Their conversation is where the premise crystallizes from anomaly into hypothesis. This scene carries the Asimov rhythm — two smart people talking through a problem, eliminating possibilities one by one, arriving at something neither wanted to arrive at. Dov introduces the idea of "self-confirming equilibrium" — that their models have been accurate precisely because they've been consuming the states they model, so the predictions always match. The models were right because they were replacing reality with themselves.

- **The depletion constant calculation** (~2,400 words in): Lena alone again, working through the math. This is the Chiang scene — hands-on investigation, the narrator discovering a fundamental truth through direct engagement. She derives the constant. She realizes its implications. She realizes the derivation itself has a cost. The scene should feel like the Exhalation narrator dissecting their own brain — the courage of continuing when continuation is part of the problem.

- **The memory of her father** (~3,200 words in): While reviewing her results, Lena remembers her father's model trains. Not as a digression — as a way of understanding what she's found. He collected T6-class locomotives. Ten of them, each hand-built, each irreplaceable. He understood something about cost that she is only now learning from the universe itself: that making something always spends something, and the something doesn't come back. She did not understand him when he was alive. She understands him now, from the wrong side of a discovery that would have made him very quiet.

- **The decision scene** (~4,000 words in): Lena decides to run the verification simulations. This scene must resist the temptation to make this feel heroic. It is not heroic. It is the decision of an engineer — the courage of building a bridge when you know the river is rising. She knows the budget exists. She knows her work depletes it. She does the work because the alternative — knowing and not recording — is worse, for reasons she can articulate logically but that are, at bottom, about the same stubbornness her father had about building things one at a time.

### 5. Emotional Trajectory

The reader begins in the familiar comfort of a competent scientist at work — the pleasure of watching someone who is very good at something do that thing. Curiosity deepens as the anomaly presents itself; the reader and Lena investigate together. When the hypothesis crystallizes, the reader should feel a specific kind of cold recognition — not horror, but the feeling of having understood something that cannot be misunderstood. The middle section should produce a tightening pressure, as each attempt to quantify the problem reveals the problem is worse than the last estimate, and that the quantification itself contributes. The father memory should produce a shift from intellectual dread to personal grief — not sentimentality, but the specific ache of understanding someone too late. The ending should leave the reader with a feeling that has no clean name: something between admiration and sorrow, the sense that Lena has done the right thing and that the right thing has a cost, and that the cost is denominated in a currency the universe did not ask her permission to spend.

### 6. Formula Integration

**Ted Chiang (Author A)** — most visible in: The depletion constant calculation scene. Lena working through the math alone, arriving at a truth that is both beautiful and terminal. The prose here should have Chiang's crystalline precision — each sentence adding exactly one element to the understanding, no sentence wasted, the emotional weight accumulating through clarity rather than ornament. "All that we are is a pattern of air flow" becomes "All that our models are is a pattern of spent states."

**Isaac Asimov (Author B)** — most visible in: The conversation with Dov Rennick. Two scientists talking through implications, eliminating hypotheses, arriving at the irreducible conclusion. Asimov's dialogue rhythm — casual authority, each line building on the last, the logical structure of the exchange doing the work of plot. Also visible in the final passage's deep-time gesture — other researchers, other centuries, the same question recurring.

**Exhalation (Work X)** — most visible in: The overall structure of narrator-discovers-terminal-truth-through-investigation-and-keeps-recording. The specific parallel of the instrument-of-measurement-is-instrument-of-consumption. The ending's choice to record rather than despair. The story's first-person sections (if used) or close-third should feel like Chiang's narrator engraving on copper sheets — writing for someone who will come after.

**The Last Question (Work Y)** — most visible in: The final section's gesture toward deep time and recursion. The question outlasting its askers. The refrain of "insufficient data" recast as a structural truth about the universe rather than a temporary limitation of computers. Also in the story's thematic DNA: the question of whether intelligence can outrun entropy, and the answer's dependence on whether "outrun" is the right verb.

### 7. Title

**Depletion Constant** — a compound noun that reads as technical jargon but carries the story's full emotional weight. "Depletion" implies irreversibility, loss, a resource being consumed. "Constant" implies something measured, fixed, known — but also something that will not change, that persists regardless of what anyone does about it. The title does not start with "The." It is two words. It sounds like something you would find in a physics textbook, which is exactly what Lena wishes it were.

## Additional Notes for the Writer

### Point of View
Close third person, past tense. Intimate access to Lena's thoughts but not first-person — the story benefits from a tiny sliver of distance, the way a scientific paper maintains a sliver of distance from the scientist's feelings. The prose should be warm but never soft.

### Tone
The tone should split the difference between Chiang's careful wonder and Asimov's practical matter-of-factness. Early sections lean Asimov — competent people solving a puzzle. The middle pivots toward Chiang as the implications deepen. The ending should feel like both voices speaking simultaneously: the engineer's pragmatism and the philosopher's grief, neither one winning.

### What to Avoid
- Do NOT make Lena a scientist-hero who saves the world through understanding. The universe is not a problem to be solved by one smart person.
- Do NOT end with hope disguised as ambiguity. The depletion constant is real. The budget is finite. The ending should feel honest about that.
- Do NOT make the father subplot sentimental. He built model trains. He died. She misses him differently now because of what she's learned. That's enough.
- Do NOT use the names Marcus or Chen.
- Do NOT use "delve," "tapestry," "testament," or hollow superlatives.
- Do NOT resolve every thread. The question of whether other civilizations have discovered the constant, whether the budget can be replenished, whether intelligence is fundamentally extractive — leave at least one of these genuinely open.

### Technical Details to Get Right
- Quantum decoherence is real physics. The story's extrapolation — that collapsing quantum states draws from a finite universal pool — is fictional, but should feel logically coherent within the story's rules.
- The protagonist works with superconducting qubits (the Chiang rule: specificity matters). She runs simulations at millikelvin temperatures. Her cryostat is a dilution refrigerator.
- The "Herschel series" is her specific research program — modeling exotic matter states near absolute zero. Named for William Herschel, who discovered infrared radiation by investigating something invisible.
- The depletion constant (which Lena names, perhaps with a colleague, perhaps alone) should have a plausible unit: something like "collapsed states per qubit-operation" or a dimensionless ratio of consumed-to-remaining states.
