# Story Plan: wes-literary-001

## Assignment

- **Genre**: Western / Literary Western
- **Combination ID**: wes-literary-001
- **AuthorA**: Annie Proulx — Brutal, compressed, landscape-saturated prose; the West as unforgiving
- **AuthorB**: Larry McMurtry — Elegiac, character-rich storytelling; the death of the Old West; warmth and loss
- **WorkX (structure)**: *Plainsong* by Kent Haruf — Spare, chapter-alternating parallel lives in a small town; no quotation marks; the architecture of community built sentence by patient sentence
- **WorkY (themes)**: *Housekeeping* by Marilynne Robinson — Transience vs. rootedness; the house as failed anchor; women choosing drift over domesticity; what it means to stay and what it means to go
- **Target word count**: 5,800 words (acceptable range: 5,500–6,100)
- **Risk card**: None

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Annie Proulx — *Close Range: Wyoming Stories*

**Source**: AuthorA — Annie Proulx
**Origin**: "People in Hell Just Want a Drink of Water" from *Close Range* (1999)
**Why representative**: Demonstrates Proulx's compressed, landscape-saturated prose where the land itself carries moral weight, and sensory detail functions as emotional content.

> "The morning light flooded the rim of the world, poured through the window glass, colored the wall and floor, laid its yellow blanket on the reeking bed, the kitchen table and the cups of cold coffee. There was no cloud in the sky. Grasshoppers hit against the east wall in their black and yellow thousands."

> "Dangerous and indifferent ground; against its fixed mass the tragedies of people count for nothing although the signs of misadventure are everywhere."

> "Only earth and sky matter. Only the endlessly repeated flood of morning light. You begin to see that God does not owe us much beyond that."

**Additional sample** from "The Half-Skinned Steer":

> "He was half an hour past Kearney, Nebraska, when the full moon rose, an absurd visage balanced in his rearview mirror, above it a curled wig of a cloud, filamented edges like platinum hairs. He felt his swollen nose, palped his chin, tender from the stun of the air bag."

**Additional sample** from "Brokeback Mountain":

> "They were raised on small, poor ranches in opposite corners of the state, Jack Twist in Lightning Flat up on the Montana border, Ennis del Mar from around Sage, near the Utah line, both high school dropout country boys with no prospects, brought up to hard work and privation, both rough-mannered, rough-spoken, inured to the stoic life."

> "There was some open space between what he knew and what he tried to believe, but nothing could be done about it, and if you can't fix it you've got to stand it."

**Key stylistic elements for the writer**:
- Sentences packed with physical detail, no filler
- Landscape and weather carry emotional weight; they ARE the emotional content, not a backdrop for it
- Compressed compound constructions — multiple clauses joined by commas, building velocity
- Rare use of interiority; character emerges through action and observation
- Brutal economy — what she omits matters as much as what she includes
- Frequent sentence fragments that land like blows
- Place names as incantation (Lightning Flat, Sage, the Utah line)

### Sample 2: Larry McMurtry — *Lonesome Dove*

**Source**: AuthorB — Larry McMurtry
**Origin**: *Lonesome Dove* (1985), opening chapter and Clara's speech
**Why representative**: Shows McMurtry's warmth, his character-rich observation of ordinary life, the elegiac tone of people caught in a world that has moved past them, and dialogue that reveals character through everyday rhythm.

> "The springhouse was a little lumpy adobe building, so cool on the inside that Augustus would have been tempted to live in it had it not been for its popularity with black widows, yellow jackets and centipedes."

> "Evening took a long time getting to Lonesome Dove, but when it came it was a comfort. For most of the hours of the day — and most of the months of the year — the sun had the town trapped deep in dust, far out in the chaparral flats, a heaven for snakes and horned toads, roadrunners and stinging lizards, but a hell for pigs and Tennesseans."

> "I'm sorry you and Gus McCrae ever met. All you two done was ruin one another, not to mention those close to you... You men and your promises: they're just excuses to do what you plan to do anyway, which is leave. You think you've always done right — that's your ugly pride, Mr. Call. But you never did right and it would be a sad woman that needed anything from you. You're a vain coward, for all your fighting. I despised you then, for what you were, and I despise you now, for what you're doing."

**Key stylistic elements for the writer**:
- Warmth in the observation — even harsh truths delivered with intimacy
- Long, unhurried sentences that meander the way conversation does
- Characters defined by small physical habits (adjusting a hat, checking a porch, the way someone sits a horse)
- Humor woven into grief; the two are never entirely separate
- Women's intelligence and rage given full articulation (Clara's speech is the moral center of the novel)
- The elegiac register — everything is already ending, even as it's being lived
- Accumulation of homely detail that builds into something devastating

### Sample 3: Kent Haruf — *Plainsong*

**Source**: WorkX (structure) — Kent Haruf
**Origin**: *Plainsong* (1999), various passages
**Why representative**: Shows Haruf's signature structural choices — no quotation marks, spare dialogue that lives on the same plane as narration, parallel lives unfolding with patient silence.

> That's too much, their father said. Should we give it back? No, he said. He took his hat off and scratched the back of his head and put his hat back on. I guess not. That would be an insult.

> He told me things. Like what for instance? Like once he said I had beautiful eyes. He said my eyes were like black diamonds lit up on a starry night. They are, honey. But nobody ever told me. No, Maggie said, they never do.

> It's so quiet out there, the girl said. I don't know what I'm supposed to do. We eat supper. They read the paper. I go into my room and study. And that's about it. Every day it's like that. Is everything else all right? Oh, they're kind to me. If that's what you mean. They're nice enough. But they don't talk, Maggie said.

> You're not talking to her, Maggie Jones said. You and Raymond don't talk like you should to that girl. Women want to hear some conversation in the evening.

**Key structural elements for the writer**:
- NO quotation marks. Speech, thought, and narration exist on the same plane. This is MANDATORY for this story.
- Alternating sections between parallel lives that never explicitly comment on each other
- Dialogue that reads like narration — no speech tags set it apart
- Silence as a structural element — what characters DON'T say is as present as what they do
- Patient accumulation of domestic detail (taking a hat off, scratching a head, putting it back on)
- Community built sentence by sentence, not through declarations of belonging
- The emotional weight is in the juxtaposition of parallel lives, not in any single storyline

### Sample 4: Marilynne Robinson — *Housekeeping*

**Source**: WorkY (themes) — Marilynne Robinson
**Origin**: *Housekeeping* (1980), various chapters
**Why representative**: Shows Robinson's meditation on transience, the house as metaphor and material reality, and prose that achieves luminous philosophical weight without abandoning specificity.

> "My name is Ruth. I grew up with my younger sister, Lucille, under the care of my grandmother, Mrs. Sylvia Foster, and when she died, of her sisters-in-law, Misses Lily and Nona Foster, and when they fled, of her daughter, Mrs. Sylvia Fisher."

> "I simply let the darkness in the sky become coextensive with the darkness in my skull and bowels and bones. Everything that falls upon the eye is apparition, a sheet dropped over the world's true workings. The nerves and the brain are tricked, and one is left with dreams that these specters loose their hands from ours and walk away, the curve of the back and the swing of the coat so familiar as to imply that they should be permanent fixtures of the world, when in fact nothing is more perishable."

> "To crave and to have are as like as a thing and its shadow. For when does a berry break upon the tongue as sweetly as when one longs to taste it, and when is the taste refracted into so many hues and savors of ripeness and earth, and when do our senses know any thing so utterly as when we lack it?"

> "Fingerbone was never an impressive town. It was chastened by an outsized landscape and extravagant weather..."

> "She was a music I no longer heard, that rang in my mind, itself and nothing else, lost to all sense, but not perished, not perished."

> "Sylvie pushed at the water with the side of her foot. A ribbed circle spread to the four walls and the curves of its four sides rebounded, interpenetrated, and the orderly ranks of light swept and swung about the room."

> "I have never distinguished readily between thinking and dreaming. I know my life would be much different if I could ever say, This I have learned from my senses, while that I have merely imagined."

**Key thematic elements for the writer**:
- The house is both literal structure and philosophical argument — it can fail as both simultaneously
- Transience and rootedness are NOT opposites but two responses to the same condition
- Women who choose drift are not punished for it, nor romanticized — they simply are
- The accumulation and loss of domestic order (dishes, curtains, stored newspapers) as spiritual condition
- Water as the force that undoes housekeeping — flood, rain, the lake that swallows
- Memory and perception blur; what is imagined is as real as what is experienced
- The prose is meditative but never abstract — every thought is grounded in a physical image

### 2 Selected Concepts

**1. Pronghorn migration and fences**
Integration: The pronghorn that can't jump fences — built for speed on open ground but defeated by four strands of wire — will be a recurring presence in the landscape sections. The staying woman, Edith, watches them press against the bottom wire every autumn, belly-low, sliding under or turning back. She removes the bottom strand of barbed wire from her own fence line each October and replaces it after the herd passes, a ritual of accommodation that her neighbors find pointless. This detail embodies the story's central tension: the land demands flexibility, and the structures people build on it (fences, houses, marriages) are tests of whether you'll adapt to the land or force the land to adapt to you. The pronghorn go under because they cannot go over. Edith stays because she has learned to go under too.

**2. Selenite crystals**
Integration: Edith's daughter, Nola (the woman who leaves), collected selenite crystals as a child from a dry wash behind the house — transparent, moon-named, fragile gypsum formations that grow in the evaporation basins of shallow salt flats. She kept them on the windowsill of her room in the shotgun house, a row of cloudy prisms that caught the morning light. When she leaves, she leaves the crystals. They remain on the sill through the whole story, mentioned twice: once when Edith dusts them, and once when the house sits empty and the light passes through them to no one. The selenite is the opposite of the house — a thing formed by absence (evaporation), transparent where the house is opaque, beautiful because it preserves the shape of what has already gone. Nola's taste for these crystals tells you everything about why she left: she was drawn to things made by disappearance.

## 1. Premise

In a three-room shotgun house on a declining cattle operation outside Buckshot, Wyoming, a woman named Edith Oster has lived for forty-one years — through a marriage, a daughter, a widowing, and the slow contraction of the ranch to a few dozen head on leased BLM ground. Her daughter Nola, thirty-four, left for Billings nine years ago after her father's death and has not returned. In alternating sections (Edith's days / Nola's days), the story follows three weeks in late October when the Aermotor windmill that feeds the stock tank breaks and Edith must decide whether to spend the money to fix it — money that would come from selling the last of the cattle — or let the operation die. In Billings, Nola works at a title company recording land transactions and learns that a subdivision developer has bought the grazing lease Edith depends on. The story asks: What does it mean to hold ground that is actively refusing to hold you? And is leaving an act of wisdom or a failure of nerve — and can it be both?

---

## 2. Protagonist

**Edith Oster**, 63, rancher's widow. Lives alone in a wood-frame shotgun house (front room, bedroom, kitchen in a line) seven miles outside Buckshot, Wyoming, population 340 and falling.

- **What she WANTS**: To keep the ranch running through one more winter. Specifically, she wants to repair the windmill so the stock tank fills and the cattle can stay on the BLM lease through December.
- **What she NEEDS**: To understand that staying is not the same as loyalty — that her refusal to adapt (sell cattle, leave the house, accept Nola's absence as a choice rather than a betrayal) has calcified into a form of self-punishment she mistakes for virtue.
- **Her flaw**: Edith equates rootedness with moral superiority. She privately judges everyone who has left — Nola, the neighbors who sold to developers, the families who moved to Casper for oil work — as people who broke a promise. She cannot see that her staying has become its own kind of running: running from the admission that the place she loves has already ended, that the ranch her husband built was never viable, and that the house she maintains so carefully was built on shale with a crooked doorframe because her husband's father didn't know what he was doing and was too proud to ask.

**Nola Oster**, 34, title researcher. Lives in a one-bedroom apartment in Billings, Montana. The parallel figure, NOT a second protagonist — she exists in her own sections but the story belongs to Edith. Nola is practical where Edith is stubborn. She left after her father's death not in grief but in recognition: the ranch was ending and she did not want to be the one holding it when it did. She did the dishes first. She left on a Tuesday.

---

## 3. Structure

The story uses Haruf's parallel-lives architecture: alternating numbered sections (no chapter titles) between EDITH and NOLA. Six to eight sections total, roughly equal in length, alternating. NO QUOTATION MARKS throughout — all speech rendered as narration, as in Haruf.

The sections do not directly comment on each other. They do not mirror. They are two lives in the same landscape (one still in it, one 300 miles north of it), in the same weather system (late October, the killing frost approaching), connected by the same house but experiencing it from opposite positions.

### Opening image/scene (THE HOOK)
Edith is outside at dawn, refilling the stock tank by hand with a garden hose from the cistern because the Aermotor windmill stopped turning three days ago. The fan assembly is seized. She can hear the metal groaning in the wind but the blades won't turn. The cattle are at the fence line watching her, thirty-one head, and she counts them the way she counts them every morning — not because the number changes but because counting is what she does, the way breathing is what she does. The hose takes forty minutes to fill the tank. She stands in the cold and holds it. The Bighorn foothills are visible to the west, the first snow on the peaks, and the light is the color of old paper.

### The inciting disruption
Two disruptions, one per life:
- **Edith**: A windmill repairman from Sheridan gives her the estimate: $2,200 for parts, $800 for labor. She has $1,400 in the checking account. The rest would require selling eight head at the Kaycee auction, which would drop her below the minimum for the BLM lease. She can either fix the windmill and lose the lease, or keep the cattle and haul water by hand until the cistern empties, which at current draw will be mid-November.
- **Nola**: At the title company, she processes a deed transfer that includes the legal description of the BLM grazing allotment her mother depends on. A subdivision company out of Denver has acquired the lease. Nola does not call her mother. She stares at the plat map and recognizes the section lines, the creek, the road — the place she grew up rendered in surveyor's language, stripped of everything except boundary and measurement.

### The escalating middle
- Edith begins hauling water by hand from the cistern. The physical labor of it structures her days: two hours morning, two hours evening. She develops a routine that is punishing but sustainable. Her neighbor, Dale Kessler, offers to run a water line from his well. She refuses. The refusal is quiet, not dramatic — she says she'll figure it out, and Dale knows what that means.
- Nola, in Billings, begins looking at the plat book records for her mother's property. She discovers that the original homestead patent, filed in 1919 under the Stock-Raising Homestead Act by Edith's husband's grandfather (Alvar Oster), required range improvements but no cultivation — that the land was never meant to be farmed, only grazed. The dower interest Edith holds is legally strong but practically meaningless: you can own a house on land that has no economic function. Nola does not tell her mother any of this.
- The pronghorn appear. Every October they press south through the Oster property on their migration. Edith takes down the bottom wire of her fence, as she has every year since the 1990s when a wildlife biologist told her the pronghorn can't jump. She stands and watches them pass — thirty, forty, sixty animals moving low and fast across the brown grass, going through the gap she made.
- Edith's routine of hauling water is interrupted when the cistern drops below the intake pipe. She drives to Buckshot to fill five-gallon jugs at the hardware store. The hardware store owner, a woman named Gayle, asks if she needs help. Edith says no. On the drive back, one of the jugs tips and soaks the truck bed and she pulls over and sits in the cab and does not cry but sits with her hands on the steering wheel looking at the clinker hills — the red-capped ridges of burnt coal that line the Powder River drainage, land that burned from the inside out and is harder for it.

### The crisis point
Dale Kessler comes by to tell Edith what she already senses: the BLM lease is being transferred. A developer out of Denver. The cattle will have to come off the allotment by December 1. Dale is kind about it — he's lost ground too, sold half his own herd two years ago — but the information is final. Without the lease, the cattle have nowhere to graze. Without the cattle, there is no ranch. Without the ranch, the house is just a house — three rooms in a line on ground that owes her nothing.

Edith drives to the BLM field office in Buffalo. She sits in the truck in the parking lot for twenty minutes. She goes inside. She learns the transfer is legitimate. She asks what her options are. The clerk, who is young and has never worked cattle, says she can apply for a new allotment in the next allocation cycle, which is three years out. Edith thanks him and leaves.

Meanwhile, Nola has drafted a letter to her mother — a practical letter explaining the legal situation, the lease transfer, the homestead patent history, the options. She prints it. She does not mail it. She puts it in her desk drawer. After work she drives to a bar she doesn't usually go to and drinks two beers and drives home and stands in her apartment looking at the wall where she has hung nothing.

### The ending
Edith sells the cattle. Not all at once — she calls the Kaycee auction and arranges to trailer them in two loads, sixteen and fifteen. The selling takes four days. She writes the check to the windmill repairman from the auction proceeds — not because the windmill is needed now (there are no cattle to water) but because a windmill that doesn't turn is a broken thing on the skyline, and she will not live under a broken thing.

The last section is Edith alone in the house. It is early November. The pronghorn have passed. The fence wire is back up. The windmill turns again in the wind, pumping water into a stock tank that nothing drinks from. The house is warm on the kitchen side where the stove is and cold on the front-room side where the stove is not. The selenite crystals on the sill in Nola's old room catch the late light. Edith is sweeping the kitchen floor — not because it is dirty but because her hands know the broom and the broom knows the floor and the floor is the one surface in her life that responds to effort, that is clean when she cleans it and dirty when she doesn't and in this is as reliable as the morning.

She does NOT have an epiphany. She does NOT understand anything new about herself or Nola or the land. She sweeps. The windmill turns. The stock tank fills. The water has nowhere to go.

**Open thread**: Nola's unmailed letter. We never learn whether she sends it. We never learn whether she comes back. The story ends with Edith, not with Nola, and the question of whether the two women will ever occupy the same house again is left completely open.

## 4. Key Scenes (5 scenes that MUST appear)

1. **The stock tank filling by hose** (opening): Edith standing in the cold, holding a garden hose, counting cattle. Establishes her physical presence, the scale of her labor, the landscape. The Aermotor windmill groaning overhead but not turning. This scene does double duty: it introduces the material crisis (no water) and the spiritual condition (a woman who fills what is empty because filling is what she does).

2. **Nola reading the plat map**: In the title company office, fluorescent light, the legal description of her childhood rendered in sections and township ranges. She traces the creek with her finger. The map shows no house, no barn, no windmill — just boundaries and measurements. This is the Housekeeping inversion: the house as failed anchor, visible only in its absence from the official record.

3. **The pronghorn passing through**: Edith takes down the bottom wire and stands watching. The animals move low and fast, their bodies amber in the late light, pressing through the gap in a line. This scene must be written with Proulx's landscape compression — the animals ARE the theme, not an illustration of it. They cannot jump. They go under. They have been doing this for ten thousand years.

4. **Edith at the hardware store / the spilled water**: The drive to Buckshot, the jugs in the truck bed, the one that tips. The moment she pulls over and sits with her hands on the wheel. Not crying. The clinker hills. This is McMurtry's territory: the small failure that carries the weight of all the larger ones, observed with warmth and without sentimentality.

5. **The empty house paragraph**: After Edith leaves (to sell cattle, to go to the BLM office — any moment she is away), the story stays with the house for a beat. The stain on the ceiling. The stuck drawer. The selenite crystals. The floor, cold on one side, warm on the other. The house existing without the person who gives it purpose. This fulfills Proulx's directive from the author meeting and is structurally necessary: the house must exist as an entity separate from the people in it, because the house will outlast them and then not outlast them.

## 5. The Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning**: The reader feels the weight of routine — the satisfaction and the exhaustion of a life organized around physical labor that has a clear purpose (water the cattle, maintain the fence, keep the house). There is dignity in it and the reader respects it.

**Middle**: The respect curdles slightly. As the obstacles accumulate and Edith refuses help, the reader begins to feel something closer to frustration laced with recognition — the feeling of watching someone you admire do something you know is wrong but cannot name. Is this strength or stubbornness? Is this loyalty or fear? The reader doesn't know. Nola's sections provide no relief because Nola's refusal to call is its own kind of stubbornness, a mirror Edith would not recognize.

**End**: The reader feels the specific ache of watching someone choose something that makes no practical sense and understanding, against all reason, why they choose it. The windmill turning over an empty stock tank is not tragic and not noble. It is just a thing that is happening on a piece of ground where other things used to happen and will not happen again. The reader puts the story down and thinks about the rooms in their own life that they maintain for no reason they can explain.

## 6. Formula Integration

### Annie Proulx — most visible in: The landscape sections and the pronghorn scene
The landscape must be written in Proulx's mode: compressed, physical, carrying emotional weight through specificity rather than metaphor. The clinker hills, the frost heave cracking the road, the grasshoppers against the wall, the wind that is never not there. Sentences that pile detail without connective tissue. The pronghorn scene in particular should feel like Proulx wrote it — animal bodies moving through wire and grass, described with the precision of someone who has watched this happen and does not sentimentalize it.

### Larry McMurtry — most visible in: The Dale Kessler conversations and the hardware store scene
McMurtry's warmth lives in the interactions between people who know each other well enough to say little. Dale's offer of the water line, Edith's refusal, the silence that follows — this is McMurtry's territory, where kindness and loss coexist in the same gesture. The hardware store, Gayle's question, the drive home with the spilled water — these carry McMurtry's gift for finding the devastating moment inside the mundane one. Clara's speech from *Lonesome Dove* resonates through Nola's unmailed letter: the accusation that the people who stay are not brave but vain, not loyal but proud.

### Kent Haruf — most visible in: The entire structural architecture
The parallel-lives structure IS Haruf. The story alternates between Edith and Nola without commentary, without mirrors, without explicit connection. The spare prose, the no-quotation-marks formatting, the patient sentence-by-sentence accumulation of domestic detail — these are all Haruf. The dialogue (what little there is) should read as Haruf's dialogue reads: indistinguishable from narration, speech on the same plane as thought and description.

### Marilynne Robinson — most visible in: The empty-house passage and the selenite crystals
Robinson's meditation on transience vs. rootedness is the story's philosophical engine. The empty-house paragraph — the stain, the drawer, the light on the floor — is Robinson's Fingerbone, a house that exists apart from the people who gave it meaning. The selenite crystals are Robinson's "apparition, a sheet dropped over the world's true workings" — objects that preserve the shape of an absence. The story's refusal to choose between staying and leaving, its insistence that both are real and neither is right, is Robinson's deepest contribution.

## 7. Title

**Windmill Over No Herd**

This title does not start with "The." It is a concrete image from the story's final beat — the Aermotor turning over a stock tank that nothing drinks from. The preposition "over" carries spatial and temporal weight: the windmill presides over emptiness, stands over a landscape that has moved past it. The absent herd gives the windmill its meaning (it pumps water for cattle) and simultaneously removes it (there are no cattle). The title is a complete sentence fragment — a noun phrase that describes a condition rather than an action — and it announces the story's emotional register without explaining it.

### Alternate titles considered:
- *Going Under* (too on-the-nose with the pronghorn metaphor)
- *Three Rooms in a Line* (too architectural; sounds like an essay)
- *Bottom Wire* (good but too cryptic without context)
- *Stock Tank* (too spare; doesn't carry enough)
- *Buckshot* (could work as a place-name title but sounds like a different genre)

## Notes for the Writer

1. **No quotation marks.** This is non-negotiable. All speech rendered as part of the narration. Study the Haruf samples above — the dialogue is embedded, not set apart. Use "she said" and "he said" but never quotation marks.

2. **Alternating sections.** Number them or separate with a line break. EDITH / NOLA / EDITH / NOLA / EDITH / NOLA / EDITH (or similar). The sections do not need to be exactly equal but should be roughly balanced. Edith gets the first and last sections.

3. **The landscape is not backdrop.** The clinker hills, the frost, the windmill, the stock tank, the pronghorn — these are not set dressing. They carry the story's meaning. Write them with Proulx's precision and physical weight.

4. **The ending is a task, not a thought.** Edith sweeping the floor. Her hands on the broom. Do NOT give her an epiphany. Do NOT let her understand something she didn't understand before. Let her hands work and let the reader do the understanding.

5. **Nola's letter stays in the drawer.** Do NOT resolve this. The letter exists. It is not sent. That is the open thread.

6. **The house must exist independently.** At least one passage where the house is described without anyone in it. This is the Robinson element and it must be earned — not a quick paragraph but a sustained beat where the house breathes on its own.

7. **Word count target: 5,800 (range 5,500–6,100).** This is a patient story. It needs room. Don't compress to hit a lower count — let the sections breathe. But don't pad either. Every sentence does work or it goes.

8. **Specific names and numbers.** Buckshot, Wyoming. Thirty-one head. $2,200 for parts. Seven miles from town. Forty-one years. These specifics are Proulx's gift and McMurtry's habit and Haruf's method. A world made real by counting.
