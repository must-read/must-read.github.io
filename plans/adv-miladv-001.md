# Story Plan: adv-miladv-001

**Genre**: Adventure
**Subgenre**: Military Adventure
**Combination ID**: adv-miladv-001

## Style Sources

**Author A — Robert Louis Stevenson**: Elegantly crafted Victorian adventure prose; moral complexity woven into action settings. First-person retrospective narration that appears transparent but conceals moral turbulence beneath. Sentences that arrive fully formed, composed, every clause earning its place. The narrator who seems reliable precisely because his prose is so polished — and that polish is itself suspicious. Physical description rendered with a painter's economy: "a tall, strong, heavy, nut-brown man, his tarry pigtail falling over the shoulder of his soiled blue coat." Characters revealed through gesture and accumulated detail rather than psychological exposition. The moral landscape as inseparable from the physical one.

**Author B — Wilbur Smith**: Sweeping African adventure sagas; vivid, kinetic landscape prose; visceral conflict and survival. The African bush as a character — not backdrop but active force. The mopane woodland that smells of turpentine, the Limpopo at low water the colour of milky tea, the kudu bull crashing through jesse bush sounding like someone tearing a bedsheet. Smith's men are bodies in landscapes, and the body's knowledge — the sweat, the breath, the ground underfoot — is the truest form of narrative. No character who explains his own psychology. The weight shows in how he holds the rifle, whether he flinches when the shot comes.

## Structural/Thematic Sources

**Work X — *The Thin Red Line* by James Jones (structure)**: Ensemble military narrative cycling through multiple soldier perspectives in combat. The company as a dysfunctional family. Each man given a specific consciousness — not just rank and name but a way of perceiving. The narrator's access to others is limited to faces, gestures, overheard words. What you see of the men around you in combat: your own panic and then their faces, and the faces tell you things the men behind them would never say. The cycling between perspectives as a structural engine for revealing how the same events register differently in different minds.

**Work Y — *All Quiet on the Western Front* by Remarque (themes)**: Destruction of youth by war. Camaraderie as the only truth the war does not corrupt. The impossibility of returning — men who cannot speak of what happened, who sit in their rooms and the war just sits inside them like a stone. The false version constructed for survival: "I remember his breath hanging like smoke in his wake." The gap between the story told at home and the story that actually happened. The voices of comrades as "more than motherliness and more than fear; they are the strongest, most comforting thing there is anywhere."

## Target Word Count
4,309 words (acceptable range: 4,009-4,609)

## Risk Card: #1 UNRELIABLE NARRATOR
The narrator presents a version of events that protects him from the truth of his own experience. He believes his own version completely — the self-deception is total, not performed. The events may be accurate; the inner account is fabricated. He tells us he felt courage where he felt terror, righteous anger where he felt nothing at all, conviction where he acted from cowardice. The reader gradually senses the weight of the lie — not through contradictions but through the excessive polish of the telling, the too-neat moral architecture, the way certain moments are described with suspicious precision while others are rushed past.

## Writing Samples

### Sample 1: Robert Louis Stevenson — *Treasure Island* (Opening / First-Person Retrospective)

**Source**: Author A
**Origin**: *Treasure Island*, Chapter 1
**Why representative**: Establishes Stevenson's retrospective first-person narration — the narrator looking back, composing memory into polished prose, the small details (tarry pigtail, broken nails, sabre cut) that create the illusion of honest recollection.

> Squire Trelawney, Dr. Livesey, and the rest of these gentlemen having asked me to write down the whole particulars about Treasure Island, from the beginning to the end, keeping nothing back but the bearings of the island, and that only because there is still treasure not yet lifted, I take up my pen in the year of grace 17__ and go back to the time when my father kept the Admiral Benbow inn and the brown old seaman with the sabre cut first took up his lodging under our roof.
>
> I remember him as if it were yesterday, as he came plodding to the inn door, his sea-chest following behind him in a hand-barrow—a tall, strong, heavy, nut-brown man, his tarry pigtail falling over the shoulder of his soiled blue coat, his hands ragged and scarred, with black, broken nails, and the sabre cut across one cheek, a dirty, livid white.

### Sample 2: Robert Louis Stevenson — *Kidnapped* (Moral Complexity in Action)

**Source**: Author A
**Origin**: *Kidnapped*, David Balfour's assessment of Alan Breck Stewart
**Why representative**: Demonstrates Stevenson's capacity for moral nuance within adventure — the narrator forced to acknowledge that moral and immoral exist in the same person, that loyalty and violence are not opposites.

> Alan's morals were tail-first; but he was ready to give his life for them, such as they were.

This compressed moral judgment — the narrator who simultaneously admires and condemns — is the Stevenson voice the story needs. The narrator of our story will make similar compressed judgments about his comrades, and the compressions will be where the lies hide.

### Sample 3: Wilbur Smith — Style Description and Characteristic Voice

**Source**: Author B
**Origin**: Composite from Smith's body of work and critical descriptions
**Why representative**: Smith's prose is kinetic, embodied, landscape-first. The African terrain is never neutral. Physical sensation carries psychological truth. Men do not explain themselves; they act, and the action reveals.

Key Smith techniques for the writer to internalize:
- Landscape described with sensory specificity: "the mopane woodland smells of turpentine"; the Limpopo "the colour of milky tea"; a kudu crashing through bush sounding "like someone tearing a bedsheet"
- Bodies in motion — sweat, breath, the ground underfoot
- Dialogue that reveals through what is not said
- Violence rendered without moral commentary — the act and the silence after
- Africa's light, heat, and terrain as forces that shape human behavior as much as any enemy

### Sample 4: James Jones — *The Thin Red Line* (Soldier's Interior)

**Source**: Work X
**Origin**: *The Thin Red Line*, First Sergeant Welsh's perspective
**Why representative**: Shows Jones's technique of rendering combat through individual consciousness — the internal monologue raw, profane, honest in a way the narrator of our story will NOT be, which makes the contrast instructive.

> All Welsh knew was that he was scared shitless, and at the same time was afflicted with a choking gorge of anger that any social coercion existed in the world which could force him to be here.

This is the truth our narrator cannot afford to tell. Welsh's honesty is what our narrator's polish is designed to conceal.

### Sample 5: Erich Maria Remarque — *All Quiet on the Western Front* (Comradeship)

**Source**: Work Y
**Origin**: *All Quiet on the Western Front*, Paul Baumer on the voices of comrades
**Why representative**: The emotional core the story must reach — the love between soldiers as the one truth that survives everything, including the narrator's unreliability.

> These quiet words, these footsteps in the trench behind me recall me at a bound from the terrible loneliness and fear of death by which I had been almost destroyed. They are more to me than life, these voices, they are more than motherliness and more than fear; they are the strongest, most comforting thing there is anywhere: they are the voices of my comrades.

This passage is the key to the unreliable narrator constraint. As Smith argued in the author meeting: "You don't lie about what destroyed you. You lie about everything around it." The love for comrades is the one thing the narrator gets right — because it is the thing that destroyed him.

## Wikipedia Concepts

### Concept Mining (Searched Topics)

1. **Leadwood tree (Combretum imberbe)** — A southern African tree so dense its heartwood sinks in water and is termite-resistant. Even after death, the tree remains standing for hundreds of years. Radiocarbon dating shows they can live over 1,000 years. The bark is pale to dark grey, deeply fissured lengthwise.

2. **Mopane worm (Gonimbrasia belina)** — The large edible caterpillar of the emperor moth, harvested from mopane trees during the rainy season. To prepare them, the innards are squeezed out between thumb and forefinger, then they are sun-dried or smoked. Communities gather to harvest them as both cultural ritual and economic necessity.

3. **Southern yellow-billed hornbill (Tockus leucomelas)** — A territorial bird of dry thornveld and broadleaf woodland, 48-60 cm long with a massive curved yellow beak. Forages cooperatively with dwarf mongoose — catching prey the mongoose disturbs while alerting them to predators. Has a piercing cry and wide range of sounds: whistling, grunting, caqueting.

4. **Fire Force operations** — Rhodesian vertical envelopment tactic using Alouette III helicopters and DC-3 Dakotas. A first wave of 32 soldiers: four-man "sticks" each with a stick leader (carrying an A63 radio and FN FAL), a machine gunner (FN MAG, 400 rounds), and two riflemen. Commander orbited at 800 feet in the K-Car armed with a 20mm cannon. Typical contact involved 6-12 insurgents; 80:1 kill ratio. Men rotated between heliborne insertion, paratrooper, landtail, and off-duty during six-week bush trips.

5. **Phantom limb phenomenon** — 80-100% of amputees experience sensations in the missing limb. First documented by Ambroise Pare in the 16th century, named by Silas Weir Mitchell in 1872. People try to pick things up, feel itches, gesture.

6. **Kariba Dam** — Built 1955-1959 on the Zambezi, displacing 57,000 Tonga people. 128 metres tall, forming a lake extending 280 kilometres. Over 85 workers died during construction.

### Selected Concepts (2)

**1. Leadwood tree (Combretum imberbe)**
**Integration**: A massive dead leadwood stands at the edge of the operational area — a tree that has been dead for centuries but refuses to fall. The narrator describes it in his composed, Stevenson-inflected prose as a landmark, a rallying point. But the leadwood becomes a quiet metaphor the narrator never acknowledges: he, too, is something dead that remains standing. The tree is described early — pale grey bark, deeply fissured — and returns at the story's end. The narrator passes it on the way out and notes it is "unchanged," which is both literally true and the lie he needs to believe about himself.

**2. Mopane worm harvesting**
**Integration**: During a patrol's rest in mopane woodland, one of the African soldiers (a Selous Scout tracker or an RAR soldier attached to the unit) shows the narrator how to harvest and prepare mopane worms — squeezing the innards between thumb and forefinger, the particular pressure required. This becomes a moment of genuine human contact across the racial divide of the Rhodesian military, a moment of body-knowledge (Smith's territory) that the narrator renders with excessive literary care (Stevenson's territory). The specificity of the act — the green frass, the careful pressure, the texture of the dried worm — grounds the story in a detail so particular it must be real. It is also the kind of moment that a man telling a story at a dinner party would never include, which is how the reader begins to suspect that this narrator is performing something other than casual remembrance.

## Story Design

### 1. Title

**Farms and Drinkers**

(Drawn from the author meeting — Smith's observation: "I knew boys who went. Some of them came back and bought farms. Some of them came back and drank. Some of them came back and did both." The title is a compound form, concrete and enigmatic, suggesting two outcomes without explaining either.)

### 2. Premise

A former Rhodesian Light Infantry soldier, now a tobacco farmer in the Lowveld twenty years after the Bush War ended, tells the story of a six-week bush trip in 1977 as though he were recounting an adventure — moral, coherent, populated by men who behaved with clarity and purpose. He tells it the way a man tells a story at a dinner table: with economy and shape. But the shape is wrong. The story is too clean, the deaths too meaningful, and the narrator's role in each critical moment too precisely calibrated between courage and regret. Underneath the polished telling, another story presses upward — the one where he was not brave, where his friend did not die heroically, where the cause was already ash.

### 3. Protagonist

**Rory Gilchrist**, 42 at the time of telling (1997), 22 during the events described (1977). A second-generation Rhodesian whose father farmed tobacco outside Karoi and who volunteered for the RLI at eighteen with the conviction that he was defending his country. He is now a farmer on land adjacent to his father's former holding (which he could not afford to keep intact after independence). He has a wife, two children, and a controlled drinking habit that he does not recognize as controlled because the control is the problem — if he lost control, something might surface.

**What he WANTS**: To tell the story of his bush war service as evidence that what happened had meaning — that the men he served with were good, that the cause was defensible, that the cost was the kind of cost that buys something.

**What he NEEDS**: To admit that the cause was lost before it started, that his friend Pieter's death was not heroic but stupid and preventable, and that his own survival was not earned but accidental — that he froze at the wrong moment and lived because someone else moved.

**His flaw**: Rory believes that the quality of the telling determines the quality of the truth. If he can tell it well enough — with enough precise detail, enough moral nuance, enough of the right kind of regret — then the version he tells becomes the version that happened. His prose (rendered through Stevenson's influence) is his primary instrument of self-deception. He is a man whose eloquence is his wound.

### 4. Setting

**Eastern Rhodesia, 1977 — the operational area along the Mozambique border.** Specifically, the mopane woodland and granite kopje terrain between Mount Darwin and the Mavhuradonha mountains. The RLI is conducting Fire Force operations from a forward airfield (FAF), rotating through six-week bush trips.

**Key researched details for the writer**:
- The Alouette III G-Cars carry four-man sticks: stick leader with A63 VHF radio and FN FAL, machine gunner with FN MAG (400 rounds), two riflemen with FALs and 100 rounds each
- The K-Car orbits at 800 feet with the Fire Force Commander and a 20mm MG 151 cannon
- The DC-3 Dakota ("Dak") carries paratroops — five stops, 32 men total
- Bush trips last six weeks, with ten days rest afterward
- Men rotate through heliborne insertion, paratrooper, landtail, and off-duty
- Mopane woodland: flat terrain, sparse undergrowth, deeply fissured drainage channels, exposed tree roots on eroded soil. Smells of turpentine. Canopy 10-15m.
- The FN FAL (called "SLR" by Rhodesian forces) — heavy emphasis on individual marksmanship; trained to fire low and graduate upward
- Contact typically 6-12 insurgents; kill ratio 80:1 (the obscenity of this ratio should be felt but never stated by the narrator)
- The red soil, the milky-tea colour of rivers at low water, the violent high-altitude sunlight

### 5. Structure (5 Beats)

**Beat 1: The Farm, 1997 — The Narrator Begins (approx. 600 words)**
Rory is on his veranda at dusk, gin and tonic in hand, watching the Lowveld light turn golden. He begins to tell a story — addressing an implied listener (perhaps a journalist, perhaps a friend, perhaps the reader directly; this is never resolved). The telling is polished, retrospective, Stevensonian. He describes his younger self arriving at the FAF near Mount Darwin for a six-week rotation, 1977. He introduces the landscape with Smith-like precision — the mopane, the red earth, the heat haze. He introduces his stick: Pieter van Rensburg (the machine gunner, a farmer's son from Chinhoyi, big and quiet), Toombs (a rifleman, English-speaking, from Bulawayo, who read paperback westerns), and Ndlovu (an African tracker seconded to their stick, who spoke three languages and said very little in any of them). The narrator's tone is warm, confident, shaped. This is a man who has told this story before.

**Beat 2: The Bush Trip — Patrols and Waiting (approx. 1,000 words)**
The daily rhythm of the bush trip: rotations between standby and deployment, the boredom punctuated by terror. A Fire Force contact early in the rotation that goes textbook — the Dak drops, the G-Cars sweep, the sweep line moves through the bush. Rory describes it with the kinetic precision of Smith: the downdraft of the Alouette, the red dust, the noise, the way the bush swallows sound. This contact is clean — no casualties on the Rhodesian side. Rory uses it to establish his credibility as a narrator: he was there, he saw, he can describe.

The mopane worm scene occurs here: during a rest in mopane woodland, Ndlovu shows Rory and Pieter how to harvest and prepare the worms. Pieter is disgusted. Rory tries one. The moment is rendered with too much care, too much literary precision — the reader should sense that this moment matters more to the narrator than he is admitting. Ndlovu's hands, the green frass, the specific pressure between thumb and forefinger. This is the point where Stevenson's and Smith's styles overlap: precise physical observation wrapped in retrospective composition.

The dead leadwood tree is introduced here — enormous, grey, standing at the edge of a dried watercourse. The narrator notes it as a landmark but describes it with a reverence that seems disproportionate.

**Beat 3: The Contact That Matters (approx. 1,200 words)**
The Fire Force scramble that changes everything. The K-Car commander calls in a large contact — bigger than expected, 15-20 insurgents in a drainage channel. Rory's stick is heliborne. The insertion goes wrong: the G-Car puts them down 200 metres from where they should be, in thick jesse bush with poor sight lines. The narrator describes the next minutes with enormous precision — the kind of precision that serves as evidence of presence, of truthfulness. But the precision has holes.

What the narrator tells us: Pieter moved forward. Rory covered him. They advanced under fire to a position where Pieter could lay down suppressive fire with the MAG. Pieter was hit. Rory pulled him back. Pieter died in his arms, saying something Rory could not hear over the gunfire.

What the narrator does NOT tell us — what the reader begins to sense: Rory froze. He did not move when Pieter moved. Pieter went forward alone because Rory did not go with him. The covering fire came late. The insurgent who shot Pieter had time to shoot because no one was suppressing from Rory's position. And the thing Pieter said — which the narrator claims he could not hear — was audible. Rory heard it. He has replaced it with silence.

The ensemble cycling occurs here, filtered through the narrator's limited perspective (Jones's influence): Toombs somewhere to the right, his voice on the radio clipped and mechanical; the K-Car overhead, the 20mm cannon tearing up the bush; Ndlovu moving without sound, appearing and disappearing. Each man registered through the narrator's consciousness — but the narrator is inside his own terror and cannot see them whole.

**Beat 4: The Aftermath (approx. 800 words)**
After the contact. The casevac. Pieter's body in the Alouette, the MAG still slung across him because no one remembered to take it. The return to the FAF. The debrief. The narrator describes the days that follow with the same composed precision, but something has changed in the prose — not a collapse but a tightening. The sentences are shorter. The descriptions are less generous. The landscape, which was previously rendered with wonder, becomes merely accurate.

Toombs gets drunk and says something oblique about what happened. The narrator reports the words but deflects their meaning. Ndlovu says nothing — but his silence is described with too much attention, as though the narrator is monitoring it for accusation.

The narrator tells us he grieved. He tells us the grief was "the kind that has no performance in it." This sentence is the most suspicious in the story — it is precisely the kind of sentence a man performing grief would construct.

**Beat 5: The Return — or the Impossibility of It (approx. 700 words)**
The narrator brings us back to the farm, 1997. The story is told. He describes the evening light, the gin, the sound of his children inside the house. He mentions, as though in passing, that he has not seen Toombs in twelve years. That Ndlovu is dead — "of causes I did not ask about" — a sentence that conceals the entire history of Zimbabwe's post-independence violence in a dependent clause.

He says he bought the farm. He says he was lucky. He mentions the leadwood tree, which he drove past recently — it is still standing, "unchanged," he says, "which I find remarkable, that a dead thing should remain so perfectly itself."

The story ends on the veranda. Not a resolution but a silence. The narrator has told his version. He believes it. The reader is left with the weight of what was not said — not as a puzzle to solve but as an ache that will not resolve. Remarque's impossibility of return, but displaced: Rory did return, physically. He bought the farm. He is one of the ones who came back and bought farms. But the story he tells — polished, moral, elegantly crafted — is the price of staying upright.

The final image: the narrator lifts his glass, and the reader notices — or should notice — that this is the second gin mentioned in the telling. He is also one of the ones who came back and drank.

### 6. Key Scenes (Must Be in the Story)

1. **The mopane worm scene**: Ndlovu teaching Rory and Pieter to harvest mopane worms. Physical, specific, grounded. The first moment where the narrator's literary precision reveals something he is not intending to reveal — that this memory of Ndlovu's hands matters to him more than the battles.

2. **The clean contact**: An early Fire Force operation described with Smith's kinetic prose — helicopters, dust, gunfire, sweep lines. Establishes the narrator's reliability before undoing it. The reader should believe him here.

3. **The contact where Pieter dies**: The structural center. Rendered in Stevenson's composed retrospective voice, but the composition is doing work — smoothing, shaping, omitting. The ensemble cycling (Jones) shows us Toombs, Ndlovu, the K-Car, all through Rory's fractured attention.

4. **Toombs saying the oblique thing**: After the contact, drunk at the FAF, Toombs says something that almost names what actually happened. The narrator quotes him but redirects the meaning. This is the closest the text comes to breaking through the unreliability — and the narrator's deflection is the most telling gesture in the story.

5. **The leadwood tree, revisited**: Mentioned early, returned to at the end. A dead thing that remains standing for centuries. The narrator finds it "remarkable" without understanding why it compels him.

### 7. Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning**: The reader feels warmth, even nostalgia. The narrator's voice is confident, generous, slightly elegiac. The African landscape is beautiful. The men are vivid and likeable. This feels like a war story told by a man who has made peace with it.

**Middle**: Unease creeps in. The precision starts to feel wrong — not dishonest exactly, but too careful. The mopane worm scene is too tender. The clean contact is too clean. The landscape descriptions are too controlled. The reader begins to sense that this fluency is a construction.

**Crisis**: During the contact where Pieter dies, the reader feels the gap between what the narrator says happened and what the story's architecture implies. The emotion is not dramatic realization but a slow, sickening awareness that the most eloquent account in the room is the least trustworthy. The narrator's grief is real. His version of why he grieves is not.

**End**: The reader is left not with revelation but with the full weight of the narrator's need to believe his own story. Not anger at being deceived — sympathy for the necessity of the deception. The feeling is something like watching a man carry a heavy object across a long distance and understanding that if he puts it down, even for a moment, he will not be able to pick it up again. He is both the farmer and the drinker. He is both the man who came back and the man who cannot return.

### 8. Risk Card Integration: Unreliable Narrator

The unreliability is architecturally central, not a twist or reveal:

- **Surface level**: Rory's Stevensonian prose polish is itself the instrument of unreliability. The elegance is the lie. A man who witnessed what he claims to have witnessed would not describe it with this much literary composure — unless the composure is the point, the thing that holds him together.

- **Structural level**: The story never breaks into "the real version." There is no confession scene, no moment where the narrator admits what actually happened. Instead, the architecture of the story creates pressure — the too-clean description, the elided moments, Toombs's deflected comment, the suspicious sentence about grief — and the reader assembles the counter-narrative from the gaps. As Stevenson said in the meeting: "The self-deception must be total, or it is not self-deception — it is merely storytelling."

- **Thematic level**: The unreliable narrator embodies Remarque's impossibility of return. Rory cannot tell the true story because the true story would end him. His version is not cynical or performative — it is necessary. "The lie was structural. It held them together," as Smith said. The narrative form itself becomes a survival mechanism, and the reader's growing awareness of this is the story's deepest emotional register.

- **What the narrator gets right**: The love. As established in the author meeting, the love between soldiers is the one thing that survives intact. When Rory describes Pieter, when he describes Ndlovu's hands on the mopane worms, when he describes Toombs reading paperback westerns by torchlight — this is true. The reader should feel the truth of the love as vividly as they feel the falseness of the heroism.

### 9. Formula Integration

**Robert Louis Stevenson (Author A)**: Most visible in the narrator's voice throughout — the retrospective first-person composition, the moral architecture that seems to organize events into legible meaning, the compressed character portraits ("Pieter was a big man who moved quietly, which is the most disconcerting combination"). But concentrate Stevenson most intensely in the contact scene (Beat 3), where the composed prose is doing the most work to conceal. The gap between Stevenson's elegant sentences and the raw experience they are smoothing over IS the story.

**Wilbur Smith (Author B)**: Most visible in the landscape passages and the Fire Force contact scenes. The African bush as active force — the mopane, the red earth, the heat haze, the violence of the light. The clean Fire Force operation in Beat 2 should read like Smith at his most kinetic: helicopter downdraft, red dust, the bush swallowing sound, bodies in motion. Also present in the mopane worm scene (Smith's insistence on body-knowledge, on the physical as the truest form of narrative) and in the fundamental worldview: "a man running from a lion is not a metaphor for anything."

**The Thin Red Line (Work X)**: Most visible in Beat 3 — the ensemble cycling during the contact. Rory's fractured awareness of Toombs, Ndlovu, the K-Car, other sticks in the sweep line, all registered through his consciousness but incomplete, partial. Each man perceived as a face, a voice on the radio, a movement at the edge of vision. Jones's technique of giving each soldier a specific consciousness is filtered through the unreliable narrator: we get Rory's version of each man's interiority, which may be as fabricated as his own.

**All Quiet on the Western Front (Work Y)**: Most visible in the thematic architecture — the destruction of youth, the impossibility of return, camaraderie as the only truth. But also present in the story's ending: Rory on the veranda, twenty years later, alive and farming and drinking, unable to return to the version of himself that existed before 1977. Remarque's Paul Baumer goes home and sits in his room and cannot speak. Rory goes home and speaks too well — his fluency is the mirror image of Paul's silence, and equally diagnostic.

### 10. Key Images and Researched Details for the Writer

- **Leadwood tree (Combretum imberbe)**: Pale grey bark, deeply fissured lengthwise. Heartwood so dense it sinks in water. Dead trees remain standing for centuries. Radiocarbon-dated at 1,070 years. Use early and late — the tree that is dead but will not fall.
- **Mopane worms (Gonimbrasia belina)**: Harvested in rainy season from mopane trees. Innards squeezed out between thumb and forefinger. Green frass. Sun-dried or smoked. The specific pressure required — too much and you burst it, too little and the gut contents remain.
- **Alouette III G-Car**: French-built, could carry only four men. The downdraft in red soil creates a visible dust cloud. The stick leader sits beside the pilot.
- **FN FAL / SLR**: The standard Rhodesian infantry rifle. 7.62mm, heavy, accurate. Rhodesian troops trained to fire low and graduate upward. 100 rounds carried per rifleman.
- **FN MAG**: The machine gun. 400 rounds. Heavy. Pieter carries this.
- **K-Car**: The command helicopter armed with 20mm MG 151 cannon. Orbits at 800 feet. The Fire Force commander directs ground operations from here. The sound of the 20mm from the ground is specific and unmistakable.
- **Six-week bush trip**: The rotation pattern. Men rotate between heliborne, paratrooper, landtail, off-duty. Ten days rest afterward.
- **Mopane woodland**: Flat, sparse undergrowth, deeply fissured drainage channels, erosion gullies, exposed roots. Two-storeyed canopy — mature trees 10-15m, understorey of saplings. Smells of turpentine.
- **Yellow-billed hornbill**: Use for atmosphere — the piercing cry, the territorial call at dawn. A sound that becomes associated with the operational area.
- **The 80:1 kill ratio**: Never state this explicitly. Let the narrator's clean contact (Beat 2) imply the asymmetry. The reader should feel the disproportion without being told the number.
- **Red soil of the eastern operational area**: The dust that coats everything — uniforms, weapons, skin.
- **Bush trips and the FAF**: Forward airfield, tents, the particular institutional squalor of a temporary military installation.

### 11. Tone and Voice Notes for the Writer

The narrator's voice is the story's primary instrument. It should feel like the best version of a dinner-table war story: composed, specific, generous with detail, capable of wit and tenderness. The Stevenson influence gives it a literary quality that should feel natural — Rory is an educated man, a reader, someone who thinks in sentences. But the literary quality is also the warning sign. Real war stories told by men who have processed their experience have a different texture — more fragmentary, less shaped, with the kind of gaps Smith described in the meeting: "The drinkers — their stories had holes in them. Gaps. Things they circled around without landing on."

Rory's story has no gaps. It has no holes. It is perfectly shaped. That perfection is the tell.

The writer should NOT signal the unreliability through contradiction or error. The narrator does not slip up. The unreliability emerges from the architecture: from the story's suspicious completeness, from the literary quality of the grief, from the moments where Toombs or Ndlovu almost say something the narrator redirects, from the leadwood tree that carries more weight than a landmark should.

The prose should oscillate between Stevenson's composed retrospection and Smith's kinetic African landscape, with the oscillation itself meaningful — the narrator retreats into Stevensonian composure precisely when the material is most dangerous, and relaxes into Smithian physicality when the material is safe (landscape, the clean contact, the mopane worms).
