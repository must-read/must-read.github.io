## Writing Samples

### Source 1: Roxane Gay — Author A (Style)

**From *Hunger: A Memoir of (My) Body* (2017)**

Gay's prose is characterized by short, declarative sentences that accumulate factual weight rather than emotional ornamentation. She uses the first-person "I" at the start of many sentences in succession — not as carelessness but as a formal strategy, each "I" landing like a blow. Her voice is confessional without being performative: she states what happened, states what she felt, and refuses to connect the two with a bridge of wisdom or growth. The structure of *Hunger* is 88 short chapters that read as vignettes — each a contained observation, rarely more than a few pages, with the precision of someone who has measured every space she has ever entered.

Representative passages:

> "This is a book about my body, about my hunger, and ultimately, this is a book about disappearing and being lost and wanting so very much, wanting to be seen and understood. This is a book about learning, however slowly, to allow myself to be seen and understood."

> "I ate and ate and ate in the hopes that if I made myself big, my body would be safe. I buried the girl I was because she ran into all kinds of trouble. I tried to erase every memory of her, but she is still there, somewhere. . . . I was trapped in my body, one that I barely recognized or understood, but at least I was safe."

> "Lovers were often rough with me as if that was the only way they could understand touching a body as fat as mine."

> "I accepted this because I did not deserve kindness or a gentle touch."

> "My throat constricts. The clothes shrink. Sleeves become tourniquets. Slacks become shackles."

> "Fierce vanity smolders in the cave of my chest."

> "I want to look good. I want to feel good. I want to be beautiful in this body I am in."

> "I do not know why I turned to food. Or I do."

**Voice DNA to capture**: Declarative precision. Sentences that land flat, one after another, without connective tissue. The "I" as both weapon and wound. Facts stated with the tone of someone reporting the weather — except the weather is her body. Repetition used structurally: phrases that return slightly altered. The refusal of metaphor except when metaphor arrives unbidden ("sleeves become tourniquets"). Short paragraphs, sometimes a single sentence. A total absence of self-pity that is more devastating than self-pity could ever be.

### Source 2: Hunter S. Thompson — Author B (Style)

**From *Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas* (1971)**

Thompson's prose accelerates. His sentences begin reasonably and then leave the road, piling clause on clause until the grammar itself is a controlled skid. He catalogs objects and substances with the obsessiveness of an archivist who has taken everything in the archive. He shifts between deadpan reportage and hallucinatory excess without transition — the reader is simply there, suddenly, in a different register. His paragraphs can run for a page.

Representative passages:

> "We were somewhere around Barstow, on the edge of the desert, when the drugs began to take hold."

> "We had two bags of grass, seventy-five pellets of mescaline, five sheets of high powered blotter acid, a salt shaker half full of cocaine, and a whole galaxy of multi-colored uppers, downers, screamers, laughers... and also a quart of tequila, a quart of rum, a case of Budweiser, a pint of raw ether and two dozen amyls. Not that we needed all that for the trip, but once you get locked into a serious drug collection, the tendency is to push it as far as you can."

> "No sympathy for the devil; keep that in mind. Buy the ticket, take the ride...and if it occasionally gets a little heavier than what you had in mind, well...maybe chalk it up to forced consciousness expansion: Tune in, freak out, get beaten."

> "Strange memories on this nervous night in Las Vegas. Five years later? Six? It seems like a lifetime, or at least a Main Era—the kind of peak that never comes again. San Francisco in the middle sixties was a very special time and place to be a part of. Maybe it meant something. Maybe not, in the long run... but no explanation, no mix of words or music or memories can touch that sense of knowing that you were there and alive in that corner of time and the world."

> "In a closed society where everybody's guilty, the only crime is getting caught. In a world of thieves, the only final sin is stupidity."

The wave speech continues with the famous high-water-mark image: standing on a hill in Las Vegas, looking west, and "with the right kind of eyes you can almost see the high-water mark — that place where the wave finally broke and rolled back."

**Voice DNA to capture**: The catalog as acceleration — lists that gain momentum and never stop where they should. Sentences that overshoot their destination. Hyperbole deployed not for comedy but for truth-telling (the exaggeration is the accurate part). Sudden shifts from frenzy to elegy. The first person as unreliable witness who is somehow more reliable than any "objective" account. A sense that the prose itself is slightly out of control and that this is the point. The American landscape as psychic hallucination. Short, punchy declaratives ("We can't stop here, this is bat country!") amid long, sprawling accumulations.

### Source 3: *Hunger* — Work X (Structure)

*Hunger* is structured as 88 vignettes — short chapters, many only a page or two, that refuse to build toward climax. There is no arc. There is accumulation. Gay describes the mundane logistics of living in a body the world was not designed for: the airplane seat, the doctor's office, the restaurant booth, the waiting room with bolted-down armrests. The structure is additive, not linear — each chapter adds a layer of physical experience without promising that the layers will form a shape. The book begins with trauma and circles back to it, but it does not resolve. The ending is not triumph. The "or I do" construction — "I don't know how things got so out of control, or I do" — recurs as a structural motif: the simultaneous knowing and not-knowing.

**Structural DNA to capture**: Accumulation over revelation. Short sections that could be read in any order but gain weight through sequence. The refusal of arc. No redemption, no epiphany, no "and then I understood." The mundane as devastating: measurements, chair widths, armrest dimensions. A memoir that is also an inventory.

---

### Source 4: *Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas* — Work Y (Themes)

Thompson's book documents a road trip as psychic collapse. The "savage journey to the heart of the American Dream" finds at the heart of America nothing but a casino, a drug haze, and the wreckage of the 1960s. The American landscape is rendered as hallucination — Las Vegas as the place where the national performance of itself reaches its purest, most grotesque form. The line between observer and spectacle dissolves: Thompson cannot document the excess without becoming it. The book's engine is forward motion — the car, the road, the velocity — that arrives nowhere. Every promise America makes is visible in Las Vegas and every promise is a lie.

**Thematic DNA to capture**: The American landscape as a performance space that reveals its own emptiness. Forward motion that arrives nowhere. The observer consumed by the spectacle. Documentation as participation. The American Dream as architecture — built, visible, occupying space, and hollow. The national hallucination of who bodies are and what they deserve.

## Wikipedia Concepts

### 20 Articles Surveyed

1. **Henry Dreyfuss** — Industrial designer who created "Joe and Josephine," the average human figures used to design everything from telephone handsets to airplane cockpits. Joe: 5'11", 162 lbs. Josephine: 5'5", 135 lbs. Published in *The Measure of Man* (1960).
2. **Desire path** — Unplanned trail formed by pedestrian traffic where designed paths fail to serve actual movement. As few as 15 passages create a visible trail.
3. **Procrustes** — Greek myth: a bandit who forced travelers to fit his iron bed by stretching the short and amputating the tall. Nobody ever fit.
4. **Anthropometry** — The systematic measurement of human body dimensions; developed in the 19th century; used in ergonomics, clothing, and architecture.
5. **Plimsoll line** — The mark on a ship's hull indicating maximum safe load. Named for Samuel Plimsoll, who campaigned against overloaded "coffin ships" that killed sailors.
6. **Cattle chute** — A narrow corridor for livestock that only allows animals to move forward. Too narrow to turn around. Connected to a squeeze mechanism.
7. **Turnstile** — One-person gates originally used to separate humans from livestock. Adopted by transit systems; the IRT installed them because its own employees were cheating it.
8. **Vitruvian Man** — Leonardo's drawing of the ideal human proportions inscribed in a circle and square. The measurements are Leonardo's own, not Vitruvius's.
9. **Body schema** — The brain's internal model of the body's position; modular, adaptable, updated with movement.
10. **Proprioception** — The sense of one's own body position; transmitted to the central nervous system and integrated with vision and balance.
11. **Phantom limb** — Amputees feeling a limb that is no longer there, taking up space. 90–98% of amputees report this.
12. **Load-bearing capacity** — Maximum weight a structure can carry without failure. Roman Colosseum walls. Limited to 2–3 stories typically.
13. **Spite house** — A building constructed specifically to annoy neighbors by blocking light or access.
14. **Coffin ship** — Overloaded or unseaworthy vessels, especially during the Irish Famine. The term that prompted the Plimsoll line.
15. **Featherweight pressure gate** — The 1921 IRT turnstile design by Frank Hedley, named with bitter irony.
16. **Cosmic latte** — The average color of the universe: a beige-ish white. Scientists named it after surveying 200,000 galaxies.
17. **Dancing mania** — Medieval phenomenon of groups dancing erratically for days without rest. No agreed-upon cause.
18. **Broadway (New York)** — May follow a trail developed by the Wecquaesgeek people, predating colonization. A desire path that became America's most famous street.
19. **Squeeze crush** — The veterinary device that immobilizes an animal by pressing from the sides. Manual or hydraulic.
20. **Gavle goat** — A giant straw goat erected annually in Sweden since 1966, famous for being burned down by arson almost every year.

### 2 Selected Concepts

**1. Henry Dreyfuss's "Joe and Josephine" (1960)**
*Integration*: The narrator will discover — or already know — that every airplane seat, every turnstile, every hospital gown was designed for two fictional people: Joe (5'11", 162 lbs) and Josephine (5'5", 135 lbs), created by industrial designer Henry Dreyfuss for his 1960 reference book *The Measure of Man*. These ghosts of average Americans haunt every space the narrator enters. The essay will invoke them by name — not as research dump but as a running presence, the way Gay invokes the armrest. Joe and Josephine are the people the world was built for. The narrator is not Joe. The narrator is not Josephine. The narrator has never been either of them, and the world has never let her forget it. This provides the essay a concrete historical anchor: the spaces are not accidentally hostile. Someone drew blueprints. Someone measured. Someone decided who counted.

**2. Desire paths**
*Integration*: The narrator will describe the routes she actually takes through spaces — the wide berths, the avoidances, the detours that have become automatic. These are desire paths: trails worn by repetition where the designed paths do not serve. But unlike the pedestrian desire path (which eventually gets paved, legitimized), the fat person's desire path stays unofficial. Nobody paves it. Nobody studies the erosion pattern. The concept appears in a single passage — the narrator noticing a worn strip of grass on a hospital campus, a shortcut between the parking lot and the entrance made by people who didn't want to walk through the too-narrow revolving door — and recognizing it as her own navigation strategy made visible. Note: the platform already has a title "Desire Path to a Counting Room," so this concept will NOT appear in the title, only in the body.

## 1. Premise / Engine

A woman in a large body moves through five American spaces in a single week — airport, amusement park, hospital, subway, diner — and each space asks the same question of her body: do you fit? The essay is the record of that question asked five different ways, and of the rage and precision that coexist in the answer. The engine is not story; it is accumulation. Each space adds weight until the essay itself is heavy with measurement, and the measurements never resolve into meaning, and the meaning never resolves into acceptance, and the absence of acceptance is not a failure of the narrator but a failure of the country.

The central tension: the narrator knows, politically and intellectually, that her body is not the problem — the architecture is the problem, the design standards are the problem, the legacy of Henry Dreyfuss's Joe and Josephine is the problem. But knowing does not stop the flinch. The gap between knowing and flinching is the essay's territory.

---

## 2. Narrator-Protagonist

- **Who**: Unnamed woman, mid-thirties, weighs over 300 pounds (she knows the number but will not perform it for the reader on demand — it appears once, without emphasis, in the middle of a sentence about something else). A writer. Lives alone. Travels frequently for work. Has been this size since her twenties.
- **What she WANTS**: To get through the week. To make the connecting flight, to ride the roller coaster with her niece, to get the MRI, to take the subway downtown, to eat dinner at a restaurant without calculating whether she will fit in the booth. To move through the country the way Joe and Josephine move: without arithmetic.
- **What she NEEDS**: To stop translating rage into precision — to let the rage exist on the page without disciplining it into measurement and fact.
- **Her flaw / blind spot**: She believes that precision is control and that control is survival. It is — but it is also a cage. Her hypervigilance (surveilling the space before the space surveils her) has become so automatic that she no longer recognizes it as a cost. She thinks she is being practical. She is being exhausted.

## 3. Structure (Essay Architecture)

This is NOT a five-act story. It is a memoir essay with five sections organized by space, not by chronology. The sections are not equal in length. The essay does not alternate neatly between precision and rage — per Gay's instruction in the meeting, the rage interrupts the precision without warning.

### Opening Image / Hook
The essay opens mid-calculation. The narrator is on an airplane, calculating: the width of the armrest (18 inches in coach, she knows this the way a carpenter knows the width of a two-by-four), the exact angle at which her body presses against it, the duration of the flight (3 hours, 47 minutes), the number of times the person beside her will shift away. She is doing this math before the plane has left the gate. She has been doing this math for fifteen years. The sentence structure is Gay-tight: short, declarative, fact after fact, no connective tissue. Then — three paragraphs in — the prose breaks open. A Thompson-velocity passage: a sentence that starts with the armrest and accelerates through every armrest she has ever pressed against, every seat belt extender she has ever asked for in a whisper, every flight attendant's eyes, every middle seat, every country mile of American airspace engineered for a body she does not have, and the sentence does not stop when it should, it keeps going the way her body keeps going, because what choice does it have.

### Section 1: Airport (Gay-dominant)
Precision mode. The logistics of air travel in a large body. The seatbelt extender. The tray table that won't fold down. The armrest as weapon (per the meeting). The pre-boarding survey of the gate area: which seats are open, which have arms, which allow her to sit without touching a stranger. Henry Dreyfuss appears here: she learned about Joe and Josephine in college and has never been able to unknow it. Joe is 5'11" and 162 pounds. The airplane seat was designed for Joe. She is not Joe. This section is measured, controlled, devastating in its flatness. It ends with a single detail: the bruise on her hip from the armrest, already forming, already purple.

### Section 2: Amusement Park (the essay's gravitational center)
The narrator takes her eleven-year-old niece to an amusement park. She knows what will happen. She has always known. There is a test seat outside the roller coaster — a single seat, in public, where you sit down and see if the harness closes over your body. A rehearsal for humiliation. She sits. The harness does not close. Her niece looks at her and says nothing. She stands up. They walk to the next ride. She buys her niece a funnel cake. This is the passage that the meeting identified as the essay's center of gravity — but the narrator refuses to let it be the center. She treats it as one more measurement, one more space that told her she was wrong. She does not linger. She does not explain. The reader is left in the seat.

But here — at the amusement park — the Thompson-register erupts without warning. A passage about the phrase "for your safety." For your safety, the harness must close. For your safety, there is a weight limit. For your safety, this country built a fun-machine and excluded your body from the fun and called the exclusion a precaution. For your safety. The passage accelerates, catalogs the "for your safety" language of airports, hospitals, amusement parks, the whole American apparatus of concern that is not concern at all but architecture, and the prose goes feral for a page, and then it stops. And she is standing outside the roller coaster. And her niece is eating a funnel cake. And the silence between them is the loudest thing in the park.

### Section 3: Hospital (braided — precision with undertow)
An MRI. The machine has a weight limit (the Plimsoll line of the body — though she does not use that phrase). The technician is kind, which is worse than cruelty because kindness requires her to perform gratitude. The open MRI is in a different building, a twenty-minute drive. She knows about the open MRI because she has done this before. She drives to the other building. She notices a desire path across the hospital lawn — a strip of worn grass between the parking lot and the entrance, made by people who chose not to navigate the too-narrow revolving door. She walks it. She thinks about the fifteen passages it takes to make a visible trail and how many invisible trails her own body has worn through American space.

This section introduces the body schema concept obliquely: the narrator describes the phantom geography of spaces she has learned — the exact clearance of every doorway she regularly passes through, the weight limit of her desk chair at work (250 pounds, she checked), the distance between the bathroom stall wall and the toilet (never enough). Her body's proprioception includes the architecture. She carries the measurements of rooms she will never enter again.

### Section 4: Subway (Thompson-dominant)
Short, feral, fast. The turnstile. The narrator approaches a New York subway turnstile and it is, she thinks, essentially a medieval device — a body-sorting mechanism that would not be out of place in a stockyard, a cattle chute made of chrome and branded with the MTA logo, and the city installed it originally because its own employees were stealing fares, which means the turnstile is not about safety or efficiency but about trust, or the absence of trust, and the body that cannot pass through it is not failing a physical test but being judged by an apparatus that was designed to judge, to sort, to say: you may pass, you may not, your hips are wider than the slot we have allotted, and the slot is the law. This section is one long paragraph, a single sustained Thompson-acceleration that does not stop for breath, does not break for a new paragraph, does not offer the reader a place to rest. It ends with her getting through the turnstile — she always gets through — and the metal bar pressing into her abdomen, and the click of the mechanism, and the sound of it resetting behind her, already ready for the next body, already sorting.

### Section 5: Diner (convergence — stillness that contains velocity)
The essay ends in a diner booth. The booth is too small. She knew it would be. She is eating alone. She orders coffee and a cheeseburger (Thompson's move, though she doesn't know Thompson). She sits in the booth and her hip presses against the wall and her other hip is in the aisle and she takes up more than her half of the booth, which is not a half but whatever fraction the architecture decided she deserved.

Here the two voices merge: Gay's precision (the measurements of the booth, the temperature of the coffee, the exact quality of fluorescent light) and Thompson's scope (the country that built this booth, the America that designs its spaces like declarations of who belongs). She thinks about Joe and Josephine one last time — the ghosts who fit everywhere, who were designed to fit, whose fictional comfort determined the real discomfort of millions. She does not forgive them. She does not forgive the architect. She does not forgive the country.

The essay does NOT end with acceptance. It does not end with rage. It ends with the narrator still sitting in the booth, still eating, still here. The armrest is still there. The booth is still too small. Nothing has changed. The last image is the coffee, still hot, and the fluorescent tube above her buzzing with the particular frequency of a tube that should have been replaced months ago.

## 4. Key Scenes / Passages

1. **The airplane armrest calculation** (opening): Establishes the Gay-register precision, introduces the mathematical hypervigilance, and then breaks into Thompson-velocity. The first shift between modes. Sets the formal rules for the essay.

2. **The test seat at the amusement park**: The essay's emotional core, delivered with deliberate understatement. The harness does not close. The niece says nothing. The funnel cake. The silence. This is the passage a reader will remember. It must be written with zero sentimentality — just the facts of the body and the machine and the child's face.

3. **The "for your safety" eruption**: The Thompson-register detonation inside the amusement park section. This is where the rage lives. A full page of accelerating prose that catalogs the "safety" language used to exclude bodies from American spaces. The comedy here is vicious and structural — not the narrator laughing at herself but the narrator laughing at a country that put a man on the moon but cannot build an airplane seat.

4. **The desire path on the hospital lawn**: A quiet moment of recognition. The narrator sees her own navigation strategy made visible in the worn grass. Fifteen passages to make a trail. How many invisible trails has her body worn? This is the essay's most meditative passage.

5. **The turnstile paragraph**: A single sustained paragraph, no breaks, Thompson-pure. The turnstile as medieval body-sorting device, cattle chute, architecture of distrust. The prose moves as the body moves — forward, because there is no other direction.

6. **Joe and Josephine**: Not a scene but a recurring presence. First introduced at the airport, referenced again at the hospital, and returning in the final diner section. The ghosts who fit everywhere. The fictional average that determined the real world.

## 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Opening**: The reader feels the weight of constant calculation — the exhaustion of a body that has to do arithmetic before it can sit down. Claustrophobia that is not metaphorical.

**Amusement park**: The reader feels the specific vertigo of witnessed humiliation — the niece's face, the funnel cake, the silence. Then the "for your safety" passage hits like a fist, and the reader feels the rage that has been held under the precision, and the rage is funny and the funny is furious.

**Hospital**: A quieter devastation. The reader feels the accumulation of a lifetime of architectural exclusion and recognizes it as systemic, designed, intentional. Not carelessness. Architecture.

**Subway**: The reader cannot breathe. The paragraph does not let them. They are in the turnstile with the narrator and the metal is pressing and the prose is pressing and there is no paragraph break to hide in.

**Diner**: The reader is sitting in the booth. The booth is too small. The coffee is hot. The narrator is still here. The reader feels the particular weight of a body that knows it belongs to a country that has never agreed. Not sadness. Not anger. Something heavier than both, and more ordinary, and ongoing.

## 6. Formula Integration

**Roxane Gay (Author A)**: Most visible in Section 1 (Airport) and Section 2 (Amusement Park — the test seat passage). Gay's influence controls the essay's baseline register: the declarative sentences, the factual precision, the "I" that begins sentence after sentence, the refusal to metaphorize pain. The amusement park passage — harness, niece, funnel cake, silence — is pure Gay: devastation delivered without devastation's performance.

**Hunter S. Thompson (Author B)**: Most visible in Section 4 (Subway) — the single-paragraph turnstile passage that accelerates past grammar — and in the "for your safety" eruption in Section 2. Thompson's influence provides the essay's second register: the velocity, the catalogs, the sentences that overshoot their landing. Also in the diner section, where the narrator's cheeseburger echoes Thompson's appetite and the American landscape is rendered as a hallucination of comfort that comforts nobody.

***Hunger* (Work X)**: Controls the essay's architecture. The five-section structure that refuses arc. The accumulation of spaces instead of plot. The vignette quality — each section self-contained but gaining weight through sequence. The "or I do" motif: the narrator knowing and not-knowing simultaneously. The memoir as inventory of how the world measures her body.

***Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas* (Work Y)**: Provides the essay's thematic engine: America as a hallucination projected onto space, the savage journey to the heart of the American Dream rewritten as the savage journey to the heart of the American Booth. The country's promises visible in its architecture. The observer consumed by the spectacle — the narrator cannot describe the spaces without being inside them, cannot document the exclusion without being excluded. The forward motion (airport to amusement park to hospital to subway to diner) that arrives not at the American Dream but at a too-small booth in a diner with fluorescent light, which is the American Dream, or what's left of it.

## 7. Title

**"Armrest Width, American Standard"**

Why: It captures the essay's fusion — the Gay-precise measurement ("armrest width") fused with the Thompson-scaled American indictment ("American Standard"). It sounds like a specification, a plumbing code, a blueprint — which is exactly what the essay argues these spaces are. It does not start with "The." It is not sentimental. It is not clever. It is a fact that is also a judgment, which is the essay's mode.

## Additional Notes for the Writer

- **Tense**: Present tense throughout. The essay is happening now. The body is always in the present.
- **Paragraph length**: Varies radically. Some sections have one-sentence paragraphs (Gay). The subway section is a single paragraph that runs for a full page (Thompson). The contrast IS the form.
- **Humor**: Present but controlled. The "for your safety" passage should be viciously funny. The narrator's observation of Henry Dreyfuss's Joe should have the dry comedy of someone who has learned to find institutional absurdity hilarious because the alternative is screaming. But the humor is NEVER self-deprecating. The body is not the joke. The country is the joke.
- **The niece**: She appears only in the amusement park section. She is not named. She is eleven. She says almost nothing. She is there so the reader can see the narrator through another person's eyes — and what the reader sees is not pity but the beginning of the niece's own education in what spaces cost.
- **Banned**: No "Marcus," no "Chen," no redemption arc, no "and then I learned to accept myself," no tidy epiphany, no symmetrical bookend, no announced theme. The essay ends in the booth. The booth is still too small.
- **Word count management**: Airport ~1,000 words. Amusement park ~1,500 words (the longest section, containing both the test seat and the "for your safety" eruption). Hospital ~1,000 words. Subway ~700 words (short but dense — one paragraph). Diner ~800 words. Opening hook integrated into the airport section. Total should hit 5,000-5,200.
