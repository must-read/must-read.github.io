## Writing Samples

### Source 1: Charles Portis (Author A) — True Grit

**Origin**: *True Grit* (1968), opening paragraph
**Why representative**: This passage establishes Portis's signature: a first-person narrator with absolute tonal control, colloquial precision that reads as effortless, and humor that never winks. Mattie Ross tells you exactly what happened with zero self-pity and the cadence of someone settling a ledger.

> "People do not give it credence that a fourteen-year-old girl could leave home and go off in the wintertime to avenge her father's blood but it did not seem so strange then, although I will say it did not happen every day. I was just fourteen years of age when a coward going by the name of Tom Chaney shot my father down in Fort Smith, Arkansas, and robbed him of his life and his horse and $150 in cash money plus two California gold pieces that he carried in his trouser band."

**Origin**: *True Grit*, serialized version (Saturday Evening Post)
**Why representative**: Shows the variant opening — tighter, more procedural, and even more Portis-dry in its accounting of practical next-steps after a murder.

> "My name is Mattie Ross. When I was fourteen years of age a coward going by the name of Tom Chaney shot my father dead in Fort Smith, Arkansas. After I had arranged for Papa's burial, I sought out the sheriff in Fort Smith and inquired what measures had been taken to catch the murderer. None, it seemed. Chaney had fled to Indian Territory, into a jurisdiction in which only U.S. marshals could pursue him; he was believed to have tied up with a band of robbers led by Lucky Ned Pepper. I asked the sheriff who was the best marshal, and he said the meanest was a one-eyed ruffian named Rooster Cogburn."

**Origin**: *Norwood* (1966), opening lines
**Why representative**: Portis's deadpan voice in a non-Western context — the same flat declarative sentences, the specificity of objects (Ithaca Featherweight, two towels), the humor embedded in precision rather than punchline.

> "Sometime during the night the Remleys decamped, taking with them a television set and a 16-gauge Ithaca Featherweight and two towels. No one could say how they got out of town with all that gear, least of all the night marshal. The day marshal came by and looked at the place where the television set had been. He made notes."

**Origin**: *The Dog of the South* (1979), opening
**Why representative**: The Ray Midge voice — a man whose narration is funny precisely because he reports disaster with clerical calm.

> "My wife Norma had run off with Guy Dupree and I was waiting around for the credit card billings to come in so I could see where they had gone."

### Source 2: Louis L'Amour (Author B) — Hondo, The Daybreakers

**Origin**: *Hondo* (1953), opening description
**Why representative**: L'Amour's frontier-immersive prose — the physical world rendered in clean, efficient strokes, the body and landscape fused, no ornament.

> He was a big man, wide-shouldered, with the lean, hard-boned face of the desert rider. There was no softness in him. His toughness was ingrained and deep, without cruelty, yet quick, hard, and dangerous. Whatever wells of gentleness might lie within him were guarded and deep.

Setting: "a far, lost land, a land of beige-gray silences and distance where the eye reached out farther and farther to lose itself finally against the sky, and where the only movement was the lazy swing of a remote buzzard."

**Origin**: *The Daybreakers* (1960)
**Why representative**: Shows L'Amour's ability to write wonder — the frontier as revelation, the world opening up.

> "Suddenly, something had happened to me, and it happened to Orrin, too. The world had burst wide open, and where our narrow valleys had been, our hog-backed ridges, our huddled towns and villages, there was now a world without end or limit..."

**Origin**: L'Amour on gunfights
**Why representative**: The anti-romantic, experience-authenticated voice.

> "Nothing exciting or thrilling about a gunfight. She's a mighty cold proposition for both parties."

### Source 3: Lonesome Dove (Work X) — Larry McMurtry

**Origin**: *Lonesome Dove* (1985), Chapter 1 description
**Why representative**: McMurtry's gift for making the mundane frontier hypnotic — the physical world as texture, humor threaded through heat and discomfort.

Opening atmosphere: "Evening took a long time getting to Lonesome Dove, but when it came it was a comfort. For most of the hours of the day — and most of the months of the year — the sun had the town trapped deep in dust, far out in the chaparral flats, a heaven for snakes and horned toads, roadrunners and stinging lizards, but a hell for pigs and Tennesseans."

**Origin**: *Lonesome Dove*, Clara's speech to Call (late in the novel)
**Why representative**: The emotional core of Lonesome Dove — the accusation that Call's code of action is actually a code of abandonment. This is what the book builds toward: the indictment of the silent man by the woman who loved his partner.

> "And I'll tell you another thing, I'm sorry you and Gus McCrae ever met. All you two done was ruin one another, not to mention those close to you... You men and your promises: they're just excuses to do what you plan to do anyway, which is leave. You think you've always done right — that's your ugly pride, Mr. Call. But you never did right and it would be a sad woman that needed anything from you. You're a vain coward, for all your fighting. I despised you then, for what you were, and I despise you now, for what you're doing."

**Origin**: *Lonesome Dove*, on frontier women and reading
**Why representative**: McMurtry's understanding of the women left behind by the Western myth.

> "The ladies' magazines had stories and parts of novels in them, in many of which were ladies who led lives so different from hers that she felt she might as well be on another planet."

### Source 4: The Virginian (Work Y) — Owen Wister

**Origin**: *The Virginian* (1902), Chapter I — "Enter the Man"
**Why representative**: The tenderfoot's first encounter with the Virginian at Medicine Bow — the template for every Western entrance. Wister's prose is more ornate than L'Amour's but shares the fascination with male competence observed from outside. The tenderfoot/narrator is the mirror; the Virginian is the substance.

> Some notable sight was drawing the passengers, both men and women, to the window; and therefore I rose and crossed the car to see what it was. I saw near the track an enclosure, and round it some laughing men, and inside it some whirling dust, and amid the dust some horses, plunging, huddling, and dodging. They were cow ponies in a corral, and one of them would not be caught, no matter who threw the rope.

> Then for the first time I noticed a man who sat on the high gate of the corral, looking on. For he now climbed down with the undulations of a tiger, smooth and easy, as if his muscles flowed beneath his skin... He appeared to hold the rope down low, by his leg. But like a sudden snake I saw the noose go out its length and fall true; and the thing was done. As the captured pony walked in with a sweet, church-door expression, our train moved slowly on to the station, and a passenger remarked, "That man knows his business."

> Lounging there at ease against the wall was a slim young giant, more beautiful than pictures. His broad, soft hat was pushed back; a loose-knotted, dull-scarlet handkerchief sagged from his throat; and one casual thumb was hooked in the cartridge-belt that slanted across his hips.

**Origin**: *The Virginian*, Chapter I — the Virginian's voice
**Why representative**: The gentle, drawling humor of the Virginian — teasing Uncle Hughey about his marriages. This is the comedic Western voice that predates Portis.

> "Off to get married AGAIN? Oh, don't!"

> "Why, your Sunday clothes told me, Uncle Hughey. They are speakin' mighty loud o' nuptials."

> "Ain't them gloves the same yu' wore to your last weddin'?"

### 2 Selected Concepts

**1. Clinton (automobile)** — A Canadian car company that made no more than 8 automobiles before disappearing. Descended from a thresher company whose factory burned down. Integration: The buyer at the end of the cattle drive is a man who has invested in a mechanical threshing company and is converting his ranch from cattle to wheat — he represents the future that is erasing the cowboys' profession. He owns one of these short-lived early automobiles (relocated to a fictional American context), which sits broken in his yard. The narrator will describe this machine with the same deadpan precision Portis uses for any absurd object — something that is supposed to replace the horse and can't even get itself up a hill. The automobile is the future that doesn't work yet but will.

**2. Social osmosis** — Knowing the details of something you've never directly experienced; acquiring knowledge indirectly. Integration: This is the narrator's condition regarding his own emotions. He knows everything about his partnership with the silent man — thirty years of proximity — but he has learned it all through social osmosis rather than direct acknowledgment. He can describe what his partner feels in any given moment, but the knowledge was never exchanged through conversation. It was absorbed through a thousand small observations: how the man holds a rope, how he sleeps lighter on nights before trouble, how his jaw sets when a calf is suffering. The narrator knows his friend is dying (or leaving, or both) not because anyone told him, but because he has been osmotically reading the man for three decades. This concept also maps to the tenderfoot element from The Virginian — the narrator absorbed the West the way one absorbs a TV show: by proximity, not by being native to it. He came from somewhere else and learned this life indirectly, which is why he can describe it with the clarity of someone who had to teach himself a foreign language.

## 1. Premise

Two aging cowboys — Everett Teague, who talks, and Boyd Sill, who doesn't — drive eighty-seven head of cattle across the Texas Panhandle to a buyer in Hardesty, Oklahoma, in the autumn of 1893. It should be three days' work. It takes six. The buyer, when they arrive, has sold his cattle pens to a thresher company and isn't sure he wants beef anymore. The question the story asks: What does loyalty look like when it has no useful object? These two men have spent thirty years being good at something together, and the something is ending, and neither one can say what the other means to him, and the drive is the last excuse they have for not saying it.

---

## 2. Protagonist

**Everett Teague**, 56, narrator. A Pennsylvanian by birth who came west at nineteen to work cattle and never went back. He has been riding with Boyd Sill for thirty-one years. He is the talker — the one who fills silence the way other men fill canteens, as a provision against emergency. He has a vocabulary larger than the situation requires, and he knows this, and the knowledge makes him both proud and ashamed.

- **What he WANTS**: To complete the drive, deliver the cattle, get paid, and keep things as they have always been.
- **What he NEEDS**: To acknowledge that Boyd is leaving — not dying, but retiring, moving to his sister's place in Wichita to sit on a porch — and that this drive is the last one, and that without Boyd his life will make no sense.
- **His flaw/blind spot**: He believes that naming a feeling destroys it. He thinks his jokes and observations preserve the friendship by keeping it unexamined. In fact, his refusal to speak directly is a form of cowardice that has cost him every close relationship except this one, and it is about to cost him this one too — not because Boyd will leave (Boyd is leaving regardless) but because Boyd will leave without ever hearing Everett say the thing, and Everett will spend the rest of his life knowing he had the chance and told a joke instead.
- **His specific wound**: At twenty-three, his first year on a real drive, he watched a man named Petrie drown crossing the Canadian River. Everett was forty feet upstream and could have thrown a rope. He didn't, not because he was afraid but because he was calculating — judging the current, the angle, the odds — and by the time he finished calculating, Petrie was gone. He has been compensating ever since: talking instead of acting, narrating instead of doing. The talking is how he makes sure he never again sits in the silence of having failed to move.

## 3. Structure

### Opening Image/Scene

Everett is trying to explain to a cow why it needs to cross a dry creek bed. The cow is not persuaded. Boyd is fifty yards back, doing nothing about it, because Boyd knows that a cow will cross when it decides to cross and that talking to it is as productive as talking to weather. Everett talks to the cow anyway. This is the dynamic in miniature: one man who narrates the world, one who acts in it, and an animal that doesn't care about either approach.

The opening establishes voice immediately — Portis-dry, with the colloquial precision and deadpan authority of a man recounting events he has already decided are both ridiculous and important. Everett addresses the reader with the calm confidence of someone filing a report that happens to be funny.

### The Inciting Disruption

On the second morning of what should be a three-day drive, Boyd tells Everett he has accepted his sister's invitation to come to Wichita. He says it the way he says everything: briefly, without emphasis, while doing something else (cinching a saddle). He says it as though it is a fact about the weather rather than the end of a thirty-one-year partnership. Everett asks when. Boyd says after this drive. Everett makes a joke about Wichita — something about the quality of the beef there, or the width of the streets — and the joke sits between them like a fence post that has been driven into the wrong place.

### The Escalating Middle

The drive encounters a series of small, specific problems that require the two men to work together one last time:

1. **The fence** — A barbed-wire fence crosses what used to be open range. It wasn't there five years ago. They have to find the property owner and negotiate passage, or cut the fence and risk consequences. The property owner is a wheat farmer who doesn't understand cattle and is afraid of them. The negotiation — Everett talking, Boyd standing behind him like a silent argument — is comic and also the first encounter with the new world that is replacing theirs.

2. **The storm** — A November squall drives the cattle into a wash. Boyd and Everett spend a night in the rain keeping the herd from scattering. This is pure L'Amour territory: physical, tactile, the body working against weather, two men doing the competent thing without discussion. But during the storm, Everett sees Boyd wince reaching for a rope, and the wince is not from cold — it's from something in the shoulder, something that has been there awhile and that Boyd has not mentioned, because Boyd does not mention things about his body any more than he mentions things about his feelings.

3. **The third rider** — They pick up a young man named Lyle Dunaway, nineteen, heading to Hardesty for work. He is, unmistakably, a tenderfoot: Eastern clothes, new boots, a hat that has never seen weather. He knows about cattle the way a man knows about a country he has read about in magazines. Everett recognizes in Lyle what Everett himself was at nineteen — someone absorbing the frontier through proximity rather than birthright. But Lyle will never get to learn what Everett learned, because the thing Everett learned is ending. Lyle is a tenderfoot arriving at a frontier that is already closed. This is the Virginian/tenderfoot dynamic inverted: instead of the young man being transformed, the young man is simply too late.

4. **The steer** — One steer, a brindled longhorn with a broken horn, refuses every direction. It goes left when the herd goes right. It stands when the herd moves. Boyd, without being asked, takes personal charge of this animal, and for two days engages in a private contest of wills with it. Everett narrates this contest with increasing admiration and decreasing comedy, because what he is watching is Boyd being Boyd — stubborn, patient, competent, relentless — and what he is also watching is the last time he will ever see it.

### The Crisis Point

They arrive in Hardesty. The buyer, a man named Fossett, has torn down his cattle pens. In his yard sits a broken-down mechanical automobile — purchased from a company that went bankrupt after building fewer than a dozen — which he is convinced represents the future of transportation. He has invested in a threshing operation and is converting his ranch to wheat. He'll take the cattle, maybe, but at a price so low it barely covers the cost of the drive.

Everett, for the first time in the story, stops talking. He stands in Fossett's yard, looking at the automobile that can't drive and the wheat field that used to be pasture, and he has nothing to say. Every joke he has ever told was a way of managing reality by describing it. This reality resists description. He is standing in the future and the future does not need him.

Boyd handles the negotiation. Boyd — the silent one, the one who speaks through action — makes the deal. He doesn't argue about the price. He doesn't try to explain what the cattle are worth. He simply says what he will accept and waits, and Fossett meets him halfway, and it is done. And Everett watches this and understands something he has known through social osmosis for thirty-one years but has never articulated: Boyd's silence is not a limitation. It is a discipline. Boyd does not waste words because Boyd understands that words, like rope, should only be thrown when they can reach something.

### The Ending

The cattle are delivered. The money is split. Lyle Dunaway shakes their hands and walks toward Hardesty to find work that may or may not exist. Boyd and Everett make camp one last time, a mile outside town, because neither of them says so but neither of them is ready to stop yet.

The brindled steer, which was sold with the rest, has broken out of Fossett's inadequate temporary pen and found its way back to Boyd. It stands at the edge of the firelight, looking at him. Boyd gets up, walks to the steer, and puts his hand on its broken horn. He stands there for a while. He doesn't say anything. Everett doesn't say anything. The narrator shuts up — this is the half-page of silence that Portis described in the meeting, the moment where if the jokes have done their work, the silence is louder than anything.

Then morning. Boyd packs his things. Everett makes coffee. Boyd says he'll catch the train in Hardesty. Everett says something about eggs — asks whether Boyd thinks Fossett's hens lay any better than his cattle buy, something dry and specific and completely beside the point. Boyd almost smiles. He rides toward town. Everett watches him go.

The final paragraph: Everett addresses the reader directly, with Mattie Ross clarity, and says something about the morning — how it looked, what the light did, what the coffee tasted like — and then says he stayed at that camp for two more days before he rode anywhere, and that he told himself it was because the horse needed rest, and that the horse did not need rest, and that he knew it, and that he stayed anyway.

**Open thread**: Lyle Dunaway. The boy walked into Hardesty looking for frontier work in a town that is becoming a wheat depot. We don't learn what happens to him. He's the future, or he's nothing, and the story doesn't tell you which.

## 4. Key Scenes (7 scenes for a 7,500-word piece)

1. **The cow in the creek bed** (opening, ~600 words): Establishes voice, dynamic, and the narrator's relationship to language. Comic surface, elegiac undertone. Everett talks to a cow; Boyd waits; the cow decides for itself. The reader learns everything about these two men from how they handle an animal that doesn't want to cooperate.

2. **Boyd's announcement** (~800 words): The Wichita conversation. Boyd says he's leaving while cinching a saddle. Everett makes a joke. The joke fails — not because it isn't funny, but because the situation refuses to be managed by humor. First crack in Everett's verbal armor.

3. **The fence negotiation** (~1,000 words): Everett and the wheat farmer. Comic set-piece that is also a confrontation with modernity. The farmer is afraid of cows. Everett explains cows with excessive patience. Boyd stands behind him and the farmer negotiates faster because Boyd's silence is more persuasive than Everett's eloquence. Lyle Dunaway joins them here — he was walking on the road and the cattle caught up with him.

4. **The storm in the wash** (~1,200 words): Pure physical Western. L'Amour territory — rain, mud, cattle, two men working by instinct and training. No humor. Everett's narration strips down to essentials because the situation demands it. Boyd's shoulder wince. Lyle Dunaway proves useless but brave — he tries, he just doesn't know what to try. At the end of the storm, Everett wraps Lyle in a blanket and tells him he did all right, and the kindness in it is a surprise to everyone including Everett.

5. **Boyd and the brindled steer** (~1,000 words): A two-day running battle of wills between a silent man and a stubborn animal. Everett narrates with increasing respect and decreasing irony. This is the scene where the narrator's comedy gives way — not to sentimentality, but to something closer to testimony. He is watching mastery and he knows it is the last time.

6. **Fossett's yard** (~1,200 words): The arrival. The broken automobile. The wheat. The negotiation where Boyd talks and Everett is silent. The inversion of their dynamic — the talking man struck dumb by the future, the silent man suddenly the one who handles language. Fossett's automobile is described in Portis-precise detail: make, condition, the specific sound it makes when Fossett tries to start it, the specific way it sits in the yard like a horse that has died standing up.

7. **The last camp** (~1,000 words): The steer comes back. Boyd touches its horn. The silence. Morning. Eggs. Boyd rides away. Everett stays. The final paragraph, addressed to the reader, precise and quiet and devastatingly plain.

## 5. The Emotional Trajectory

**Beginning**: Amusement. The reader is entertained by Everett's voice — the Portis deadpan, the observations, the way he talks to animals and about people with the same dry precision. The partnership seems comfortable, permanent, comic.

**Middle**: Unease. The reader starts to feel what Everett won't say. Every joke starts to sound like a man patching a wall. The storm scene strips away the comedy and reveals the physical reality underneath — these are old men doing hard work, and one of them is hurt, and neither will admit it. The tenderfoot Lyle makes the reader conscious of time — this boy is the future, and the future doesn't know anything.

**End**: The reader feels the weight of a silence they've been taught to hear. When Everett stops talking — when the narrator who has been filling every page with language simply stops — the absence is physical. Not grief exactly. More like the moment after a long piece of music ends and you sit in the quiet and realize the music was holding something up, and now it's not, and you can feel the gravity. The reader should put this down and sit for a moment and think about the person they never told the important thing to, and whether the joke they told instead was enough.

## 6. Formula Integration

### Author A — Charles Portis (deadpan comic voice, colloquial precision, narrators smarter than they let on)
**Concentrated in**: Everett's entire narration, but most visible in the **fence negotiation scene** and the **opening cow scene**. Everett describes the wheat farmer's fear of cattle with the same flat, precise, relentless clarity that Mattie Ross uses to describe the legal inadequacies of Fort Smith. He catalogs the farmer's specific anxieties — the width of the horns, the sound of the hooves, the farmer's conviction that cattle carry disease — with a patience that is itself the joke. Portis's narrators are always smarter than the situation requires; Everett is a man with a Pennsylvania education and a thirty-year cowboy career, and the gap between his vocabulary and his profession is the comedy.

### Author B — Louis L'Amour (authentic frontier prose, physical textures, clean action, the code of the West)
**Concentrated in**: The **storm scene**. When the squall hits and the cattle bolt, the narration shifts register. The sentences shorten. The landscape becomes tactile — mud, rain, the smell of wet hide, the sound a cow makes when it's afraid. Boyd moves through this scene the way L'Amour's protagonists move: efficiently, without wasted motion, the body doing what the body has trained to do. This is also where Boyd's physical presence is most fully rendered — the wide shoulders, the way he sits a horse in weather, the ingrained toughness that L'Amour describes as "without cruelty, yet quick, hard, and dangerous."

### Work X — Lonesome Dove (the great cattle drive as last hurrah, friendship between aging cowboys who can't say what they mean, the West as elegy)
**Concentrated in**: The **last camp scene** and the **overall structure**. The entire story is a Lonesome Dove in miniature — not a thousand-head drive across three states but eighty-seven head across three days, and the smallness is the point. Gus and Call's dynamic maps directly: Everett is Gus (the talker, the one who hides feeling in language), Boyd is Call (the silent worker, the one who expresses love through endurance). The steer that comes back to Boyd echoes the way Call carries Gus's body back to Texas — an act that is either love or stubbornness or both, and the story won't tell you which.

### Work Y — The Virginian (tenderfoot transformed by frontier, the hanging as moral test, courtship across civilized-wild divide)
**Concentrated in**: **Lyle Dunaway** and the **Petrie memory**. Lyle is the Virginian's tenderfoot — the Easterner who arrives in the West and is supposed to be changed by it. But Lyle arrives too late. The frontier is closing. There is nothing left to transform him. He is the tenderfoot without a frontier, and his presence makes Everett and Boyd's obsolescence visible in a way that the cattle and the weather do not. The Petrie drowning functions as the story's version of the Virginian's hanging — the moral test that defines a man's relationship to action. Everett failed that test at twenty-three, and his failure explains everything about him: the talking, the jokes, the refusal to act directly. The Virginian hangs his friend and becomes harder; Everett watches a man drown and becomes a narrator.

## 7. Title

**Eggs at Hardesty**

Not starting with "The." Concrete and specific — names a place and an object. Carries no apparent weight until you've read the story, at which point it becomes the most loaded thing in it: the last joke, the last breakfast, the last deflection. Echoes Portis's insistence in the author meeting: "Something about eggs." The title works the way Portis's humor works — plain on the surface, doing something else underneath.
