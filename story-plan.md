## Writing Samples & Research Notes

### Louis L'Amour (Author A)
Opening of *Hondo*: "HE ROLLED the cigarette in his lips, liking the taste of the tobacco, squinting his eyes against the sun glare. It was a far, lost land, a land of beige-gray silences and distance where the eye reached out farther and farther to lose itself finally against the sky, and where the only movement was the lazy swing of a remote buzzard." L'Amour's prose is "lean, but vivid" — spare, no padding, real sense of the harsh landscape. Characters rendered through action and physical detail, not interior monologue. Dialogue is economical, frontier-colloquial. The protagonist thinks none of this — "Rather it was something he knew, something born of years in wild country."

### Dorothy M. Johnson (Author B)
From "The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance": "The Senator almost told the truth, but he caught himself in time. He said, 'Bert Barricune was my friend for more than thirty years.' He could not give the true answer: He was my enemy; he was my conscience; he made me whatever I am." Johnson's prose is graceful, sparse, and always transfixing with subtle irony. She skips over months or years in a few sentences. Her characters are simple but they live and their acts and feelings are believable. Four of the top five "best Western short stories of the 20th century" were written by Johnson.

### Shane — Jack Schaefer (Work X)
Opening: "HE RODE INTO OUR VALLEY in the summer of '89..." Narrated by Bob Starrett, a boy watching the adult world. Shane is the mysterious stranger — slim, dressed in black, deadly competence hidden beneath quiet manners. The gunfight with Wilson is the climax of communal anticipation. Shane kills Wilson and Fletcher, is wounded, and rides away. "He tells Bob that once you have killed a man, you are marked forever." The boy watches him go, weeping. The story ends at the departure — Schaefer knew that if Shane stayed, the story would become something less romantic and more true.

### True Grit — Charles Portis (Work Y)
Opening: "People do not give it credence that a fourteen-year-old girl could leave home and go off in the wintertime to avenge her father's blood but it did not seem so strange then, although I will say it did not happen every day." Mattie Ross's voice: formal, deadpan, absolutely certain. Key quotes: "You must pay for everything in this world one way and another. There is nothing free except the Grace of God." / "There is no knowing what is in a man's heart." / "The meanest one is Rooster Cogburn. He is a pitiless man, double-tough, and fear don't enter into his thinking." Mattie narrates fifty years later — the not-breaking is the wound.

### 2 Selected Concepts

**Selection 1: Tintype photography**
*Integration*: An itinerant tintype photographer (Mr. Yewell) passes through town during the three-day waiting period. He photographs Cass on the boarding house porch — the stranger sitting in a chair, hat on knee, left hand pressed flat against the arm to hide its tremor. This tintype survives forty years. Eli still has it. It becomes the story's central object: the image that froze one moment and erased everything the camera could not capture — the killing, the aftermath, the blood under the dirt, Ruth at the kitchen table. The title refers to this image. A tintype is the myth; memory is the reality. Both are incomplete.

**Selection 2: Phantom limb (metaphorical)**
*Integration*: Operates as subtext, never named. After the stranger leaves, the town feels his absence as a presence — the empty chair, the unclaimed plate. After Eli's father was killed, the family felt his absence the same way. The story is about phantom presences: the dead father who is still dead, the stranger who is gone, the blood under fresh dirt. Ruth's line — "Your father is still dead" — is the phantom limb statement: the killing did not restore what was lost, only added another absence. Eli's forty years of staring at the tintype is his phantom reach toward understanding that won't solidify.

## Story Design

### 1. Premise

A boy of eleven watches a stranger ride into his mother's boarding house in a small Wyoming cattle town in 1882. His father was killed six months earlier by a cattle baron's enforcer over a water dispute, and his mother has been surviving by feeding the men who took her husband. The stranger, who gives only the name Cass, kills the enforcer in a four-second gunfight, then rides out at dawn. The boy narrates forty years later, and the story's true subject is not the killing but the morning after — the town that had to go on being a town, and the mother who had to decide whether what happened was justice or just more killing.

### 2. Protagonist

**Eli Pardoe**, age 11 at the time of the story, narrating at approximately 50-55.

- **WANT**: He wants Cass to be Shane — the pure hero who solves everything. He wants a clean story he can tell himself about his father's death being avenged.
- **NEED**: He needs to accept that violence is not justice and justice is not resolution; that his mother's quiet endurance was the real courage, not the stranger's fast hands.
- **FLAW**: He romanticizes violence. Even at fifty, he still half-believes the stranger was a hero, and the narration reveals this without the narrator fully realizing it. He is Mattie Ross without Mattie's certainty — he has spent forty years trying to build certainty and hasn't managed it.

**Other key characters**:

- **Ruth Pardoe** (Eli's mother): Runs the boarding house. Has been feeding Frawley's men for six months because refusing them would mean losing the business. She is not passive — she is calculating. She saw what Cass was the moment he rode in. She could have turned him away. She didn't. She set a place for him at her table and she knew what she was setting in motion. Johnson's camera-subject: the woman who has to live in the town after. She never breaks. The not-breaking is the wound.

- **Cass** (the stranger): We never learn his full name. L'Amour's archetypal loner — lean, weathered, competent, taciturn. He has a tremor in his left hand that he controls by keeping it in his pocket or pressing it flat against surfaces. He is good at violence the way other men are good at carpentry. Not a villain, not a hero. A tool the frontier made and the frontier uses.

- **Lute Frawley**: The cattle baron. Not a monster — a businessman who has made calculations about water and land. Never seen directly, only referenced. His power felt through his hired gun and through the fact that half the town depends on his payroll.

- **Dodd Sweeney**: Frawley's enforcer. A man who has killed before and does not appear to mind it. He eats at Ruth's boarding house. He is polite. He tips his hat. He has a hole in the sole of one boot that Eli will not know about until the man is dead.

- **Mr. Yewell**: An itinerant tintype photographer passing through town. Takes a photograph of Cass the day before the gunfight. This tintype becomes the story's central image — the "daguerreotype of a good man" of the title.

### 3. Structure (5 Beats)

**Beat 1 — ARRIVAL (~900 words)**

The adult Eli's voice establishes time and place — Wyoming, 1882, the six months since his father's death. Ruth's boarding house. The quality of September light. L'Amour's tactile world: sagebrush smell after rain, the creak of the boarding house porch, the taste of well water with iron in it.

Cass arrives on a horse ridden hard. Described through physical detail only — the way he carries his saddle, the way he scans a room before entering. He pays Ruth for a week, eats supper without speaking except to compliment the biscuits, and goes upstairs.

Eli watches from the kitchen doorway. The adult narrator: "I thought he was the answer to something. I was eleven and I still believed things had answers."

**Beat 2 — THREE DAYS OF WAITING (~1,200 words)**

Town tension rendered through daily life. Cass eats breakfast and supper at the boarding house. He walks the single street. He sits on the porch. He speaks to no one except Ruth, and those conversations are brief and practical — weather, road conditions, the price of beef. But the town knows why he's there, and the town waits.

Key scene: Dodd Sweeney comes to the boarding house for supper. He sits at the same table as Cass. They eat in silence. Ruth serves them both. Sweeney is polite — "thank you, ma'am" and "fine meal, ma'am." Cass says nothing. Eli sees his mother's hands: she grips the serving ladle too hard and the knuckles go white. Nobody mentions Eli's father. Nobody mentions water rights. The conversation is about nothing and is about everything.

Mr. Yewell arrives with his wagon — an itinerant tintype photographer working the small towns between Cheyenne and the Bighorns. He photographs three families and one cowhand wanting to send his portrait back to Missouri. He photographs Cass on the porch. Cass allows it without explanation. The camera sees a lean man with tired eyes and a hand pressed flat against the chair arm.

Eli's narration reveals Ruth's calculations: she told him years later that she saw what Cass was the moment he rode in. She could have turned him away. She set a place for him and knew what she was setting in motion. "She never said she asked for it. She said she allowed it. In the West, that's the same thing."

**Beat 3 — THE GUNFIGHT (~700 words)**

Short. Johnson's economy — not a wasted word.

Late afternoon. Cass walks to the saloon. The whole town knows. People on the boardwalks, watching. Eli follows at a distance. Ruth does not follow — she stays at the boarding house, washing dishes. She does not look out the window.

The gunfight itself is four sentences. Cass enters the saloon. Sweeney is at the bar. Words are exchanged — Eli cannot hear through the walls. Two shots. Cass comes out. His left hand is bleeding — not from a bullet but from the recoil; his tremor made his grip wrong and the hammer bit his palm. Sweeney does not come out.

Eli runs to the saloon door and sees Sweeney on the floor. The blood. The boy's noticing is detailed, specific — the pattern the blood makes on the plank floor, the position of Sweeney's hat three feet from his head, the hole in the sole of one of Sweeney's boots. Eli did not know that. He did not know that the man who killed his father had a hole in his boot.

**Beat 4 — THE EVENING AFTER (~900 words)**

Cass returns to the boarding house. His hand wrapped in a bandanna. He goes upstairs. He does not eat supper. Ruth sets his place anyway, then clears it after an hour.

That night, Eli cannot sleep. He goes downstairs and finds his mother at the kitchen table, not sleeping either. She is not crying. She is staring at the table surface, scarred from years of knives and forks and hot pans. They sit together without speaking. The silence is not peaceful, not tense — the silence that happens when something large has passed through and left the air different.

Ruth says one thing: "Your father is still dead."

It is the only time she references the killing as connected to their loss. What she means: this didn't fix it. Nothing was repaired. A man is dead and another man is dead and the water still flows the way Frawley wants it to flow and nothing has changed except the number of dead men went up by one.

Eli asks: "Was he a good man?" He means Cass, but the question applies to everyone.

Ruth says: "He was a man who did a thing." She does not answer the question. She will never answer the question.

**Beat 5 — THE MORNING AFTER (~900 words)**

Cass rides out at first light. Eli watches from the upstairs window. L'Amour's physical language — the way a man cinches a saddle, the way a horse turns into the sun. No words. No farewell.

The rest of the morning: Ruth opens for business. She makes coffee. She sets out plates. A freighter comes through — two men hauling goods to the railroad camp — and she feeds them eggs and bacon and charges the full rate. Life resumes.

But: there's blood on the street. Not literal — someone from the saloon shoveled dirt over it at dawn. The absence of the blood. The dirt that covers it. Children walking past on their way to the schoolhouse, not knowing what's underneath. Eli walks past and does know.

The adult narrator closes with the tintype of Cass. Eli still has it. He takes it out sometimes. The image shows a man sitting in a chair, hat on knee, one hand flat against the chair arm. You cannot see the tremor. You cannot see what happened the next day. You cannot see the hole in Sweeney's boot or the blood on the floor or Ruth staring at the table at midnight. You see a man in a chair, and that is all you see, and that is what a hero looks like when you flatten him into an image: a man in a chair who could be anyone.

Final line — open, unresolved: "I have looked at that picture a thousand times and I still do not know what I am looking at."

### 4. Key Scenes

1. **The shared supper**: Cass and Sweeney eating at the same table in Ruth's boarding house, served by the widow of the man Sweeney killed. Silence. Politeness. Ruth's white knuckles on the serving ladle. *(Johnson's compression; ordinary people caught in the machinery of violence)*

2. **The tintype session**: Mr. Yewell photographing Cass on the porch. The hidden tremor. The image that will outlast the man. *(Tintype concept fused naturally into the frontier setting; carries the phantom-limb theme — the image as phantom of the person)*

3. **The gunfight**: Four sentences. What Eli sees from outside. The boot with the hole in the sole. *(L'Amour's violence-as-skill combined with Johnson's refusal to let violence be clean)*

4. **Ruth at the kitchen table**: "Your father is still dead." The silence after. *(Johnson's aftermath; True Grit's unshakeable voice; the real subject of the story)*

5. **The morning after**: Dirt covering the blood. Children walking over it. Ruth making coffee. The town resuming. *(Shane's departure inverted — we stay with the town, not the rider)*

### 5. Emotional Trajectory

**Opening**: Nostalgia cut with grief. The adult narrator's voice is careful, measured — Mattie Ross's certainty replaced with Eli's uncertainty. He's telling this story because he still doesn't understand it.

**Rising**: Tension compressed into domestic detail. The boarding house as stage where violence is anticipated through politeness. Every meal is a countdown.

**Climax**: Not the gunfight. The gunfight is the shortest section. The climax is Ruth saying "Your father is still dead" — the moment the story declares its true subject.

**Falling**: The morning after. The banality of resumption. L'Amour's physical detail applied not to heroism but to the ordinary labor of continuing to live.

**Closing**: Unresolved. The narrator stares at a photograph and does not know what he sees. This is the open thread — forty years of looking and still not understanding whether what happened was justice or killing or both or neither.

### 6. Formula Integration

**From Louis L'Amour (Author A):**
*Identifiable passage/scene*: The entire physical rendering of Cass — his arrival, his body, his movements, his departure. L'Amour's sagebrush-and-leather tactile prose governs every description of the landscape, the weather, the horses, the leather, the iron-tasting well water. The action-driven gunfight sequence, though brief, has L'Amour's choreographic precision. Cass IS L'Amour's loner archetype: rides in, solves the problem, rides out. The physical texture of Beat 1 and Beat 5 — cinching saddles, rolling cigarettes, the smell of sage, frost on window glass — is pure L'Amour.

**From Dorothy M. Johnson (Author B):**
*Identifiable passage/scene*: The economy of the gunfight (four sentences). Ruth's character — the ordinary woman caught in the machinery of Western violence. The entire structural commitment to aftermath over action. Johnson's insight from "The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance" — that the person who shot Liberty Valance has to live in the town that knows — is the engine of Ruth's character. Not a wasted word. The story's leanness, its refusal to explain or resolve.

**From Shane (Work X):**
*Identifiable passage/scene*: The child narrator watching an adult world he cannot comprehend. The mysterious stranger arriving at a homestead with a problem. The gunfight as communal ritual — the whole town on the boardwalks, everyone knowing what's about to happen. The departure scene: the stranger riding away at first light while the boy watches from an upstairs window. The specific inversion: in Shane, we ride away with the stranger; here, we stay with the town.

**From True Grit (Work Y):**
*Identifiable passage/scene*: The retrospective narration — an adult voice looking back at childhood violence. Mattie Ross's unshakeable voice is recast as Eli's shakeable one: where Mattie built certainty to contain the killing she organized, Eli has built forty years of uncertainty. The quest for justice as personal covenant: Ruth's allowance of the stranger is her covenant, never spoken, never acknowledged, but governing everything. The unlikely partnership: Ruth and Cass never speak of their arrangement, but both understand it. "She allowed it. In the West, that's the same thing."

### 7. Title

**Daguerreotype of a Good Man**

- Does NOT start with "The" (compliant with <30% rule)
- Does not duplicate any existing western title
- Works on multiple levels: the literal tintype photograph of Cass; the way memory fixes a person in a single image; the question of whether the man in the image was "good" — which is the question the entire story refuses to answer
- Eli would call any photograph a "daguerreotype" because that's the word he heard once and kept — a child's misremembering that becomes the adult narrator's deliberate word choice
- The word "daguerreotype" is older, more formal, slightly wrong — mirroring how Eli's memory of the event is older, more formal, and slightly wrong
- Echoes True Grit's formal, slightly archaic diction without imitating it

## Synopsis (max 300 chars)

A boy watches a stranger kill his father's murderer in a four-second gunfight, then ride away at dawn. Forty years later, he narrates the aftermath — a mother's silence, a town's resumption, and a photograph he still cannot decipher.

## Additional Guidance for the Writer

### Prose Style
Two registers, layered. The physical world is L'Amour — tactile, specific, sensory. Sagebrush, leather, dust, iron-tasting water, the creak of a porch board, the way a man sits a horse. The emotional and moral world is Johnson — compressed, ironic, the myth and the reality existing in the same sentence. The narrative voice is Portis-inflected: retrospective, colloquial but formal, with dry observations that undercut without mocking. Eli's voice is not literary — he sounds like a man who has thought about this for decades without arriving at a conclusion. Short sentences at peak intensity. Longer sentences for memory and landscape.

### What to Avoid
- Do NOT resolve the question of whether Cass was a good man. The irresolution is the story.
- Do NOT give Cass a backstory or explain why he came. He rode in. That's all.
- Do NOT let the gunfight be the climax. It is the event; the story is what happens after.
- Do NOT use the names Marcus or Chen.
- Do NOT announce the theme. The theme is visible in the structure (aftermath > violence) and in Ruth's single line. Never stated.
- Do NOT give the ending symmetry with the opening. The story opens with an arrival and closes with a photograph — not a mirror, not a bookend, but a displacement.
- Do NOT let Ruth cry, break down, or have a moment of emotional release. Her not-breaking is the wound. She makes coffee. She sets out plates. She charges the full rate.
- Do NOT use: delve, tapestry, testament, ineffable, profound, myriad.
- Do NOT write a tidy epiphany. Eli does not "finally understand" anything. He stares at a picture and does not know what he's looking at.

### Open Thread
The final line refuses resolution. Eli has looked at the photograph a thousand times and still does not know what he is looking at. Frawley is never punished — his power continues. The water still flows the way Frawley wants. Ruth never speaks about what she allowed. The only thing that changed is the number of dead men. This is the thread left open: whether any of it meant anything, or whether it just happened.
